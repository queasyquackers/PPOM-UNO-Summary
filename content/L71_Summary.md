## Structural Overview and Clinical Approach (Slide 2-5)
The shoulder is a complex region formed by multiple articulations, including the sternoclavicular (SC), acromioclavicular (AC), and glenohumeral (GH) joints, as well as the scapulothoracic articulation. Evaluating shoulder pain requires carefully determining whether the symptoms truly stem from the shoulder joint itself or if they are referred from other regions such as the cervical spine, thoracic spine, or ribcage. A comprehensive clinical assessment—incorporating history taking, orthopedic examination, and neurological tests (reflexes, strength, dermatomal sensation)—must be combined with osteopathic evaluation to pinpoint the origin of somatic dysfunctions accurately.

*   **Clinical Considerations**:
    *   Pain referred from the neck, upper back, or ribcage can present as "shoulder pain."
    *   Assess for involvement of nerve structures, vasculature, and lymphatics traversing the shoulder.
    *   Previous injuries, surgeries, or immobility can alter normal motion, presenting as restriction or excessive mobility independent of pure somatic dysfunction.

## Sternoclavicular (SC) Joint Diagnosis (Slide 6-12)
Conceptually, treating the sternum and manubrium as the "torso" and the SC joint as the "shoulder joint" helps in understanding clavicular motion. During shoulder motion, the medial clavicle at the SC joint moves reciprocally to the distal clavicle. Testing is typically performed with the physician behind or in front of the patient, using palpation and observation to assess active or passive glides.

*   **SC Joint Adduction/Abduction dysfunctions**:
    *   **Abduction Dysfunction**: When shrugging the shoulders, the medial clavicle glides inferiorly. If it glides inferiorly well but fails to rise superiorly upon relaxation, it is an SC abduction somatic dysfunction.
    *   **Adduction Dysfunction**: When depressing the shoulder, the medial clavicle glides superiorly. If it glides superiorly freely but stays superior and does not move inferiorly upon relaxation, it is an SC adduction somatic dysfunction.
*   **SC Joint Flexion/Extension dysfunctions**:
    *   **Flexion Dysfunction**: When protracting the shoulder, the medial clavicle glides posteriorly. If it remains posterior and resists moving anteriorly upon relaxation (returning to neutral), it is an SC flexion somatic dysfunction.
    *   **Extension Dysfunction**: When retracting the shoulder, the medial clavicle glides anteriorly. If it stays stuck anteriorly and does not move back posteriorly upon returning to neutral, it is an SC extension somatic dysfunction.
*   **Prevalence**: Extension and Adduction are the most common dysfunctions at the SC joint.
*   **Combination Dysfunctions**: A patient can have a combined dysfunction (e.g., an extended and adducted clavicle). Always diagnose and treat the more prominent restriction first, then reassess to see if a secondary dysfunction remains.

:::highyield
**SC Joint Reciprocal Motion**
Because the clavicle acts like a lever system, motion at the distal clavicle creates opposite motion at the medial (SC) clavicle:
- Shoulder Elevation (shrug) -> Distal clavicle goes UP -> Medial clavicle glides INFERIORLY.
- Shoulder Depression -> Distal clavicle goes DOWN -> Medial clavicle glides SUPERIORLY.
- Shoulder Protraction -> Distal clavicle goes ANTERIOR -> Medial clavicle glides POSTERIORLY.
- Shoulder Retraction -> Distal clavicle goes POSTERIOR -> Medial clavicle glides ANTERIORLY.
:::

## Acromioclavicular (AC) Joint Diagnosis (Slide 13-20)
The AC joint is tested using the distal clavicle's motion relative to the acromion. Motion testing is primarily passive and utilizes gross movements of the upper extremity to induce gapping or closing at the AC joint, rather than trying to move the joint directly with the physician's fingers. To palpate properly, place the middle finger over the joint space, with the index and ring fingers on the acromion and distal clavicle, respectively.

*   **AC Joint Superior/Inferior Glides**:
    *   **Inferior Glide ("Step-up")**: The distal clavicle glides inferiorly explicitly but does not move superiorly. The palpating finger feels a "step up" moving from the clavicle to the higher acromion.
    *   **Superior Glide ("Step-down")**: The distal clavicle resists springing inferiorly—it favors a superior position. Moving from the distal clavicle to the acromion feels like a "step down."
*   **AC Joint Adduction/Abduction dysfunctions**:
    *   **Adduction Dysfunction**: Induced by adducting the arm (pulling it across the chest); this physiologically gaps the AC joint. If the joint remains gapped and does not close appropriately during abduction, it is an AC adduction dysfunction.
    *   **Abduction Dysfunction**: Induced by abducting the arm; this physically closes the AC joint space. If it closes well but fails to gap upon adduction, it is an AC abduction dysfunction.
*   **Joint Motion Couples**:
    *   **Abduction** somatic dysfunction frequently couples with **superior** glide and **internal** rotation of the distal clavicle.
    *   **Adduction** somatic dysfunction frequently couples with **inferior** glide and **external** rotation of the distal clavicle.

:::highyield
**AC Joint Diagnosis Rules**
When diagnosing AC joint adduction/abduction, use gross shoulder motions to move the joint. Adducting the patient's arm across the chest will physiologically gap the AC joint. Abducting their arm laterally will close the AC joint space. The dysfunction is named for the position in which the joint is free or "prefers" to rest.
:::

## Glenohumeral (GH) Joint Diagnosis (Slide 21-25)
The GH joint is critical for overall upper extremity function. Osteopathic evaluation here involves establishing both major (gross range of motion) and minor (gliding) motions to ensure proper seating of the humeral head within the glenoid fossa. Analogous to gently wiggling a stuck dresser drawer, restoring minor glides often frees up major motion barriers without requiring excessive force.

*   **Major Motions**:
    *   Flexion, Extension, Abduction, Adduction, Internal, and External Rotation.
*   **Minor Motions (Glides)**:
    *   Evaluated by stabilizing the top of the shoulder (acromion/scapula) and using the active hand to manipulate the proximal humerus.
    *   Specific tests check for Anterior vs. Posterior glides and Superior vs. Inferior glides.
*   **Coupled Mechanics of the GH Joint**:
    *   **Extension / Abduction / External Rotation**: These major motions mechanically pair with an **anterior** glide of the humeral head.
    *   **Flexion / Adduction / Internal Rotation**: These major motions structurally pair with a **posterior** glide of the humeral head.
*   **Clinical Relevance**: Abnormal structural motion at the GH joint can generate pain and contribute to impingement syndromes involving rotator cuff tendons, nerves, and adjacent vasculature.
