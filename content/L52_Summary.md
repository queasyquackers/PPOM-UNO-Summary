## 1. Introduction to Seizures & Epilepsy
**Story Mode:**
A seizure is essentially an electrical storm in the brain, defined as a paroxysmal event due to abnormal, excessive, or synchronous neuronal activity. When a patient has *recurrent* seizures due to a chronic underlying process, we diagnose **Epilepsy**. The goal of pharmacotherapy is to stabilize these neurons, either by putting the brakes on excitation (blocking Sodium/Calcium channels, inhibiting Glutamate) or stepping on the clutch (enhancing GABA inhibition).

### 1.1 Classification (Slide 6, 8)
*   **Focal Onset Seizures:** 
    *   Arise from a specific, discrete local population of neurons in **one** hemisphere.
    *   **Mechanism:** Hyperexcitability due to rapidly firing **Voltage-Gated Sodium Channels**.
    *   **Evolution:** Can evolve into bilateral tonic-clonic seizures.
*   **Primary Generalized Onset Seizures:**
    *   Arise in **both** hemispheres simultaneously.
    *   Involve deep structures (Thalamus/Brainstem).
*   **Status Epilepticus:**
    *   Definition: **>5 minutes** of continuous seizure activity OR >2 seizures without recovery of consciousness in between.

### 1.2 Pharmacokinetics Overview (Slide 15-22)
*   **Absorption/Distribution:** All ASDs (Anti-Seizure Drugs) must cross the BBB. Most are highly protein bound (potential for displacement interactions).
*   **Metabolism:** Most undergo hepatic metabolism.
    *   **Inducers:** Carbamazepine, Phenytoin, Phenobarbital.
    *   **Inhibitor:** Valproate.
*   **Withdrawal:** **Gradual taper** is mandatory. Abrupt cessation can precipitate seizures or status epilepticus.

:::correlate
Suicidality in ASDs

- **Pathophysiology:** Mechanism unclear; controversial FDA warning.
- **Presentation:** New onset suicidal ideation or behavior.
- **Lecturer's Point:** All ASDs carry a warning for increased risk of suicidality. Patients (especially young ones) should be monitored for mood changes.
:::

## 2. Voltage-Gated Sodium Channel Blockers
**Story Mode:**
The "fast" sodium current is responsible for the upstroke of the action potential. In a seizure, neurons fire repetitively at high frequencies. Sodium channel blockers work by binding to the channel in its **Inactivated State**, preventing it from resetting to the active state. This prolongs the refractory period. Importantly, these drugs exhibit **Use-Dependence**: they bind *more* strongly to neurons that are firing rapidly (seizing), while sparing normal neurons firing at normal rates.

### 2.1 Mechanism of Action (Slide 30-35)
*   **The Cycle:** Resting (Closed) $\rightarrow$ Depolarization $\rightarrow$ Open (Na+ in) $\rightarrow$ **Inactivated** (Gate closed) $\rightarrow$ Repolarization $\rightarrow$ Resting.
*   **Fast Inactivation Enhancers:**
    *   Drugs: **Phenytoin, Carbamazepine, Lamotrigine, Oxcarbazepine**.
    *   Bind to the channel pore during the **Open/Inactivated** state.
    *   Stabilize the channel in the inactivated state (refractory).
*   **Slow Inactivation Enhancer:**
    *   Drug: **Lacosamide**.
    *   Stabilizes the "Slow Inactivation" state (a conformational change occurring over seconds during prolonged depolarization).

## 3. Phenytoin (PHT) & Fosphenytoin
**Story Mode:**
Phenytoin is the "classic" dirty drug of neurology. It’s effective, but it follows **Zero-Order Kinetics** (saturation kinetics), meaning small dose increases can lead to exponential spikes in blood levels. It also has a laundry list of cosmetic and teratogenic side effects ("P-H-E-N-Y-T-O-I-N"). Fosphenytoin was developed as a water-soluble prodrug to solve the infusion issues of Phenytoin (Phlebitis/Purple Glove).

### 3.1 Pharmacokinetics (Slide 40-55)
*   **Absorption:**
    *   **Phenytoin Base (Chewable/Suspension):** Erratic absorption.
    *   **Phenytoin Sodium (Capsules):** Better absorption.
*   **Distribution:** Highly protein bound (Albumin).
    *   **Hypoalbuminemia (Liver failure, Nephrotic syndrome, Pregnancy):** Leads to **INCREASED Free Phenytoin** levels $\rightarrow$ Toxicity.
    *   **Displacement:** Uremia (kidney failure) displaces PHT from albumin.
*   **Metabolism (CRITICAL):**
    *   **Zero-Order Kinetics:** Enzymes (CYP2C9/2C19) become **saturated** at therapeutic levels.
    *   Elimination is constant amount per time (not constant fraction).
    *   **Consequence:** Small dose increase $\rightarrow$ **Disproportionately LARGE** increase in serum concentration.
    *   **Induction:** Potent INDUCER of CYP3A4, UGT, etc. (Eats up other drugs).

### 3.2 Formulations: Phenytoin vs. Fosphenytoin (Slide 43-45)
| Feature | Phenytoin (IV) | Fosphenytoin (IV/IM) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Solubility** | Poor (Lipophilic) | High (Water soluble Prodrug) |
| **Solvent** | **Propylene Glycol** + Ethanol (pH 12) | Water based |
| **Adverse Effects** | **Hypotension/Arrhythmias** (due to Propylene Glycol), Phlebitis | Less Hypotension, Minimal irritation |
| **Infusion Rate** | Must be SLOW (risk of chemical burn) | Can be faster |
| **IM Use** | **CONTRAINDICATED** (Tissue necrosis) | Safe for IM |

### 3.3 Adverse Effects (Slide 61-68)
:::mnemonic
**PHENYTOIN Side Effects**
**PHENYTOIN**

- **P:** **P**-450 Inducer / **P**eripheral Neuropathy / **P**urple Glove
- **H:** **H**irsutism (excess hair)
- **E:** **E**nlarged Gums (**Gingival Hyperplasia**) / **E**rythema Multiforme (SJS)
- **N:** **N**ystagmus / Neurotoxicity (Ataxia, Slurred speech)
- **Y:** **Y**ellowing (Hepatotoxicity - rare)
- **T:** **T**eratogenicity (**Fetal Hydantoin Syndrome**)
- **O:** **O**steomalacia (Vit D deficiency from enzyme induction)
- **I:** **I**nterference with **Folate** (Megaloblastic Anemia)
- **N:** **N**europathy
:::

:::correlate
Purple Glove Syndrome

- **Pathophysiology:** Extravasation of IV **Phenytoin** (highly alkaline + propylene glycol).
- **Presentation:** Distal limb edema, discoloration (purple/blue), pain.
- **Complications:** Skin necrosis, compartment syndrome, amputation.
- **Prevention:** Use **Fosphenytoin** instead.
:::

:::correlate
Fetal Hydantoin Syndrome

- **Pathophysiology:** Teratogenic effect of Phenytoin (and Carbamazepine/Valproate).
- **Presentation:** Cleft lip/palate, Microcephaly, Hypoplastic fingernails, IUGR.
- **Risk:** 2-3x risk of major malformations.
- **Management:** Folic acid supplementation (though may not fully prevent).
:::

## 4. Carbamazepine (CBZ) & Pharmacogenetics
**Story Mode:**
Carbamazepine is chemically related to Tricyclic Antidepressants. It is a potent sodium channel blocker used for focal seizures. Its claim to fame is **Auto-Induction** (it induces its own metabolism, so half-life decreases over the first few weeks) and severe, genetically determined skin reactions.

### 4.1 Pharmacogenetics (Slide 23-28)
Screening is **MANDATORY** in at-risk populations before starting Carbamazepine (and chemically related Oxcarbazepine/Phneytoin/Lamotrigine).

*   **HLA-B*1502:**
    *   **Population:** **Asian** ancestry (Han Chinese, South Asian).
    *   **Risk:** **SJS / TEN** (Stevens-Johnson Syndrome / Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis).
    *   **Action:** **SCREEN** all Asian patients. If positive $\rightarrow$ AVOID Carbamazepine.
*   **HLA-A*3101:**
    *   **Population:** European, Japanese, Korean.
    *   **Risk:** **DRESS** (Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms).

:::highyield
**Auto-Induction**
Carbamazepine induces CYP3A4, the very enzyme that metabolizes it.
*   **Effect:** Serum levels **DROP** over the first few weeks of therapy despite constant dosing.
*   **Management:** Dose may need to be increased after 3-4 weeks once auto-induction is maximal.
:::

## 5. Lamotrigine (LTG)
**Story Mode:**
Lamotrigine is the "Clean" broad-spectrum agent. It blocks Sodium channels AND inhibits Glutamate release. It is effective for almost everything (Focal, Generalized, Absence, Bipolar). Its major Achilles heel is the **Life-Threatening Rash** (SJS), which is why we must titrate it painfully slowly ("Start low, go slow").

### 5.1 Pharmacokinetics & Dosing (Slide 69-75)
*   **Interaction with Valproate:**
    *   **Valproate** inhibits UGT (glucuronidation).
    *   **Effect:** **DOUBLES** Lamotrigine half-life (60 hours vs 24 hours).
    *   **Risk:** Massive increase in **SJS/Rash risk**.
    *   **Management:**reduce dose by 50% also titrate even **SLOWER** (every other day dosing initially).
*   **Interaction with Inducers (Phenytoin/Carbamazepine):**
    *   Decreases Lamotrigine half-life.
    *   Management: Higher loading/maintenance doses needed.

### 5.2 Clinical Uses
*   Broad Spectrum: Focal, Generalized Tonic-Clonic, **Lennox-Gastaut Syndrome**, Absence (2nd line).
*   **Bipolar Disorder:** Maintenance treatment (prevents Depressive episodes); NOT for acute Mania.

## 6. Lacosamide (LCM)
**Story Mode:**
Lacosamide is the newer, cleaner cousin. Instead of fast inactivation, it enhances **Slow Inactivation**. It has excellent bioavailability (100%), minimal protein binding, and renal excretion (fewer drug interactions). The one specific catch is the heart.

### 6.1 Mechanism & Side Effects (Slide 80-87)
*   **Mechanism:** Enhances **Slow Inactivation** of Voltage-Gated Na+ Channels.
*   **Key Toxicity:** **PR Interval Prolongation**.
    *   **Risk:** Bradycardia, AV Block.
    *   **Action:** Get a baseline **ECG** in patients with known cardiac conduction issues.
*   **Pharmacokinetics:**
    *   Renally excreted (40% unchanged).
    *   No major CYP interactions (unlike Phenytoin/Carbamazepine).

## 7. Summary Tables

### 7.1 Sodium Channel Blockers Comparison
| Drug | Mechanism | Key PK Feature | Key Toxicity | Clinical Pearl |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Phenytoin** | Fast Inactivation | **Zero-Order Kinetics**; Potent Inducer | Gingival Hyperplasia, Hirsutism, **Purple Glove**, Osteomalacia | Fosphenytoin is safer for IV. |
| **Carbamazepine** | Fast Inactivation | **Auto-Inducer** | **SJS (HLA-B*1502)**, DRESS, Hyponatremia (SIADH) | Screen Asians for HLA-B*1502. |
| **Lamotrigine** | Fast Inactivation + Glutamate Inhibit | Valproate **Doubles** Half-life | **SJS (Rash)** | **"Start Low, Go Slow"** titration. |
| **Lacosamide** | **Slow** Inactivation | Renal Elimination | **PR Prolongation** | Get ECG aimed at cardiac patients. |

:::highyield
**Teratogenicity Hierarchy**
1.  **Valproate:** HIGHEST Risk (Neural Tube Defects/Spina Bifida) - AVOID.
2.  **Phenytoin/Carbamazepine:** High Risk (Cleft palate, cardiac defects).
3.  **Lamotrigine/Levetiracetam:** Safest options for pregnancy.
:::
