--- SLIDE 7 ---
Endothelial Injury
• Severe endothelial injury exposes vWF and tissue factor: may trigger
thrombosis
• Inflammation promotes thrombosis: shifts gene expression in endothelium
to “prothrombotic.” (i.e., endothelial activation or dysfunction) – causes
include:
• physical injury
• infectious agents
• abnormal blood flow
• inflammatory mediators
• metabolic abnormalities (e.g.,hypercholesterolemia or homocystinemia)
• toxins absorbed from cigarette smoke
• Procoagulant changes: e.g.,: endothelial cells activated by inflammatory
cytokines, downregulate expression of thrombomodulin
procoagulant and pro-inflammatory actions of thrombin enhanced
• Antifibrinolytic effects: activated endothelial cells secrete plasminogen
activator inhibitors (PAIs) (limit fibrinolysis and downregulate expression of t-
PA) – favors development of thrombi
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 4

--- SLIDE 8 ---
Turbulence (Abnormal blood flow)
• Turbulence contributes to arterial and cardiac thrombosis by causing
endothelial injury/ dysfunction and forming countercurrents
(contribute to stasis)
• Turbulence and stasis:
• Promote endothelial activation, enhancing procoagulant activity
• Disrupt laminar flow- bring platelets into contact with endothelium
• Prevent washout and dilution of activated clotting factors by fresh flowing
blood and inflow of clotting factor inhibitors
• Examples:
• Ulcerated atherosclerotic plaques expose subendothelial vWF and
tissue factor, (also cause turbulence)
• Aneurysms result in local stasis
• Acute myocardial infarction - areas of noncontractile myocardium
associated with stasis and flow abnormalities (promote formation of
cardiac mural thrombi)
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 4

Hypercoagulability
• Abnormal tendency of blood to clot, usually caused by
changes in coagulation factors
• Important in venous thrombosis
• Divided into primary (genetic) and secondary (acquired)
disorders
• Primary include:
• Factor V Leiden
• Prothrombin gene mutation
• Elevated levels of homocysteine
• Rare inherited causes of primary hypercoagulability:
deficiencies of anticoagulants (e.g., antithrombin III,
protein C, or protein S); present with venous
thrombosis and recurrent thromboembolism beginning in

Pathology of Thrombosis
Thrombi
• Size and shape vary, depend on site and underlying cause;
obstruct arteries or veins, or embolize
• Gross and microscopic finding: lines of Zahn
• pale platelet and fibrin deposits alternating with darker red cell–rich
layers
• indicate thrombus formed in flowing blood (antemortem); versus
bland, non-laminated, postmortem clots
• Focally attached to underlying vascular surface
• Arterial thrombi (atherosclerotic plaques, MI) tend to grow
retrograde, and venous thrombi (DVTs ) extend in direction of
blood flow (both propagate toward heart)

--- SLIDE 14 ---
Pathology of Thrombosis
• Venous thrombi
• can cause painful congestion and edema distal to obstruction
• may embolize to lungs; almost always occlusive
• Form long luminal cast – form in sluggish venous circulation - usually occur at sites of
stasis
• aka red thrombi; firm, focally attached to vessel wall, contain lines of Zahn
• veins of lower extremities most commonly involved (90% of cases)
• Arterial thrombi
• can also embolize; cause downstream infarctions; often occlusive
• most common sites: coronary, cerebral, and femoral arteries (decreasing order of
frequency)
• Arterial or cardiac thrombi usually begin at sites of turbulence or endothelial injury
• Thrombi in heart chambers aka mural thrombi, esp. seen in:
• abnormal myocardial contraction (e.g., arrhythmias, dilated cardiomyopathy,
myocardial infarction)
• endomyocardial injury (myocarditis or catheter trauma)
• Postmortem clots - gelatinous with dark-red dependent portion (red cells settled by gravity)
and yellow “chicken fat” upper
Robbins and Cotran, Pathoogic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2021
--- SLIDE 15 ---
Venous Thrombosis (Phlebothrombosis)
• Most occur in superficial or deep veins of leg
• Superficial venous thrombi usually occur in saphenous veins in setting of
varicosities; can cause local congestion, swelling, pain, and tenderness,
rarely embolize
• Deep venous thrombus involving large leg veins (e.g., popliteal, femoral,
and iliac)— more often embolize to lungs; may cause pulmonary infarction
• DVTs may cause local pain and edema due to venous obstruction, but
because of venous collateral channels, about 50% of DVTs asymptomatic;
recognized only after embolization
• Lower extremity DVTs often associated with hypercoagulable states;
common predisposing factors - bed rest and immobilization, congestive
heart failure; trauma, surgery, and burns cause immobilization and also
vascular insults
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 4

--- SLIDE 20 ---
FACTOR V LEIDEN
• Most common hereditary thrombosis syndrome, 2% to 15% of Caucasians are
carriers
• Autosomal Dominant with incomplete penetrance
• Single nucleotide mutation makes Factor V resistant to cleavage and inactivation
by protein C (loss of antithrombotic regulator)
• Heterozygotes: 5X increased risk venous thrombosis, homozygotes 50X increase
• Factor V Leiden heterozygote (usually asymptomatic) may develop DVTs when
combined with inactivity (e.g., prolonged airplane travel)
• Mutation frequency near 60% in patients with recurrent DVTs
• Patients < 50 years old with thrombotic events should be considered for any
clotting disorder, especially Factor V Leiden — even when acquired risk factors are
present
• Diagnosis: Activated Protein C (APC) Assay (failure to prolong PTT with addition of
purified protein C) or genetic testing
Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, – Chapter 4

Protein C & S Deficiency
• Natural anticoagulants; made by liver, need to be activated
• Vitamin K-dependent proteins that serves as a cofactor for activated protein
C to inactivate the procoagulant factors Va and VIIIa, thereby reducing
thrombin generation
• Mutation results in inability to inactivate factors V & VIII; results in
increased thrombin formation
• Individuals have increased risk of thrombotic skin necrosis with warfarin
CLINICAL CORRELATE: if patient is placed on warfarin therapy, the liver will
not produce factors 2,7,9,10, protein C and S. All pre-existing protein C and S
will degrade first leaving pre-existing factors 2,7,9,10 to be relatively high
• Need Heparin bridge
• Presents with venous thrombosis and recurrent thromboembolism beginning
in adolescence/early adulthood

Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) Syndrome
• Thrombocytopenia most common manifestation (about 5% of persons
receiving unfractionated heparin (see Path of Hemorrhage lecture);
thrombosis most serious complication occurring in ~50% of cases; affects
both veins and arteries
• Diagnosis: demonstration of anti–PF4-heparin antibodies
• Low-molecular-weight heparin preparations induce HIT less frequently
• May present with sudden onset of pain, redness, and swelling of an arm or
leg; ecchymosis, necrosis of skin, gangrene, stroke or MI may develop
• 2 Types:
• HIT Type I: (non-immune) asymptomatic transient drop of platelets, returns
to normal
• HIT Type II: (immune mediated) Clinically serious condition
• Thrombosis may occur in either veins or arteries
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch.4

Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome (APS)
• Autoimmune disorder characterized by:
• Presence of one or more antiphospholipid (aPL) autoantibodies
• Venous or arterial thromboses, or pregnancy complications (e.g., recurrent
miscarriages, unexplained fetal death, and premature birth.)
• Primary or secondary (e.g., lupus)
• Primary APS: patients only have hypercoagulable state and no evidence of
autoimmune diseases
• Presence of Antiphospholipid autoantibodies
• Anticardiolipin antibodies (IgG or IgM)
• Anti-beta-2-glycoprotein-I antibodies (IgG or IgM)
• Lupus anticoagulants
• Various clinical manifestions: recurrent thromboses, repeated miscarriages,
cardiac valve vegetations, and thrombocytopenia
• Clinical presentations can include pulmonary embolism (following lower
extremity venous thrombosis), pulmonary hypertension (from recurrent
subclinical pulmonary emboli), valvular heart disease, stroke, bowel
infarction, or renovascular hypertension
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch.4
30
--- SLIDE 31 ---
Antiphospholipid Syndrome - Pathogenesis
• Not fully understood: APL antibodies directed against
anionic membrane phospholipids or proteins associated
with phospholipids (include cardiolipin and β 2 -glycoprotein I)
• Anti–β 2 -glycoprotein antibodies suspected to have major
role in APS by activating endothelial cells, monocytes, and
platelets
• Patients with APS also show evidence of complement activation
and inhibition of fibrinolytic processes: both favor
prothrombotic state
• Pregnancy morbidity: defining manifestation of APS; fetal
loss not thought to be caused by thrombosis, but from
antibody-mediated interference with growth and
differentiation of trophoblasts: leads to failure of
placentation
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch.4

APL Antibodies
• Antiphospholipid antibodies also identified in 5% to 15% of normal
individuals, implying Abs not sufficient to cause full-blown
syndrome
• Antibodies induce hypercoagulable state in vivo- but interfere with
phospholipids and inhibit coagulation in vitro, prolonging the
PTT
• Mixing study: patient plasma mixed with control plasma does not
correct aPTT
• Often false-positive serologic test for syphilis because the
antigen in standard assay is embedded in cardiolipin, which cross
reacts with phospholipids of Treponema pallidum
• Diagnosis based on clinical features and demonstration of APL
antibodies in serum
• Therapy - various forms of anticoagulation

Adapted Osmosis/USMLE example question
• A 25-year-old Caucasian woman presents to the ED due to difficulty breathing. Two hours
ago, she suddenly felt chest pain accompanied by dyspnea. The patient denies any recent
trauma, and she does not smoke or use illicit drugs. She is on no meds including
contraception pills. She is afebrile. On physical examination, the patient appears
distressed. Heart sounds are normal and lungs are clear to auscultation. Labs show an
elevated d-dimer. PT and PTT are 12 (normal) and 10 seconds (decreased), respectively.
Purified protein C is added to the patient’s plasma and causes no change in the lab values.
Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?
• A. Protein S deficiency
• B. Prothrombin gene mutation
• C. Antiphospholipid syndrome
• D. Antithrombin deficiency
• E. Factor V Leiden deficiency

--- SLIDE 36 ---
INCORRECT ANSWERS EXPLANATIONS
• A. Protein S deficiency: Although protein S deficiency is difficult to distinguish from factor V Leiden deficiency,
the former is far rarer and therefore less likely.
• B. Prothrombin gene mutation: Prothrombin, also known as factor II, is the precursor of thrombin, the end
product of the coagulation cascade. Thrombin’s main role is to proteolytically cleave fibrinogen to fibrin,
which in turn crosslinks to form a fibrin clot. It also interacts with other hemostatic components. The
G20210A point mutation in the prothrombin gene results in a gain-of-function of prothrombin that leads to
hypercoagulability. However, the failure to change PTT after adding purified protein C favors another
diagnosis.
• C. Antiphospholipid syndrome: Autoimmune condition characterized by venous and/or arterial thrombosis
due to the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, anti-β2 glycoprotein
I antibodies). It is most commonly associated with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Patients with APS
often show prolonged PTT. In contrast, this patient shows no features of SLE and has reduced PTT.
• D. Antithrombin deficiency: Antithrombin (AT, previously called AT III) deficiency may be acquired or inherited
in an autosomal dominant fashion with variable penetration. Antithrombin is a natural anticoagulant that
inhibits thrombin (factor IIa), factor Xa, and other factors in the coagulation cascade. A variety of mutations
have been recognized. AT has no direct effect on the PT, PTT, or thrombin time. Instead, it diminishes the
increase in PTT following heparin administration. This phenomenon exists since heparin, an indirect inhibitor
of thrombin and factor Xa, requires functional AT to be effective.
https://www.osmosis.org/blog/2021/04/07/usmle-step-1-question-of-the-day-factor-v-
leiden

FATE OF THROMBUS
• Propagation: additional platelets/fibrin attach and increase the size
of thrombus
• Embolization: thrombi dislodge, travel to other sites in vasculature
• Dissolution/Resolution: result of fibrinolysis, which can lead to
rapid shrinkage and total disappearance of recent thrombi
• extensive fibrin deposition and cross-linking in older thrombi: more resistant
to lysis (therapeutic administration of fibrinolytic agents such as t-PA (e.g., in
acute coronary thrombosis) usually effective only when given during first few
hours of thrombotic event
• Organization and recanalization: older thrombi become
organized by ingrowth of endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells,
and fibroblasts; capillary channels eventually form within
thrombus which allows blood flow
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 4

Embolism
Embolus - Detached intravascular solid, liquid, or gaseous
mass carried by blood from point of origin to distant site,
often causing tissue dysfunction or infarction
• Vast majority are dislodged thrombi (thromboembolism)
• Other rare emboli - fat droplets, nitrogen bubbles,
atherosclerotic debris (cholesterol emboli), tumor
fragments, bone marrow, or foreign bodies
Emboli travel through blood, reach vessels too small to pass
through, cause partial or complete vascular occlusion
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 4

--- SLIDE 43 ---
Pulmonary Embolism
• Originate from DVT (95%); most common form
of thromboembolic disease
• Fragmented thrombi travel through progressively
larger veins to right heart and “slam” into
pulmonary vasculature; depending on embolus
size can:
• occlude main pulmonary artery
• straddle pulmonary artery bifurcation
(saddle embolus), or
• pass into smaller, branching arteries
• Often multiple emboli, occur sequentially or
simultaneously, as shower of smaller emboli from
single large mass
• Most pulmonary emboli (60% to 80%) small and
clinically silent

Major Consequences of PE
• When emboli obstruct 60% or more of pulmonary circulation: sudden
death, acute right heart failure (cor pulmonale), or cardiovascular
collapse
• Embolic obstruction of medium-sized arteries with subsequent vascular
rupture: can result in pulmonary hemorrhage – usually NOT pulmonary
infarction (note: lung supplied by both pulmonary arteries and bronchial
arteries; intact bronchial circulation can usually sufficiently perfuse
affected area
• Embolic obstruction of small end-arteriolar pulmonary branches: often
results in hemorrhage or infarction
• Multiple emboli over time may cause pulmonary hypertension and right
ventricular failure

--- SLIDE 45 ---
Systemic Thromboembolism
• Arise in or affect arterial system
• Most systemic emboli (80%) from intracardiac mural thrombi
• 2/3rds associated with left ventricular wall infarcts (stasis and
endothelial damage)
• 1/4th with left atrial dilation and fibrillation (stasis)
• remainder originate from aortic aneurysms, atherosclerotic plaques,
valvular vegetations, or venous thrombi (paradoxical emboli)
• 10% to 15% unknown origin
• Arterial emboli can travel to many sites (v. venous thrombi, majority travels
to lung); most go to lower extremities (75%) or brain (10%); other tissues
(e.g., intestines, kidneys, spleen, and upper extremities) may be involved
• Usually result in tissue infarction

Air Embolism
• Occurs when communication between vasculature and outside air exists;
negative pressure gradient “sucks” in air
• Air may be introduced during endovascular and invasive procedures, mechanical
ventilation; large volume of air (more than 100 mL) needed to cause effect in
pulmonary circulation
• Introduction of 300 to 500 mL of air at 100 mL/sec may be fatal
• Coronary Bypass Surgery, Laparoscopic Abdominal or Obstretic Surgery, Central
Line Placement, Chest wall injuries, Scuba & Deep sea divers, Underwater
Construction
• Air in pulmonary vasculature blocks perfusion of downstream region; and
microemboli of air trapped in pulmonary capillaries cause intense inflammatory
response with release of cytokines that can injure alveoli
• Bubbles in central nervous system can cause mental impairment and sudden
onset of coma
• Classic presentation: sudden collapse, neurologic symptoms that mimic stroke,
apnea, and even cardiovascular collapse
Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020 – Chapter 4
--- SLIDE 51 ---
Decompression sickness
• Form of gas embolism - patients experience sudden decreases in atmospheric
pressure
• Scuba, deep sea divers, and underwater construction workers
• When air is breathed at high pressure (e.g.,deep sea dive), increased amounts of
gas (especially nitrogen) dissolve in blood and tissues; if diver ascends
(depressurizes) too rapidly, nitrogen comes out of solution in tissues and blood
• Rapid formation of gas bubbles within skeletal muscles and supporting tissues in/
around joints: causes pain (the bends)
• In lungs, gas bubbles in vasculature cause edema, hemorrhage, and focal
atelectasis or emphysema; respiratory distress (the chokes)
• Chronic form of decompression sickness: Caisson disease: persistence of gas
emboli in skeletal system leading to many foci of ischemic necrosis; more
common sites: femoral heads, tibia, and humeri
• Acute decompression sickness: treated by putting patient in a chamber under
enough high pressure to force gas bubbles back into solution; subsequent slow
decompression allows gradual resorption and exhalation of gases, preventing
obstructive bubbles from reforming
Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020 – Chapter 4

Amniotic Fluid Embolism
• Fifth most common cause of maternal mortality worldwide; about 10%
of maternal deaths in US; results in permanent neurologic deficit in up
to 85% of survivors
• Incidence approximately 2 to 6 in 100,000 deliveries; mortality rate up
to 80%
• Onset: sudden severe dyspnea, cyanosis, and shock, followed by
neurologic impairment (ranging from headache to seizures and coma),
and DIC
• Morbidity/mortality mostly from:
• biochemical activation of coagulation factors
• components of the innate immune system, and
• release of vasoactive substances that cause INTENSE VASOCONSTRICION in
pulmonary circulation pulmonary hypertension and right heart
failure as well as DIC (rather than obstruction)
• leads to hypoxia, left heart failure, pulmonary edema, and diffuse alveolar
damage
Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020– Chapter 4

Summary Slide
• Morphology of thrombi – lines of Zahn v. post-mortem clots, Virchow’s triad (endothelial injury,
abnormal blood flow, hypercoagulable state)
• Primary thrombotic (clotting) disorders
• Factor V Leiden – AD, most common, Factor V resistant to cleavage by protein C
• Prothrombin gene mutation – AD, increase prothrombin, increased thrombin
• Homocysteinemia – endothelial damage, prothrombotic effects
• Anti-thrombin III deficiency – def. leads to increased thrombin
• Protein C and S deficiencies – inability to inactivate factors V and VIII: results in increased thrombin
• Secondary thrombotic (clotting) disorders
• Malignancies, OCP, pregnancy, age, hyperviscosity
• Heparin induced thrombocytopenia – induced IgG autoAbs to heparin complexes and platelet
factor 4
• Anti-Phospholipid Ab syndrome – recurrent thrombosis, repeated miscarriages, decreased
platelets
• Fate of thrombi – propagation, recanalization, embolization, dissolution
• Venous v. arterial and cardiac thrombi
• Emboli – pulmonary emboli, paradoxical emboli, systemic emboli, fat emboli, air emboli, and amniotic
fluid emboli
