• Urticaria:
Intensely pruritic, well-demarcated, pink, annular
swellings of superficial dermis with central pallor and
erythematous “flare” border; wheal/hive; can rarely
progress to anaphylaxis which is life threatening
Causes: often foods and medications
→ take thorough history

Angioedema
• Angioedema:
Swelling of deeper dermal and subcutaneous
tissues tongue, lips, mouth, face, neck and lower
airway → may lead to airway compromise
Two subtypes:
1. Autosomal dominant hereditary variant
due to C1-esterase inhibitor deficiency
2. Acquired, secondary to angiotensin-
converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor (due to
increased bradykinin) or nonsteroidal
anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)
https://emcrit.org/pulmcrit/treatment-of-acei-
induced-angioedema/
--- SLIDE 8 ---
Angioedema
• Management/Treatment:
EARLY INTUBATION; Supply O2 to maintain >90% SpO2
Determine cause:
If due to medication (such as ACE inhibitor) discontinue
If urticaria also present, potential allergic trigger (meds, food, insect sting…)
Tx: Antihistamines, glucocorticoids, epinephrine
If hereditary variant,
Tx: Icatibant (a bradykinin inhibitor); Ecallantide (Kallikrein inhibitor); C1
esterase inhibitor; fresh frozen plasma if other tx not available
For pt with anaphylaxis: Prescribe autoinjector epinephrine
Identify trigger, avoid allergens, pt education
• Disposition: ICU

--- SLIDE 10 ---
Erythema Multiforme (EM)
• Cause: 90% caused by infections; 10% caused by drug
Herpes Simplex Virus is #1 infectious cause (also Mycoplasma
pneumoniae)
• Pathogenesis: EM develops 2/2 HSV infx via cell-mediated immune
response directed at viral antigens deposited in lesioned skin
• Diagnosis: clinical; targetoid lesions with central dusky appearance
EM Minor: EM without (or only mild) mucosal disease and without
associated systemic sx; resolves easily
EM Major: EM with severe mucosal involvement (may have systemic
symptoms such as fever and arthralgias); conjunctivitis, chelitis
• Symptoms: benign or malaise, low grade fever; targetoid skin eruptions
develop over several days; may be pruritic or painful
--- SLIDE 11 ---
Erythema Multiforme (EM)
• Course/prognosis:
Lesions appear over 3-5 days (soles and palms)
Resolves within 14 days
Usually don’t scar, but can be hyperpigmented for months
Avoid recurrence usually by prophylaxis for HSV; if drug induced then avoid the drug
• Management/Treatment:
Treat underlying cause-
ex: antivirals (acyclovir) if HSV-related (and may be used to prevent recurrence)
Systemic steroids if severe
Dermatologist consult
Supportive care: fluid management, electrolyte, infectious, nutritional, thermoregulatory
issues, pain control

Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) and
Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)
• SJS/TEN: epidermal detachment which is severe, life-threatening condition
SJS: <10% of body surface area with epidermal detachment
TEN: >30% of body surface area with epidermal detachment
• NEED TO RECOGNIZE RIGHT AWAY TO KEEP PT ALIVE
• Cause: typically, drug-induced; may have several doses of these meds before getting this reaction
Antibiotics: Sulfa meds and penicillin
Barbiturates
Anticonvulsants: lamotrigine, phenytoin, carbamazepine
NSAIDs
Allopurinol
• Pathogenesis: predominantly a drug-specific CD8+ cytotoxic T cell-mediated reaction → epidermal necrolysis
Potential genetic predisposition (HLA)
• Diagnosis: Clinical; + Nikolsky sign (the ability to extend the area of epidermal detachment with gentle lateral
pressure); Skin biopsy shows keratinocyte necrosis

--- SLIDE 16 ---
Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) and
Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)
• Symptoms:
About 1-3 weeks between first drug exposure and symptom onset
Prodrome: 1 -2 weeks of fever, arthralgia, anorexia before skin
eruption
Rash: irregular shaped, dusky red, painful macules on trunk that quickly
spread to upper extremities and face and coalesce; epidermis
begins to slough off; progresses over hours to days; mucous
membrane involvement (pain with swallowing)
• Pt is very aware- feels burning; tachycardic and hypertensive
--- SLIDE 17 ---
Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) and
Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN)
• Prognosis: dependent on % skin necrosis
SJS mortality rate: 5%
TEN mortality rate: 20+%
• At increased risk for electrolyte imbalance, multi-organ failure, infection, dehydration
• Management/Treatment:
Treated like a severe burn → ICU/burn unit care
D/C offending drug; future avoidance of all drugs that may cause SJS (2nd time will be worse)
Supportive care: correct electrolyte abnormalities, fluid replacement, pain control
If necessary: airway protection/ventilation; abx to avoid sepsis
• Sequalae: ocular (could lose vision); oral, pulmonary, dermatologic, genital (painful sex in future),
psych (this is a pretty traumatizing disease)

--- SLIDE 19 ---
Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome
• Epidemiology: Affects primarily children
• Cause: Skin barrier broken and leads to staphylococcus aureus infection, due to S.
aureus exfoliative toxin
• Diagnosis: clinical; + Nikolsky sign
• Symptoms:
Fever, irritability, purulent conjunctivitis, rhinorrhea, tender skin
Skin becomes scalded, blistered, erythematous and edematous with flaccid bullae
Epidermis desquamates over 3-5 days leaving behind tender raw skin similar to burn
Mucous membranes spared**
• Management/Treatment:
IV antibiotics (nafcillin, oxacillin)
Supportive care: fluids, wound care, rehydration, thermoregulation, pain control

--- SLIDE 21 ---
Toxic Shock Syndrome
• Cause: associated with tampon use; can be caused by other types of packing:
wound, nasal, and abscess
• Pathogenesis: Group A Streptococcus exotoxins (A, B, C) or Staphylococcus
aureus (TSST-1); Superantigen-mediated T-cell activation → massive cytokine
release → capillary leakage, hypotension, multiorgan failure
• Prognosis: Mortality rate of 6%, less with appropriate treatment
• Diagnosis: GAS or S. aureus, fever >102 F, hypotension, rash,
desquamation, plus at least 2 of the following: diarrhea, vomiting, renal
impairment, elevated liver enzymes, respiratory distress syndrome,
disseminated intravascular coagulopathy, cutaneous erythema, soft tissue
inflammation, or necrosis
--- SLIDE 22 ---
Toxic Shock Syndrome
• Symptoms: fever >102 F, desquamative rash on the palms and soles,
hypotension, multisystem organ involvement of three or more systems
1-2 weeks after illness onset: pruritic maculopapular rash
1-3 weeks after illness onset: desquamation, often of the palms and soles
• Management/Treatment:
Emergent removal of source of infection (i.e. remove the tampon/packing)
Supportive care: IV fluids, vasopressors if needed
IV antibiotics (clindamycin + vancomycin or nafcillin)
• Prevention: Proper tampon use, wound care, early treatment of infections with
antibiotics

--- SLIDE 25 ---
Pemphigus Vulgaris
• Cause: Autoimmune; IgG antibodies against desmoglein 3 leading to
blistering eruption
• Diagnosis: + Nikolsky sign; skin biopsy shows IgG deposits in a
reticular pattern along dermal epidermal junction on direct
immunofluorescence
• Symptoms: flaccid bullae that rupture, leading to painful areas prone to
secondary infx; mucosal involvement
• Management/Treatment:
Admit patient
Plasmapheresis and IVIG; Steroids and immunosuppressives
Treat fluid and electrolyte disturbances aggressively
Dermatology consult

--- SLIDE 27 ---
Drug reaction with eosinophilia and
systemic symptoms (DRESS)
• Epidemiology: 0.9 to 2 per 100,000 patients per year
• Pathogenesis: clear drug trigger in 80% of DRESS cases; associated with HLA genes; Delayed Type IV
T cell-mediated hypersensitivity reaction → severe drug eruption, organ involvement and peripheral
eosinophilia
• Prognosis: The mortality rate among patients with DRESS is estimated to be between 2 and 10 percent
• Symptoms:
The latency phase: time from drug initiation to onset of sx, 2-8 wks
Extensive skin rash, fever ≥101.3°F and visceral organ involvement, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia
In contrast with SJS/TEN, mucosal involvement mild and skin detachment rarely seen
Liver injury is the most common visceral manifestation
Facial edema
Can be mild, moderate or severe
--- SLIDE 28 ---
Drug reaction with eosinophilia and
systemic symptoms (DRESS)
• Management/Treatment:
- Patients with severe DRESS: admit to hospital; organ support; ICU
- Identify and withdraw causative medication
- Avoid introducing new medications
- Long taper of systemic corticosteroids
- Supportive care includes fluid, electrolyte, and nutritional support
- Gentle skin care, warm baths/wet dressings
- Follow up with multiple specialists (ex: hepatologist, nephrologist, pulmonologist)

Meningococcemia
• Epidemiology: most common in patients younger than 20 years
with most patients presenting younger than 5 years; Mortality 10-14%
• Cause: fatal systemic infection due to gram-negative diplococcus Neisseria meningitides; transmission via
aerosolized droplets
• Risk Factors: asplenia; complement deficiency; crowding (prisons, dorms..)
• Diagnosis: lumbar puncture
• Symptoms: develop 3-4 days after exposure and rapidly progresses to severe illness
Headache, fever, altered mental status, nausea/vomiting, myalgia, arthralgia, neck stiffness, hypotension
Dermatologic findings: petechial/purpuric rash
• Management/Treatment:
Contact public health officials
Admit pt to ICU for close monitoring; Supportive care
Early antibiotic treatment w IV third-generation cephalosporin + IV vancomycin
ABC of resuscitation should be performed with IV fluid resuscitation for signs of shock
• Prevention: vaccination

Necrotizing Fasciitis
• Epidemiology: 0.4 per 100,000 people annually in the United States
• Aggressive, rapidly progressing skin and soft tissue infections → bacterial toxin release →
muscle fascia and subcutaneous tissue necrosis and septic shock
• Cause:
can occur post-surgery, invasive procedures, or even minor procedure like
phlebotomy, due to breaking skin integrity; most commonly gram-positive cocci
Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococci, Clostridum, anaerobes
• Risk Factors: diabetes; immunosuppressed; trauma; liver cirrhosis; hx of alcoholism
• Prognosis: mortality rates range from 20 to 80%
Poor prognosis: certain streptococcal strains, advanced age, uncontrolled diabetes,
immunosuppression, and delayed surgery
--- SLIDE 40 ---
Necrotizing Fasciitis
• Diagnosis: must have high clinical suspicion when pt in intense pain, as infection may spread
rapidly within hours
• Signs and symptoms:
excruciating pain out of proportion to presenting symptoms
systemic septic signs
tenderness to palpation beyond the erythematous border
cellulitis
bullae, ecchymosis of skin, paresthesia
Subcutaneous emphysema and crepitus
• Management/Treatment:
ASAP Surgery consult, needs immediate debridement; keep pt NPO
Ultimately transfer to ICU
Aggressive fluid resuscitation, pressors to counteract hypotension in septic shock
Early empiric broad spectrum antibacterial coverage

--- SLIDE 45 ---
Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus (HZO)
•
Epidemiology:
HZO represents about 10% to 20% of all herpes zoster cases
•
Pathogenesis: latent VZV in trigeminal ganglion reactivates, affecting the
ophthalmic branch of CN V → vesicular rash in dermatomal distribution
•
Diagnosis: clinical recognition of the characteristic rash along the ophthalmic
dermatome; Hutchinson’s sign (vesicular lesions on nose- nasociliary branch of
the ophthalmic branch of CN V)
•
AN OPHTHALMIC EMERGENCY! Can lead to vision loss
--- SLIDE 46 ---
Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus (HZO)
•
Symptoms:
Prodrome:
vague symptoms; unilateral pain, photophobia, blurry vision, headache, and/or
vision changes (no rash yet!)
Active phase:
1-5 days after prodrome; skin lesions develop: herpetiform cluster of erythematous
vesicles; evolve through stages of pustulation, ulceration, and crusting; lesions
appear unilaterally along the ophthalmic dermatome and do not cross the midline
Chronic Phase:
can persist for months to years; chronic pain and hyperesthesia along affected
dermatome
Pain of HZO >30 days or after the resolution of the cutaneous lesions=
postherpetic neuralgia (PHN)

--- SLIDE 49 ---
Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (RMSF)
•
Acute febrile tick-borne illness caused by Rickettsia rickettsia
•
Transmitted by bite from American dog tick (Dermacentor variabilis), the Rocky Mountain wood
tick (Dermacentor andersoni); endemic areas include southeastern and south-central US
•
Mortality rate 20 to 30 percent without antibiotic treatment
•
Diagnosis: high index of suspicion of RMSF in pt with flu-like sx during summer months,
regardless of known tick exposure if they reside in or have history of travel to endemic areas
•
Pathogenesis: infects vascular endothelial cells lining small and medium vessels →
disseminated inflammation, loss of barrier function, and altered vascular permeability in body →
systemic sx
•
Symptoms: Begin 4-10 days after tick bite
Triad: fever, HA, petechial or maculopapular rash starting at wrists and ankles
Also, may present with myalgia, confusion, cardiovascular instability, lymphadenopathy
--- SLIDE 50 ---
Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (RMSF)
•
Treatment:
Doxycycline, even in children (concerns of tooth staining should not delay treatment due
to high mortality rates of RMSF); Do not delay tx while awaiting confirmatory lab tests

Burns
•
Etiology: common in household and industrial
accidents (chemical- paint thinner, gasoline);
abuse; electric currents; fire; hot liquid; hot metal;
steam; UV radiation
•
Direct tissue damage → fluid loss and infx risk
•
Evaluation: https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/ep/surge/burn/tbsa.html
Type of Burn: thermal (86%), chemical (3%), electrical (4%), or radiation
Extent of burn: percentage of total body surface area (rule of 9’s)
Depth of burn: superficial, superficial partial-thickness, deep partial-
thickness, full-thickness, and deeper burn injury

Burns
• Management/Treatment:
Minor burns:
Cool with tap water or saline solution to prevent progression of burning and to reduce pain.
Clean with mild soap and water or mild antibacterial wash.
Large blisters debrided; small blisters and blisters involving the palms or soles are left
Cover with topical antibiotic ointments/creams with absorbent or specialized burn dressing material
Comfort with OTC pain medications or prescription pain medications when needed
For burns classified as severe (> 10% Total Body SA):
Should be referred to burn center
IV fluid resuscitation to maintain urine output
Pain control
Wound care
Topical antimicrobials
If severe, skin grafting

Kawasaki Disease
• Epidemiology: almost exclusively (90-95%) in children younger than 10 years; Asian
population
• Pathogenesis: immune-mediated; inflammatory cells (macrophages, neutrophils, and
lymphocytes) invade arteries (panarteritis) → disruption of internal elastic lamina of artery
• Clinical Diagnosis: CRASH and Burn
At least 4 of 5 criteria:
Conjunctivitis
Rash (palms and soles)
Adenopathy (cervical lymphadenopathy)
Strawberry-appearing tongue with oral mucosal dry cracked lips
Hand and feet edema
And Burn: Fever > 5 days
• High risk of coronary artery vasculitis, including aneurysms and thrombosis if untreated
--- SLIDE 60 ---
Kawasaki Disease
• Treatment:
Aspirin to prevent coronary artery aneurysms
(Avoid aspirin in children due to risk of Reye disease except in
KawASAki!)
Supportive care
IVIG +/- corticosteroids
Goal is IVIG within 10 days of symptoms: reduces side effects and
significantly reduces risk of coronary artery aneurysms
Obtain echo after dx

--- SLIDE 62 ---
Measles (Rubeola)
• Epidemiology: more common in unvaccinated patients
• Cause: acute infection caused by single-stranded RNA paramyxovirus
• Transmission: via respiratory droplets; highly contagious and peaks in
the winter to spring
• Pathogenesis: enters the respiratory tract and incubates for 10 days
with the prodromal phase lasting 3 days, followed by a rash
--- SLIDE 63 ---
Measles (Rubeola)
• Symptoms: fever and 4 “C’s”: cough, conjunctivitis, coryza, and “k”oplik spots
Three days after the onset of symptoms: maculopapular erythematous rash
appears that starts on the face and spreads to the trunk and extremities
Complications include encephalitis, blindness, and subacute sclerosing
panencephalitis (SSPE)- a late CNS degenerative disease occurring 5-15
years later
• Management/Treatment:
Contact public health officials
Supportive care is suggested for uncomplicated cases
Vitamin A: prevent corneal ulcerations and blindness, as well as reduce
morbidity and mortality from measles
Prevention with MMR Vaccine

Erythema Multiforme: Herpes Simplex Virus is #1; targetoid lesions
-
Stevens-Johnson Syndrome/Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis: drug induced;
mucous membrane involvement; must recognize right away
-
Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome: Mucous membranes spared**
-
Toxic Shock Syndrome: packing; Group A Streptococcus exotoxins (A, B,
C) or Staphylococcus aureus (TSST-1); hypotension and shock
-
Pemphigoid vulgaris: Autoimmune; IgG antibodies against desmoglein 3
-
Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS): drug
induced; HLA genes; eosinophilia; liver injury
-
Meningococcemia: Early antibiotic treatment w IV third-generation
cephalosporin and vancomycin

Summary
-
Necrotizing Fasciitis: breaking skin integrity; excruciating pain;
signs of sepsis; Subcutaneous emphysema and crepitus
-
Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus: an ophthalmic emergency; latent VZV trigeminal ganglion
reactivates, ophthalmic branch of CN V →vesicular rash dermatomal distribution, doesn’t
cross midline
-
Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (RMSF): Rickettsia rickettsia transmitted via American dog
tick (Dermacentor); endemic areas southeastern and south-central US; rash starts wrists
and ankles; tx with doxycycline
-
Burns: superficial, superficial and deep partial-thickness, full-thickness, deeper burn injury
-
Kawasaki Disease: immune mediated; “CRASH and burn”; High risk of coronary artery
vasculitis, including aneurysms and thrombosis if untreated; tx: IVIG and aspirin; monitor
for future cardiovascular complications
-
Measles: single-stranded RNA paramyxovirus transmitted via respiratory droplets; fever
and 4 “C’s”: cough, conjunctivitis, coryza, and “k”oplik spots; Tx Vitamin A
