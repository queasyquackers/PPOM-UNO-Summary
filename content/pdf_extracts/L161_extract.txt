Coagulation Disorders: the coagulation panel -
PT, PTT, INR (REVIEW)
Prothrombin time (PT): time to clot (seconds), mainly measure of
extrinsic pathway (factor VII)
International normalizing ratio (INR): used to normalize PT and
compare INR between different institutions (which have different labs)
Calculation based on results of PT, used to monitor patients
being treated with warfarin (anticoagulant)
Activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT or just PTT): time to
clot (seconds), measure of intrinsic pathway
Thrombin time (TT): time to clot, dependent on thrombin catalyzing
fibrin production - not really used
Bleeding time (BT): time it takes for a patient to stop bleeding, looks
for platelet dysfunction - not really used; all platelet disorders have
increased bleeding time

THROMBOCYTOPENIA
Thrombocytopenia < 150,000 /microliter (N = 150,000-450,00/ul)
• Platelets < 50,000 – Minor Trauma = Bleed; increased risk of
bleeding during surgery
• Platelets < 20,000 – Spontaneous Bleeding; intracranial bleeding
possible
• Normal PT/PTT
• Petechiae: common sign of quantitative problems, not
commonly seen in qualitative
• Present with mucous membrane bleeding and
microhemorrhages (petechiae, epistaxis (nose bleeding)), heavy

• Check platelet count (decreased), bleeding time (increased),

--- SLIDE 18 ---
Chronic Immune Thrombocytopenic Purpura
• Platelet Deficiency (Quantitative) – 1° Hemostasis
• Caused by autoantibody-mediated destruction of platelets, primary (idiopathic) or
secondary; diagnosis of exclusion (R/O secondary causes)
• Pathogenesis: Autoantibodies usually directed against platelet membrane
glycoproteins IIb-IIIa or Ib–IX present in plasma and bound to platelet surface in
about 80% of patients
• Antiplatelet antibodies act as opsonins recognized by IgG Fc receptors expressed on
phagocytes; leads to increased platelet destruction
• Thrombocytopenia usually greatly improved by splenectomy (spleen major site of
removal of opsonized platelets; possible removal of a source of autoantibodies
since splenic red pulp has many plasma cells)
Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2021,
Ch. 14
--- SLIDE 19 ---
CHRONIC ITP - Platelet Deficiency (Quantitative) – 1° Hemostasis
• Primary chronic ITP: idiopathic/immune condition
• Secondary: SLE, HIV, CLL
• Onset: insidious
• bleeding into skin and mucosal surfaces
• pinpoint hemorrhages (petechiae), esp. in dependent areas
(capillary pressure higher)
• petechiae may become confluent, resulting in purpura
• history of easy bruising, nosebleeds (epistaxis) and gingival
bleeding
• primary manifestation may be: melena, hematuria, or excessive
menstrual flow
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 14
--- SLIDE 20 ---
CHRONIC ITP
• Spleen usually normal size; congestion of sinusoids and
enlargement of splenic follicles
• Bone marrow: modestly increased number of
megakaryocytes, some immature, with large, nonlobulated,
single nuclei (not specific finding, reflects accelerated
thrombopoiesis because of platelet destruction)
• Skin and mucous membranes: petechial bleeds
• Peripheral blood: abnormally large platelets
(megathrombocytes) (sign of accelerated thrombopoiesis)
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 14
--- SLIDE 21 ---
Chronic ITP
• Labs: low platelet count, normal or increased megakaryocytes in
bone marrow, large platelets in peripheral blood (evidence of
accelerated platelet destruction); PT and PTT normal
• Tests for platelet autoantibodies have low sensitivity and specificity,
not clinically useful; diagnosis of exclusion
• Most patients respond to glucocorticoids (inhibit phagocyte
function), but may relapse after withdrawal
• Splenectomy for severe thrombocytopenia normalizes platelet
count in about two-thirds of patients, but increases risk of bacterial
sepsis
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 14
--- SLIDE 22 ---
Acute Immune Thrombocytopenic Purpura
• Also caused by autoantibodies to platelets, but clinical features and
course distinct
• Mainly disease of childhood , esp. 2 – 5 years old
• Most common cause of isolated thrombocytopenia in pediatric patients
• Symptoms appear abruptly, often 1 to 2 weeks after self-limited
viral illness (may trigger development of autoantibodies via
uncertain mechanisms)
• Self-limited (v. chronic ITP), usually resolves spontaneously within 6
months
• Glucocorticoids given only for severe thrombocytopenia
• In about 20% of children (usually those without viral prodrome)
thrombocytopenia persists; childhood form of chronic ITP, follow
course similar to adults
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 14

Acute
Immune Thrombotic
Thrombocytopenia (ITP) - Pathogenesis
• Pediatric patient (typically)
• Sudden onset widely spread petechiae/bruising over the entire

• Platelets usually < 20,000/uL
• Signs and symptoms of mucocutaneous bleeding (petechiae,

THROMBOTIC MICROANGIOPATHIES:
THROMBOTIC THROMBOCYTOPENIA PURPURA (TTP)
HEMOLYTIC UREMIC SYNDROME (HUS)
• TTP and HUS:
• Excessive activation of platelets which deposit as thrombi in small blood vessels
• Intravascular thrombi cause microangiopathic hemolytic anemia and widespread
organ dysfunction with platelet consumption leading to thrombocytopenia
• Activation of coagulation cascade not primary importance; PT and PTT usually normal
• TTP traditionally defined as pentad:
• Fever
• Thrombocytopenia
• Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia
• Transient neurologic deficits
• Renal failure
• HUS also associated with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia and
thrombocytopenia but usually no neurologic symptoms; prominence of acute renal
failure; frequent occurrence in children
• Distinctions blurred; many adult patients with “TTP” lack one or more of the five
criteria; some patients with “HUS” have fever and neurologic dysfunction
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 14
--- SLIDE 26 ---
TTP - Platelet Deficiency (Quantitative) – 1° Hemostasis
• Pathophysiology: Inhibition (acquired antibody) OR deficiency
(genetic/inherited) of enzyme ADAMTS13 : metalloprotease that
degrades vWF multimers
• If enzyme is either deficient or inhibited, large vWF multimers
accumulate in plasma, promoting spontaneous platelet activation,
adhesion, and aggregation resulting in MICROTHROMBI FORMATION
• vWF multimers in areas of high shear stress lead to small vessel
obstruction
• Labs: Low platelets/ Normal PT/PTT, Low Hb, Schistocytes “helmet
cells” on smear
• Plasma exchange removes autoantibodies, provides functional
ADAMTS13; TTP can be treated > 80% of patients (once fatal)
Robbins and Cotran,
Pathologic Basis of
Disease, 10th ed.,
2020, Ch. 14

--- SLIDE 28 ---
HUS - Platelet Deficiency (Quantitative) – 1° Hemostasis
Hemolytic uremic syndrome
• Normal levels of ADAMTS13:
• Typical HUS: strongly associated with infectious gastroenteritis caused by
Escherichia coli strain O157:H7 enterohemorrhagic (undercooked meat) - produces
a shiga-like toxin which causes endothelial injury, provoking platelet activation and
aggregation
• Children and older adults at highest risk, often present with bloody diarrhea;
few days later HUS develops
• Atypical HUS: associated with defects in alternative complement pathway inhibitor
deficiencies
• Complement factor H
• membrane cofactor protein (CD46), or factor I (proteins that prevent excessive activation of
alternative complement pathway)
• May be caused by inherited defects or acquired inhibitory autoantibodies;
associated with remitting, relapsing clinical course
Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch.
--- SLIDE 29 ---
HUS- Platelet Deficiency (Quantitative) - 1° Hemostasis
• Basis for platelet activation in typical and atypical HUS unclear;
therapeutic antibodies that inhibit activation of complement factor C5
effective in preventing thrombosis in atypical HUS
• Typical HUS treated supportively; patients who survive acute insult
usually recover, some have permanent renal damage, require dialysis
or renal transplantation
• Shiga-like toxin vs ADAMTS13 assay for diagnosis
• Labs: Low platelets, Normal PT/PTT, Low Hb, Schistocytes, renal
dysfunction (elevated BUN and Cr)
• Certain drugs and radiation may also result in thrombotic
microangiopathies resembling HUS because of damage to endothelial
cells
Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch.14

Heparin Induced Thrombocytopenia
• Thrombocytopenia in about 5% of persons receiving
heparin
• Type 1 typically clinically silent
• Type 2 HIT: less common, often life threatening
– Occurs 5 to 14 days after therapy begins (some
sources 2 – 5 days following heparin dose)
• IgG antibodies against COMPLEX of heparin
bound to platelet factor 4 (PF4) (membrane
glycoprotein)
• Binding of antibody to these complexes
activates platelets and promotes
thrombosis, even though thrombocytopenia
present (paradoxical)
• develop petechial rash, thrombocytopenia
• Immediately stop heparin - start an alternative
nonheparin anticoagulant

Bernard-Soulier: Platelet Dysfunction (Qualitative) - 1° Hemostasis
• Autosomal recessive defect in PLATELET ADHESION (to
subendothelial matrix/collagen) – no platelet plug
• Caused by inherited deficiency of platelet membrane
glycoprotein complex Ib (GpIb) (receptor for vWF
needed for normal platelet adhesion to subendothelial
extracellular matrix)
• DECREASED expression of GpIb leads to DECREASED
adhesion of PLATELET (GpIb) to vWf
• Defect in platelet plug formation due to impaired
ADHESION of platelets to subendothelial collagen (via
vWF)
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 14

Glanzmann thrombasthenia: Platelet Dysfunction (Qualitative) - 1° Hemostasis
Glanzmann’s Thrombasthenia – defective platelet aggregation
• Autosomal recessive defect in aggregation
• Decreased GpIIb/IIIa expression
• Decreased platelet-to-platelet aggregation and defective platelet plug
formation
• Platelets fail to aggregate in response to adenosine diphosphate (ADP),
collagen, serotonin, epinephrine, or thrombin because of deficiency or
dysfunction of glycoprotein IIb–IIIa (integrin involved in “bridge
formation” between platelets by binding fibrinogen)
• PLT count usually normal
• Increased bleeding time
• Bleeding often severe
• Agglutination with ristocetin*
• Why? If there is still GpIb!! vWF can bind!

Hemophilia A: Clotting Factor Dysfunction - 2° Hemostasis
• Deficiency of factor VIII, X-linked recessive
• Any intrinsic pathway coagulation defect will specifically elevate PTT
• Most common hereditary disease associated with life-threatening bleeding;
caused by mutations in factor VIII
• X-linked recessive trait: affects mainly males and homozygous females; about 30%
of patients have no family history (new mutations)
• Wide range of clinical severity, correlates with level of factor VIII activity:
• < 1% of normal levels: severe disease
• Symptomatic cases:
• Easy bruising and massive hemorrhage after trauma or operative
procedures
• “spontaneous” hemorrhages in body areas susceptible to trauma (esp-
joints – hemarthroses)
• Excessive bleeding after circumcision or umbilical cord separation
• Petechiae usually not present
Robbins and Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed.,2020, Ch.14

--- SLIDE 49 ---
Hemophilia A: Clotting Factor Dysfunction - 2° Hemostasis
• Main concern hemophilia patient: hemorrhage
• Prolonged PTT and a normal PT (abnormality of intrinsic coagulation
pathway)
• Hemarthroses: bleeding into the joints, most common is knee; easy
bruising, bleeding after trauma or surgery (dental procedures)
• Factor VIII–specific assays needed for diagnosis
• Traditionally treated with infusions of recombinant factor VIII
• About 15% of patients develop antibodies that bind and inhibit factor VIII
• Treatment: desmopressin, factor VIII concentrate, emicizumab
• Desmopressin increases vWF release which increases functional levels
of factor VIIIa
• Before development of recombinant factor VIII therapy, thousands of
hemophiliacs received plasma-derived factor VIII concentrates containing
HIV, many developed AIDS
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch. 14

Hemophilia B: Clotting Factor Dysfunction - 2° Hemostasis
• Aka Christmas Disease (named after first patient identified, not holiday):
• Deficiency of factor IX, X-linked recessive, clinical severity varies
• Prolonged PTT and a normal PT (abnormality of intrinsic coagulation
pathway)
• Diagnosis: assay of IX factor levels
• Hemarthroses: bleeding into the joints, most common is knee; easy
bruising, bleeding after trauma or surgery (dental procedures)
• Severe factor IX deficiency - clinically indistinguishable from factor VIII
deficiency (hemophilia A) (factors VIII and IX function together to activate
factor X)
• Many mutations involving gene that encodes factor
• In about 15%, factor IX protein present but not functional
• Treated with infusions of recombinant factor IX
• Before development of recombinant factor VIII therapy, thousands of
hemophiliacs received plasma-derived factor VIII concentrates containing
HIV, many developed AIDS

Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation
• Acute, subacute, or chronic thrombohemorrhagic disorder with
excessive activation of coagulation and formation of thrombi in
microvasculature
• Not a primary disease; acquired coagulopathy from different
conditions
• Secondary complication of many disorders: most commonly
associated with obstetric complications, malignant neoplasms,
sepsis, and major trauma
• Consumption of platelets, fibrin, and coagulation factors and,
secondarily, activation of fibrinolysis
• Can present with signs and symptoms relating to tissue hypoxia and
infarction caused by microthrombi; hemorrhage, due to depletion of
factors and activation of fibrinolytic mechanisms; or both

Disseminated intravascular coagulation
• Two major mechanisms trigger DIC:
• (1) release of tissue factor or other procoagulants into circulation, and
• (2) widespread injury of endothelial cells
CAUSATIVE AGENTS (examples):
• Procoagulants (e.g., tissue factor) from, e.g.: placenta in obstetric complications;
tissues injured by trauma or burns; mucus/mucin released from certain
adenocarcinomas (may directly activate factor X); APL (acute promyelocytic
leukemia)
• Endothelial injury can initiate DIC; injuries that cause endothelial cell necrosis
expose subendothelial matrix - leads to activation of platelets and coagulation
pathway
• TNF - one mediator of endothelial injury (implicated in DIC occurring with
sepsis); induces endothelial cells to express tissue factor and decreases expression
of thrombomodulin - tilts balance towards coagulation
• Widespread endothelial injury can also occur with deposition of antigen-antibody
complexes (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus), temperature extremes (e.g., heat
stroke, burns), or microorganisms (e.g., meningococci, rickettsiae)
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch.14

DIC
• Consequences of DIC:
• Widespread deposition of fibrin thrombi within microcirculation: leads to ischemia of
vulnerable organs and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (from fragmentation of rbcs
as they squeeze through narrowed microvasculature)
• Consumption of platelets and clotting factors and activation of plasminogen; leads to
bleeding
• Thrombi: in brain, heart, lungs, kidneys, adrenals, spleen, and liver (decreasing order of
frequency) and other organs
• Kidneys: may have small thrombi in glomeruli; severe cases, microinfarcts or bilateral renal
cortical necrosis
• Many fibrin thrombi may be in alveolar capillaries, sometimes associated with pulmonary
edema and fibrin exudation
• CNS: fibrin thrombi may cause microinfarcts
• In meningococcemia, fibrin thrombi within microcirculation of adrenal cortex, massive
adrenal hemorrhages (Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome)
Robbins and Cotran, Pathologic Basis of Disease, 10th ed., 2020, Ch.14

DIC
• DIC onset: acute/fulminant (e.g.,sepsis or amniotic fluid embolism) OR
insidious/chronic (e.g.,carcinomatosis, retention of dead fetus)
• Acute DIC, usually associated with obstetric complications or major trauma,
dominated by bleeding
• Chronic DIC (e.g., cancer patients), usually more thrombosis
• Manifestations: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia; dyspnea, cyanosis,
respiratory failure; convulsions and coma; oliguria and acute renal failure;
and sudden or progressive circulatory failure and shock
• Prognosis varies, depends on underlying disorder
• Only definitive treatment: remove or treat inciting cause

• Infections: Examples: meningococcemia, other forms of septicemia, infective endocarditis,
