## Introduction to Drug Addiction (Slide 2-3)

Drug addiction remains a critical public health crisis, with over 64,000 overdose deaths reported in 2015 alone. It is a leading cause of accidental death, often initiating in young adulthood (around age 20) with gateway substances like marijuana. Beyond the staggering human toll, the economic burden—including healthcare, lost productivity, and crime—is estimated at $740 billion annually.

To understand addiction, we must move beyond the stigma and view it through a physiological lens. Addiction is defined as a chronic, relapsing disease characterized by compulsive drug seeking and use despite harmful consequences. It is not merely a failure of willpower but a fundamental hijacking of the brain's reward circuitry. The process begins with recreational use, which, in susceptible individuals, progresses to compulsive use. The cycle of addiction involves three stages: **binge/intoxication** (driven by the reward pathway), **withdrawal/negative affect** (driven by the stress pathway), and **preoccupation/anticipation** (driven by executive dysfunction and craving).

## The Mesolimbic Reward Pathway (Slide 6-8)

 The core of addiction pathology lies in the **Mesolimbic Dopamine Pathway**. This system evolved to reinforce behaviors essential for survival, such as eating, drinking, and reproduction. When we engage in these activities, the brain releases dopamine, the "learning signal" that says, "This was good, do it again."

Anatomy is destiny here. The pathway originates in the **Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA)** of the midbrain. Dopaminergic neurons from the VTA project to the **Nucleus Accumbens (NAc)** in the limbic system. This VTA-NAc connection is the primary highway of reward. However, the system is more complex, involving:
-   **Amygdala:** Processes emotions and stress (conditioned learning).
-   **Hippocampus:** Encodes context and memory (e.g., remembering *where* drugs were used).
-   **Prefrontal Cortex:** Governing executive function, planning, and decision-making.

:::highyield
**The Mesolimbic Pathway**
-   **Origin:** Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) in the midbrain.
-   **Target:** Nucleus Accumbens (NAc) in the limbic system.
-   **Neurotransmitter:** Dopamine (DA).
-   **Function:** Reinforcement Learning (Reward Prediction Error).
:::

### Reward Prediction Error (Slide 10-12)
Wait, isn't dopamine just "pleasure"? Not exactly. Seminal experiments by Wolfram Schultz revealed that dopamine neurons signal **Reward Prediction Error**.
1.  **Unexpected Reward:** Dopamine fires **strongly** (Positive error). The brain learns: "Surprise reward! Learn this context."
2.  **Predicted Reward:** Dopamine firing shifts to the *cue* (stimulus), not the reward itself. The brain expects the reward.
3.  **Omitted Reward:** Dopamine firing **drops** below baseline (Negative error). The brain learns: "Prediction failed. Update behavior."

Drugs of abuse bypass this natural calibration. They cause a massive, pharmacological release of dopamine that **never habituates**. The brain receives a constant "Better than expected!" signal, cementing the drug-taking behavior as a survival priority outweighing food or safety.

## Mechanisms of Drug Classes (Slide 14)

Drugs of abuse can be categorized by their specific molecular targets in the reward pathway. While they all ultimately increase dopamine in the Nucleus Accumbens, they get there by different routes.

### Class 1: Psychostimulants (Direct Sympathomimetics)
These agents directly increase monoamine concentration in the synaptic cleft.

#### Amphetamines (Slide 16-17)
Amphetamines are molecular trojan horses. They resemble dopamine and enter the presynaptic neuron via the **Dopamine Transporter (DAT)**. Once inside, they invade synaptic vesicles via VMAT2, displacing endogenous dopamine into the cytoplasm.
-   **Mechanism:**
    1.  Enters via DAT.
    2.  Displaces DA from vesicles.
    3.  **Reverses DAT:** The high intracellular DA concentration causes DAT to pump DA *out* into the synapse (reverse transport).
    4.  **Result:** Massive, non-impulse-dependent DA release.
-   **Intoxication:** Euphoria, psychomotor agitation, insomnia, tachycardia, mydriasis (dilated pupils).
-   **Withdrawal:** "The Crash"—depression, lethargy, increased appetite, sleep disturbance.

#### Cocaine (Slide 18-20)
Cocaine is a blockade artist. unlike amphetamine, it does not enter the neuron to work.
-   **Mechanism:** Binds to and **blocks DAT** (and NET/SERT). This prevents reuptake, trapping dopamine in the synapse.
-   **Unique Feature:** Cocaine is a locally anesthetic (Na+ channel blocker) and a **Vasoconstrictor** (blocks NE reuptake).
-   **Clinical Consequence:** Cocaine chest pain is due to **coronary artery vasospasm**.
-   **Complication:** Ischemia, myocardial infarction, intracranial hemorrhage.
-   **Micro-aneurysms:** Chronic use can cause "crack lung" or cerebral micro-aneurysms.

:::correlate
**Cocaine-Induced Chest Pain**
-   **Pathophysiology:** Inhibition of biochemical reuptake of Norepinephrine (NE) $\rightarrow$ Excess NE stimulation of Alpha-1 receptors $\rightarrow$ Vasoconstriction.
-   **Presentation:** Chest pain, hypertension, agitation.
-   **Treatment:** Benzodiazepines (first-line), Nitroglycerin, Phentolamine.
-   **CONTRAINDICATION:** **Beta-Blockers** are contraindicated!
-   **Why?** Blocking Beta-2 (vasodilatory) receptors leaves Alpha-1 (vasoconstrictive) receptors unopposed $\rightarrow$ Worsening hypertension and ischemia ("Unopposed Alpha Stimulation").
:::

#### Nicotine (Slide 22)
-   **Mechanism:** Agonist at **Nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptors (nAChRs)** on VTA dopamine neurons.
-   **Effect:** Direct depolarization of VTA neurons $\rightarrow$ increased firing $\rightarrow$ DA release in NAc.
-   **Intoxication:** Restlessness, anxiety.
-   **Withdrawal:** Irritability, anxiety, craving, increased appetite.

### Class 2: Depressants (GABA-ergic Agents)
These drugs act like the brakes of the brain, primarily targeting the GABA-A receptor.

#### Alcohol (Ethanol) (Slide 24-27)
-   **Mechanism:** Enhances **GABA-A** receptor activity (inhibitory) and inhibits **NMDA** glutamate receptors (excitatory).
-   **Intoxication:** Disinhibition, ataxia, slurred speech, blackouts.
-   **Chronic Sequelae:** Cirrhosis, Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, peripheral neuropathy.
-   **Withdrawal:** Tremors, anxiety, seizures ("Rum fits"), and the life-threatening **Delirium Tremens**.

:::correlate
**Delirium Tremens (DTs)**
-   **Pathophysiology:** Chronic alcohol use downregulates GABA receptors and upregulates NMDA receptors (to maintain homeostasis). Abrupt cessation leads to massive overexcitation.
-   **Timing:** 2-4 days (48-96 hours) after last drink.
-   **Presentation:** Altered mental status, autonomic instability (fever, tachycardia, hypertension), hallucinations.
-   **Treatment:** **Benzodiazepines** (Chlordiazepoxide, Diazepam, Lorazepam).
-   **Lecturer's Point:** This is a medical emergency with high mortality if untreated.
:::

#### Benzodiazepines & Barbiturates (Slide 29-32)
Both bind allosterically to the GABA-A receptor (chloride channel), but at different sites.
-   **Benzodiazepines:** Increase the **Frequency** of channel opening. ("Frenzodiazepines")
-   **Barbiturates:** Increase the **Duration** of channel opening. ("Barbi-durates")
-   **Addiction Mechanism:** How do inhibitors cause dopamine release? via **Disinhibition**.
    -   VTA dopamine neurons are normally kept in check by local GABAergic interneurons.
    -   Benzos/Barbs bind to GABA-A receptors on these *interneurons*.
    -   Inhibiting the inhibitor (Interneuron) $\rightarrow$ Releases the brake on the DA neuron $\rightarrow$ DA firing increases.
-   **Receptor Nuance (Slide 31):** The GABA interneurons in the VTA have **Alpha-1** containing GABA-A receptors. VTA dopamine neurons have Alpha-3. Benzos preferentially target Alpha-1, explaining why they silence the interneuron but not the DA neuron itself.

### Class 3: Opioids (Slide 34-36)
-   **Agents:** Heroin, Morphine, Fentanyl, Oxycodone.
-   **Mechanism:** Agonists at the **Mu-Opioid Receptor (Gi-coupled)**.
-   **Pathway:** Similar to repressants, they work via **Disinhibition**. They bind Mu receptors on GABA interneurons in the VTA $\rightarrow$ hyperpolarization (decrease GABA release) $\rightarrow$ VTA DA neurons fire freely.
-   **Intoxication:** "Pinpoint pupils" (Miosis), respiratory depression, CNS depression, constipation, euphoria.

:::correlate
**Opioid Withdrawal**
-   **Pathophysiology:** Upregulation of cAMP/NE pathways to counteract chronic inhibition. Removal of drug leads to noradrenergic storm.
-   **Presentation:** Dilated pupils (mydriasis), piloerection ("cold turkey"), rhinorrhea, lacrimation, yawning, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea.
-   **Lecturer's Point:** While alcohol withdrawal can kill you (seizures), opioid withdrawal makes you *want* to die (misery) but is rarely fatal.
-   **Neonatal Abstinence Syndrome:** Newborns of dependent mothers presenting with high-pitched cry, tremors, and irritability.
:::

### Class 4: Hallucinogens & Others

#### Phencyclidine (PCP) (Slide 38)
-   **Mechanism:** Non-competitive antagonist at **NMDA (Glutamate) receptors**.
-   **Presentation:** Violent behavior, nystagmus (vertical/horizontal), impulsivity, analgesia, psychosis.
-   **Complication:** Trauma (due to analgesia/aggression), rhabdomyolysis.

#### MDMA (Ecstasy) (Slide 43)
-   **Mechanism:** Functions like amphetamine (reverses transporters) but with high affinity for **SERT** (Serotonin Transporter).
-   **Effect:** Massive release of **Serotonin (5-HT)** > Dopamine/NE.
-   **Intoxication:** Euphoria, emotional closeness (empathogen), hyperthermia, hyponatremia (due to excess water intake + SIADH-like effect).
-   **Complication:** **Serotonin Syndrome** (especially if combined with SSRIs).

#### Marijuana (THC) (Slide 41)
-   **Mechanism:** Partial agonist at **CB1 Cannabinoid receptors**.
-   **Action:** CB1 receptors are presynaptic and Gi-coupled. They inhibit GABA release (Disinhibition) in the VTA.
-   **Intoxication:** Euphoria, increased appetite ("munchies"), conjunctival injection (red eyes), dry mouth, slowed reaction time.

## Summary of Mechanisms Table

| Drug Class | Primary Target | Mechanism on Reward Pathway | Key Intoxication Sign |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Amphetamine** | DAT / VMAT2 | Reverses DAT; Displaces DA from vesicles | Agitation, Pupil Dilation |
| **Cocaine** | DAT Transporter | Blocks Reuptake of DA/NE/5-HT | Chest Pain, Pupil Dilation |
| **Opioids** | Mu-Receptor | Disinhibition (inhibits GABA interneuron) | **Pinpoint Pupils**, Resp Depression |
| **Benzos** | GABA-A (Alpha-1) | Disinhibition (inhibits GABA interneuron) | Sedation, Resp Depression |
| **Nicotine** | nAChR | Direct depolarization of VTA neuron | Alertness, Anxiety |
| **PCP** | NMDA Receptor | Antagonist (Blocks Glutamate) | **Nystagmus**, Violence |
| **MDMA** | SERT > DAT | Reverses SERT (Massive 5-HT release) | Hyperthermia, Hyponatremia |
| **Marijuana** | CB1 Receptor | Disinhibition (Presynaptic inhibition of GABA) | Red Eyes, Munchies |

**Pupil Changes**
-   **Miosis (Constriction):** Opioids, Cholinergics. ("Pinpoint requires opioids")
