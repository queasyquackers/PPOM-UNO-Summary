# Lecture #48: Pharmacology: Sedative Hypnotics



## 1. Mechanisms of CNS Depression
**Story Mode:**
To induce sedation or sleep, we typically need to "calm down" the brain's excitatory pathways. The most effective way to do this is by enhancing the activity of **GABA** (Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid), the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the CNS. When GABA binds to its receptor, it opens a chloride channel, allowing negatively charged chloride ions ($Cl^-$) to flow into the neuron. This causes **hyperpolarization**, making the neuron less likely to fire. The major classes of sedative-hypnotics (Benzodiazepines, Barbiturates, Alcohol) all target this GABA-A receptor complex but interact with it in distinct ways that determine their safety profile.

### 1.1 GABA-A Receptor Interactions
*   **GABA Dependent vs. Independent:**
    *   **Benzodiazepines & Z-Drugs:**
        *   Bind to a specific site (between $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ subunits).
        *   **Require GABA** to be present to open the channel.
        *   **Effect:** Increase the **frequency** of channel opening.
        *   **Safety:** Exhibit a "Ceiling Effect" — once receptors are saturated, they can't open more channels without GABA. Less risk of fatal respiratory depression alone.
    *   **Barbiturates & Ethanol:**
        *   Bind to a distinct site (Barbiturates: between $\alpha$ and $\beta$).
        *   **Do NOT require GABA** at high doses (can open the channel independently).
        *   **Effect:** Increase the **duration** of channel opening.
        *   **Safety:** **No Ceiling Effect**. Can cause profound CNS depression, coma, and fatal respiratory depression as dose increases.

### 1.2 Dose-Response Relationship
*   **Linear Slope (Drug A - Barbiturates/Alcohol):**
    *   Steep linear increase in CNS depression with dose.
    *   Sedation $\rightarrow$ Hypnosis $\rightarrow$ Anesthesia $\rightarrow$ Coma $\rightarrow$ Death.
*   **Plateau Effect (Drug B - Benzodiazepines):**
    *   Curve flattens out.
    *   Significant sedation but reduced risk of lethal respiratory depression (unless combined with other depressants).

:::correlate
**Drug-Drug Interactions & Respiratory Depression**
**The Lethal Combo:**
While Benzodiazepines rarely cause fatal respiratory depression on their own due to the ceiling effect, **combining** them with other CNS depressants removes this safety net.
*   **Mechanism:** Synergistic effects on GABA/respiratory centers.
*   **Combinations to Avoid:**
    *   Benzodiazepines + **Alcohol**
    *   Benzodiazepines + **Opioids** (Fentanyl, Heroin, Oxycodone)
    *   Benzodiazepines + **Barbiturates**
*   **Clinical Consequence:** A patient taking Xanax (Alprazolam) who drinks alcohol at a party runs a significantly higher risk of stopping breathing.
:::

## 2. Barbiturates
**Story Mode:**
Once the mainstay of sedation, Barbiturates ("-barbital") have largely been replaced by Benzodiazepines due to their narrow therapeutic index and high abuse potential. However, they retain niche uses in neurology (epilepsy) and anesthesia. Their ability to induce liver enzymes also makes them practically troublesome for drug interactions.

### 2.1 Key Agents & Uses
*   **Phenobarbital:**
    *   **Use:** Seizure disorders (Epilepsy). First-line for **neonatal seizures**.
*   **Thiopental:**
    *   **Use:** Induction of anesthesia (rapid onset).
    *   **Mechanism:** Highly lipid-soluble $\rightarrow$ crosses Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) instantly.
*   **Primidone:** Metabolized to Phenobarbital.

### 2.2 Pharmacokinetics
*   **Metabolism:**
    *   Hepatic metabolism.
    *   **CYP Inducers (especially CYP3A4):** Increases metabolism of other drugs (e.g., Oral Contraceptives $\rightarrow$ unwanted pregnancy; Warfarin $\rightarrow$ clotting risk).
*   **Redistribution (Thiopental):**
    *   Rapid onset due to high blood flow to the brain.
    *   Short duration not because of metabolism, but because the drug **redistributes** to less vascular tissues (muscle, fat).

### 2.3 Adverse Effects
*   **Respiratory Depression:** Fatal in overdose (supported ventilation required; alkalize urine to trap and excrete).
*   **Sedation/Hangover:** Especially in elderly.
*   **Tolerance & Dependence:**
    *   **Metabolic Tolerance:** Enzyme induction leads to faster breakdown.
    *   **Pharmacodynamic Tolerance:** Receptor downregulation.
*   **Contraindication:** **Porphyria** (induces ALA synthase $\rightarrow$ heme synthesis $\rightarrow$ toxic porphyrin accumulation).

:::correlate
**Porphyria Exacerbation**
**The Mechanism:**
Barbiturates induce Cytochrome P450 enzymes. The synthesis of these enzymes requires **heme**. The body responds by ramping up heme production via ALA synthase.
*   **In Porphyria Patients:** They have a genetic block in the heme pathway. Ramping up synthesis upstream leads to a toxic buildup of porphyrin intermediates.
*   **Symptoms:** Abdominal pain, psychiatric symptoms ("madness"), red/brown urine.
*   **Rule:** Never give Barbiturates to a patient with acute intermittent porphyria.
:::

## 3. Benzodiazepines
**Story Mode:**
Benzodiazepines ("-zolam", "-zepam") are the modern workhorses for anxiety, acute seizure control, and alcohol withdrawal. By requiring GABA to work, they offer a safer profile. Choice of agent often depends on pharmacokinetics (half-life) and metabolic pathway, especially in patients with "fragile" livers like the elderly.

### 3.1 Agents & Classification
*   **Long-Acting:**
    *   **Diazepam (Valium), Chlordiazepoxide:**
    *   **Use:** Alcohol withdrawal (smoother taper), seizures, muscle spasm.
    *   **Metabolism:** Converted to active metabolites (e.g., desmethyldiazepam) with very long half-lives (40-100 hours).
*   **Intermediate/Short-Acting:**
    *   **Lorazepam (Ativan):** Status epilepticus, anxiety.
    *   **Alprazolam (Xanax):** Panic disorder (rapid onset), high abuse potential.
    *   **Midazolam (Versed):** Procedural sedation/anesthesia (colonoscopy). Causes **Anterograde Amnesia**.

### 3.2 Metabolism & The Elderly
*   **The Problem:** Elderly patients have reduced Phase I (oxidative) metabolism and renal function. Long-acting benzos (Diazepam) accumulate $\rightarrow$ falls, confusion (pseudodementia).
*   **The Solution ("OTL"):** Use agents that undergo only **Phase II (Conjugation/Gluronidation)**.
    *   **O**xazepam
    *   **T**emazepam
    *   **L**orazepam

### 3.3 Antidote: Flumazenil
*   **Mechanism:** Competitive antagonist at the Benzodiazepine binding site.
*   **Use:** Reversal of Benzodiazepine overdose (e.g., post-surgery sedation).
*   **Warning:** Can precipitate **acute withdrawal seizures** in chronic benzodiazepine users. Administer with extreme caution.

:::correlate
**"Floppy Baby" Syndrome**
**Benzos in Pregnancy:**
Benzodiazepines cross the placenta.
*   **Effect:** Neonatal CNS depression.
*   **Presentation:** Hypotonia ("rag doll" appearance), lethargy, difficulty sucking/feeding, respiratory depression.
*   **Contraindication:** Avoid benzos in pregnancy and breastfeeding if possible.
:::

## 4. Z-Drugs & Insomnia
**Story Mode:**
For patients who just need sleep without the anxiolytic, muscle relaxant, or anticonvulsant effects of benzos, we use "Z-Drugs". They are structurally different but bind to a specific subtype of the benzodiazepine receptor (BZ1/omega-1) that mediates sedation.

### 4.1 Agents
*   **Zolpidem (Ambien)**
*   **Zaleplon (Sonata)**
*   **Eszopiclone (Lunesta)**

### 4.2 Key Features
*   **Mechanism:** Agonist at BZ1 receptor subtype.
*   **Effect:** Induces sleep with **minimal effect on sleep architecture** (REM/Slow Wave Sleep preserved better than benzos).
*   **Pharmacokinetics:** Short half-life $\rightarrow$ less daytime "hangover".
*   **Adverse Effects:** Sleep-walking/driving/eating behaviors.
*   **Reversal:** Can be reversed by **Flumazenil**.

### 4.3 Melatonin Agonists
*   **Ramelteon, Tasimelteon:**
    *   **Mechanism:** Agonists at MT1/MT2 receptors in Suprachiasmatic Nucleus.
    *   **Use:** Sleep-onset insomnia, Non-24 (blind patients).
    *   **Benefit:** No abuse potential (non-controlled), no withdrawal.

## 5. Ethanol (Alcohol)
**Story Mode:**
Ethanol is a "dirty drug" that hits multiple targets. It potentiates GABA (sedation) and inhibits NMDA/Glutamate (loss of memory/excitation). Chronic use leads to a compensatory upregulation of NMDA receptors which explains the dangerous hyperexcitability seen in withdrawal.

### 5.1 Metabolism
1.  **Ethanol** $\xrightarrow{Alcohol\ Dehydrogenase}$ **Acetaldehyde** (Toxic: flushing, nausea)
2.  **Acetaldehyde** $\xrightarrow{Aldehyde\ Dehydrogenase}$ **Acetate** (Excreted)
*   **Zero-Order Kinetics:** Enzymatic pathways saturate easily. Rate of elimination is constant regardless of concentration.

### 5.2 Alcohol Withdrawal
**Mechanism:** Chronic suppression of NMDA + upregulation of receptors. Abrupt stop $\rightarrow$ unopposed glutamate excitation.
*   **Symptoms:**
    *   6-12h: Tremors, anxiety, insomnia.
    *   12-48h: Seizures, hallucinosis.
    *   48-96h: **Delirium Tremens (DTs)** - Autonomic instability (BP/HR spikes), delirium, fatal if untreated.
*   **Treatment:**
    *   **Benzodiazepines (Long-acting):** Chlordiazepoxide/Diazepam to taper (Lorazepam if liver failure).

### 5.3 Alcohol Use Disorder Treatments
*   **Disulfiram:** Inhibits Aldehyde Dehydrogenase $\rightarrow$ Acetaldehyde buildup upon drinking $\rightarrow$ sickness (negative reinforcement).
*   **Naltrexone:** Opioid antagonist $\rightarrow$ reduces "reward"/craving properties of alcohol (dopamine link).
*   **Acamprosate:** NMDA antagonist/GABA modulator $\rightarrow$ reduces craving/withdrawal dysphoria.

:::correlate
**Fetal Alcohol Syndrome (FAS)**
**Mechanism:** Maternal alcohol assumption creates teratogenic accumulation of acetaldehyde and ROS.
*   **Presentation:**
    *   **Growth Retardation** (IUGR).
    *   **Facial Dysmorphia:** Smooth philtrum, thin upper lip, small eye openings (microphthalmia).
    *   **CNS:** Microcephaly, intellectual disability, behavioral issues.
*   **Safe Limit:** There is **no known safe amount** of alcohol in pregnancy.
:::

## 6. Narcolepsy & Stimulants
**Story Mode:**
If the problem is *too much* sleep (Narcolepsy) or inability to focus (ADHD), we need to ramp up Norepinephrine and Dopamine. Note that one unique treatment, Sodium Oxybate, is actually a profound depressant used to force deep sleep at night to improve daytime wakefulness.

### 6.1 Stimulants
*   **Amphetamines (Adderall) / Methylphenidate (Ritalin):**
    *   **Mechanism:** Block reuptake **AND** increase release of NE/DA.
    *   **Use:** ADHD, Narcolepsy.
    *   **SE:** Tachycardia, hypertension, anxiety, appetite suppression (weight loss), growth stunting in kids.
*   **Modafinil:**
    *   **Mechanism:** Unclear, likely increases DA release; effects on Orexin/Histamine.
    *   **Use:** Narcolepsy, Shift-Work Sleep Disorder.
    *   **Benefit:** "Wakefulness promoting" with less sympathomimetic (jittery) side effects than amphetamines.

### 6.2 Sodium Oxybate (GHB)
*   **Mechanism:** GABA-B agonist.
*   **Use:** Narcolepsy with **Cataplexy** (sudden loss of muscle tone).
*   **Effect:** Consolidates deep sleep at night $\rightarrow$ improved daytime alertness.
*   **Safety:** "Date **** Drug" potential. highly restricted (REMS program). CNS depression risk.
