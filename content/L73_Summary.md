# Lecture #73: Primary Brain and Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumors

## Meningiomas (Slide 1-3)
Primary brain tumors rarely spread to distant organs, unlike metastatic lesions. Within the central nervous system, meningiomas are considered the most common primary benign brain tumor (representing 20-30% of primary brain tumors). They originate not from brain parenchyma, but from the arachnoid cells of the meninges. Because they are slow-growing and external to the brain tissue itself ("extra-axial"), the brain has time to adapt to the pressure, often allowing these tumors to become quite large before causing symptomatic compression. Their prognosis is generally excellent if complete surgical resection is achievable. 

*   **Epidemiology & Location**:
    *   Most common primary brain tumor (predominantly benign).
    *   Peak incidence in adults, with a 2:1 female-to-male predominance.
    *   Common locations: Parasagittal, falcine, and cerebral convexities.
*   **Pathology & Associations**:
    *   **Hormonal Influence**: Contain progesterone receptors and may rapidly increase in size during pregnancy.
    *   **Genetics**: High association with neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2). Up to 80% have *NF2* gene mutations.
    *   **Imaging**: Characteristically present with a "dural tail" on MRI, indicating their dural-based, extra-axial origin.
*   **Histology**:
    *   Spindle cells arranged in a classic whorled pattern.
    *   Presence of lamellated calcifications known as **psammoma bodies**.
*   **Clinical Picture**:
    *   Symptoms depend on location but can include headaches, seizures, vision problems, and focal neurological deficits.

:::correlate
Meningioma

- **Pathophysiology:** Benign proliferation of arachnoid cells causing extra-axial compression of brain tissue.
- **Presentation:** Often asymptomatic until large. Presents with headaches, seizures, and focal deficits.
- **Diagnosis:** MRI showing a dural-based mass with a "dural tail". Histology shows psammoma bodies and whorled spindle cells.
- **Treatment:** Surgical resection. Radiation therapy is second-line if unresectable.
- **Risk Factors:** Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) and prior radiation.
- **Lecturer's Point:** These tumors have progesterone receptors and may dramatically enlarge during pregnancy.
:::

## Medulloblastomas (Slide 4-5)
Medulloblastoma is the most common malignant pediatric brain tumor. It arises from undifferentiated embryonic tissue (embryonal tumor) that remains in the brain after birth and undergoes malignant transformation. Because it classically originates in the cerebellum and often extends into the fourth ventricle, it aggressively disrupts motor coordination and obstructs cerebrospinal fluid flow.

*   **Epidemiology**:
    *   Most common malignant brain tumor in children.
    *   70% occur in children under 10 years old.
*   **Clinical Presentation**:
    *   Typically located in the cerebellum.
    *   Presents with headache, cerebellar ataxia, cranial nerve VI palsies.
    *   Can rapidly cause hydrocephalus by invading the fourth ventricle.
*   **Histology**:
    *   Small, round blue cells of undifferentiated embryonal lineage.
    *   **Homer-Wright Rosettes**: Tumor cells surrounding a central neuropil (a tangle of nerve fibers, synapses, and glial cells).
*   **Prognosis & Treatment**:
    *   High-grade malignancy demanding aggressive treatment: resection, radiation, and chemotherapy.
    *   Highly prone to metastasis ("drop metastases"), necessitating complete whole-neuraxis imaging.
    *   Children with average risk have a 70-80% 5-year survival rate, though those under 3 years old have poorer outcomes.

:::correlate
Medulloblastoma

- **Pathophysiology:** Highly malignant embryonal neoplasm of the cerebellum.
- **Presentation:** Headache, poor coordination/ataxia, CN VI palsy, and signs of increased intracranial pressure (hydrocephalus).
- **Diagnosis:** MRI of the full neuraxis to check for CSF seeding. Histology shows Homer-Wright rosettes and small round cells.
- **Treatment:** Surgical resection, whole-brain/spinal radiation, and chemotherapy.
- **Complications:** Drop metastases to the spinal cord.
- **Lecturer's Point:** Because these tumors seed the CSF aggressively, complete whole-neuraxis imaging is strictly required.
:::

## Neuronal Tumors and Gangliogliomas (Slide 6-7)
Neuronal tumors are rare, comprising only 1% of brain neoplasms, and consist of either pure neuronal cells or a mix of both abnormal glial and neuronal elements. The most notable of these is the ganglioglioma. Because they typically arise directly within the brain parenchyma and disrupt normal neuronal networks, patients classically present with unprovoked, new-onset seizures.

*   **Ganglioglioma**:
    *   A mixed tumor comprising neoplastic ganglion cells (neurons) and glial supporting cells.
    *   Typically slow-growing and low-grade (WHO Grade I).
    *   Most common location is the temporal lobe.
*   **Histology**:
    *   Dysmorphic, often binucleated neurons with peripheral Nissl bodies (rough endoplasmic reticulum).
    *   The glial component may show Rosenthal fibers (if astrocytic) and frequent calcifications.
*   **Prognosis**:
    *   Generally benign with an excellent prognosis (10-year survival of 84-93%).
    *   Often cured via surgical resection alone.

:::correlate
Ganglioglioma

- **Pathophysiology:** Benign mixed tumor of dysplastic neurons and neoplastic glial cells.
- **Presentation:** Typically presents as new-onset seizures in a child or young adult.
- **Diagnosis:** MRI showing a temporal lobe mass. Histology reveals binucleated neurons with Nissl bodies and occasionally Rosenthal fibers.
- **Treatment:** Surgical resection is usually curative.
- **Complications:** Up to 10% may undergo malignant transformation.
:::

## Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumors: Schwannoma (Slide 8-11)
Schwann cells provide crucial myelination for the peripheral nervous system. When these cells undergo neoplastic transformation, they form Schwannomas—the most common nerve sheath tumors in the human body. Because these tumors are encapsulated and grow outside the nerve fascicles, they compress rather than invade the nerve, allowing for relatively straightforward surgical removal without destroying the underlying nerve.

*   **Pathophysiology & Genetics**:
    *   Caused by the loss of function of the tumor suppressor protein **merlin** (encoded by *NF2* on Chromosome 22). 
    *   Merlin naturally dictates "contact inhibition of proliferation" via E-cadherin; its absence allows unchecked growth.
*   **Histology**:
    *   Demonstrate dense areas (**Antoni A**) and loose areas (**Antoni B**).
    *   Diagnostic feature within Antoni A: **Verocay bodies** (nuclear palisading with alternating nuclear and anuclear zones).
*   **Vestibular Schwannoma (Acoustic Neuroma)**:
    *   Affects the vestibular branch of CN VIII.
    *   Arises near the brainstem at the cerebellopontine angle.
    *   Presents with unilateral hearing loss, tinnitus, and vestibular dizziness.
    *   As it grows, it frequently compresses CN V (trigeminal), causing facial numbness, and occasionally CN VII or the cerebellum (ipsilateral ataxia).

:::correlate
Vestibular Schwannoma

- **Pathophysiology:** Benign, encapsulated proliferation of Schwann cells on CN VIII at the cerebellopontine angle.
- **Presentation:** Unilateral sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo. Expanding tumors compress CN V causing facial numbness.
- **Diagnosis:** MRI Brain with contrast. Histology shows Antoni A/B regions and Verocay bodies.
- **Treatment:** Observation, surgical excision, or stereotactic radiosurgery.
- **Risk Factors:** Bilateral vestibular schwannomas are intimately linked with, and pathognomonic for, Neurofibromatosis type 2.
- **Lecturer's Point:** Despite the classic "cerebellopontine angle syndrome" name implying CN VII involvement, CN V is more readily compressed than CN VII because CN VII is physically more resilient.
:::

## Neurofibromas and MPNSTs (Slide 12-16)
Unlike Schwannomas, Neurofibromas are unencapsulated masses comprising a chaotic mixture of Schwann cells, mast cells, and fibroblasts. While the vast majority are benign, they infiltrate extensively into the nerve architecture itself, making surgical enucleation impossible without sacrificing the parent nerve.

*   **Pathophysiology & Genetics**:
    *   Driven by loss of function of **neurofibromin** (*NF1* gene on Chromosome 17).
    *   Neurofibromin normally inhibits the Ras oncoprotein; its loss causes Ras hyperactivity and cellular proliferation.
*   **Histology**:
    *   Unencapsulated spindle cells resembling "shredded carrots." 
*   **Subtypes**:
    *   **Localized cutaneous**: Circumscribed nodular tumors on the skin.
    *   **Plexiform**: Diffuse, disfiguring tumors tracking along nerve plexuses. Pathognomonic for NF1 and carry a high risk of malignant transformation.
    *   **Diffuse**: Extensive infiltration into the dermis/subcutis.
*   **Malignant Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumors (MPNSTs)**:
    *   Aggressive, mitotically active spindle cell sarcomas. 
    *   Often arise from malignant transition of plexiform neurofibromas in NF1 patients.
    *   Present as rapidly enlarging, firm, necrotic masses in the trunk or extremities.

:::highyield
**Schwannoma vs. Neurofibroma**
A key distinction for board questions and surgical planning is encapsulation. Schwannomas are encapsulated and displace the nerve bundle, allowing for clean surgical excision. Neurofibromas are unencapsulated, infiltrate the native nerve fascicles ("shredded carrots"), and cannot be easily resected without sacrificing the nerve entirely.
:::

## Neurofibromatosis Types 1 and 2 (Slide 17-21)
Neurofibromatosis encompasses two distinct autosomal dominant tumor-suppressor syndromes that profoundly increase the risk of developing nervous system neoplasms.

| Condition | Gene/Protein | Chromosome | Classic Tumors | Key Clinical Findings | Prognosis |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Neurofibromatosis Type 1 (NF1)** | Neurofibromin | 17 | Neurofibromas, Optic Gliomas, MPNSTs, Pheochromocytomas | Café-au-lait spots, axillary/inguinal freckling, Lisch nodules (iris hamartomas) | 15-year shortened lifespan. Risk of malignant transformation (MPNSTs). |
| **Neurofibromatosis Type 2 (NF2)** | Merlin | 22 | Vestibular Schwannomas, Meningiomas, Ependymomas | Bilateral vestibular schwannomas, cataracts | High morbidity; early death often in 30s due to massive intracranial tumor burden. |

:::correlate
Neurofibromatosis Type 1 (von Recklinghausen disease)

- **Pathophysiology:** Autosomal dominant mutation of *NF1* tumor suppressor gene (neurofibromin) driving unregulated Ras signaling.
- **Presentation:** Multiple café-au-lait macules, axillary/inguinal freckling, vision issues (optic gliomas in children <6), and disfiguring plexiform neurofibromas.
- **Diagnosis:** Clinical criteria + genetic testing. Presence of Lisch nodules is highly specific.
- **Treatment:** Regular symptom surveillance. MPNSTs require aggressive multidisciplinary treatment.
- **Complications:** Malignant transformation of neurofibromas; associated with pheochromocytomas and seizures.
:::

:::correlate
Neurofibromatosis Type 2

- **Pathophysiology:** Autosomal dominant *NF2* mutation (merlin) on chromosome 22 disrupting contact inhibition (E-cadherin).
- **Presentation:** Hearing loss/tinnitus in a young adult (bilateral symptoms pathognomonic). 
- **Diagnosis:** MRI reveals bilateral vestibular schwannomas. 
- **Treatment:** Surgical excision, radiation, and monoclonal antibodies (bevacizumab).
- **Lecturer's Point:** Avoid excessive radiation when possible for NF2-associated tumors, as it can trigger secondary tumor formation.
:::
                                        
:::mnemonic
**NF1 vs NF2 Chromosomes**
**NF1** is on chromosome **17** ("von Recklinghausen" has 17 letters).
**NF2** is on chromosome **22** ("type 2" -> 22). 
:::
