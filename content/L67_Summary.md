## Introduction & Mechanism (Slide 2-3)

Opioids are the cornerstone of management for moderate to severe pain. Derived initially from the opium poppy (*Papaver somniferum*), these agents work by mimicking endogenous peptides (endorphins, enkephalins, dynorphins) at specific receptors in the central and peripheral nervous systems. To understand their pharmacology, think of the "Mu" ($\mu$) receptor as the primary switch for analgesia—but also for the major side effects like respiratory depression and dependence.

When an opioid binds to a G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) on a neuron, two things happen to stop the pain signal:
1.  **Presynaptically:** It closes voltage-gated $Ca^{2+}$ channels, preventing the release of excitatory neurotransmitters (glutamate, substance P).
2.  **Postsynaptically:** It opens $K^+$ channels, causing potassium efflux and hyperpolarization, making the neuron harder to excite.

This dual mechanism effectively "silences" the pain pathway. However, because these receptors are ubiquitous, the "silence" extends to the gut (constipation), the brainstem respiratory centers (apnea), and the inhibitory pathways in the midbrain (euphoria/reward).

:::highyield
**Mechanism of Action**
- **Receptors:** Mu ($\mu$), Delta ($\delta$), Kappa ($\kappa$). All are **Gi/Go-coupled GPCRs**.
- **Presynaptic:** Blocks $Ca^{2+}$ channels $\rightarrow$ $\downarrow$ Neurotransmitter release.
- **Postsynaptic:** Opens $K^+$ channels $\rightarrow$ Hyperpolarization $\rightarrow$ $\downarrow$ Neuronal firing.
- **Result:** Inhibits pain transmission in the Dorsal Horn and stimulates descending inhibitory pathways.
:::

### Opioid Receptors (Slide 5-6)

| Receptor | Key Effects | Endogenous Ligand |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Mu ($\mu$)** | **Analgesia** (supraspinal/spinal), **Respiratory Depression**, Euphoria, Sedation, **Constipation**, Miosis, Dependence | Endorphins |
| **Kappa ($\kappa$)** | Analgesia (spinal), Sedation, **Dysphoria**, Hallucinations (Psychotomimetic) | Dynorphins |
| **Delta ($\delta$)** | Analgesia, antidepressants effects | Enkephalins |

## Classification of Opioids (Slide 7-12)

Opioids are classified by their origin (Natural vs. Synthetic) and their intrinsic activity at the receptor (Full Agonist vs. Partial Agonist vs. Antagonist).

### 1. Strong Agonists (Full Mu Agonists)
These drugs have high affinity and high intrinsic activity at the $\mu$ receptor. They are used for severe pain.

-   **Morphine:** The prototype. Used for severe pain (MI, cancer, trauma). Metabolized to **Morphine-6-Glucuronide (M6G)** (active, potent) and **Morphine-3-Glucuronide (M3G)** (inactive/neuroexcitatory).
-   **Methadone:** Full agonist with **Unique Properties**:
    -   **Long half-life** (variable, 15-40+ hours) allowing for once-daily dosing.
    -   **NMDA antagonist** activity (helps with neuropathic pain/tolerance).
    -   **Use:** Opioid detoxification (prevents withdrawal) and maintenance; chronic pain.
    -   **Toxicity:** Can cause **QT prolongation** (Torsades de Pointes).
-   **Fentanyl:** Highly lipophilic, **100x more potent** than morphine. Rapid onset, short duration (redistribution). Used in anesthesia, chronic pain (patches), and breakthrough pain (lollipop/lozenge). **No histamine release** (cardiac stable).
-   **Meperidine:** Antimuscarinic effects (tachycardia, dry mouth). Metabolite **Normeperidine** accumulates in renal failure $\rightarrow$ **Seizures**. **Contraindicated** with MAOIs (Serotonin Syndrome). *Lecturer Note: Rarely used now due to toxicity.*
-   **Hydromorphone / Oxymorphone:** Potent semi-synthetics.

### 2. Moderate/Weak Agonists
Used for mild-to-moderate pain or cough. often combined with NSAIDs/Acetaminophen.

-   **Codeine:** Prodrug. Metabolized to Morphine by **CYP2D6**.
    -   *Pharmacogenetics:* Ultra-rapid metabolizers (high CYP2D6) can have fatal overdose (respiratory depression). Poor metabolizers get no analgesia.
    -   **Use:** Antitussive (cough suppression) and mild pain.
-   **Oxycodone / Hydrocodone:** Oral agents. Oxycodone is metabolized by CYP2D6/3A4. often formulated with acetaminophen (Percocet/Vicodin).
-   **Tramadol:** **Dual Mechanism:** Weak $\mu$ agonist + Inhibits Serotonin/NE reuptake.
    -   **Risk:** **Seizures** (lowers threshold) and **Serotonin Syndrome**.

### 3. Mixed Agonist-Antagonists
Designed to provide analgesia with a "ceiling effect" on respiratory depression and lower abuse potential. However, they can **precipitate withdrawal** in patients dependent on full agonists.

-   **Buprenorphine:** Partial $\mu$ agonist, $\kappa$ antagonist.
    -   **High Affinity, Slow Dissociation:** Hard to reverse with Naloxone.
    -   **Use:** Opioid Use Disorder (Suboxone = Buprenorphine + Naloxone) and pain.
    -   **Ceiling Effect:** Safer therapeutic index for respiratory depression.
-   **Nalbuphine / Pentazocine:** $\kappa$ agonist, $\mu$ antagonist.
    -   **Pentazocine:** Causes **Dysphoria/Psychosis** via $\kappa$ activation.

:::correlate
**Precipitated Withdrawal**
Administering a **partial agonist** (Buprenorphine) or mixed agonist-antagonist (Pentazocine) to a patient physically dependent on a full agonist (Morphine/Heroin) will displace the full agonist from the receptor.
-   **Result:** Immediate, severe withdrawal symptoms (pain, agitation, diarrhea).
:::

### 4. Antagonists
-   **Naloxone:** Short-acting IV/Intranasal. Used for **Acute Overdose**.
    -   *Clinical Pearl:* Duration (1-2 hrs) is shorter than most opioids; **monitor for re-narcotization**.
-   **Naltrexone:** Long-acting Oral/IM. Used for **Maintenance** (relapse prevention) in Opioid and Alcohol Use Disorder.

## Pharmacokinetics & Adverse Effects (Slide 15-20)

### Metabolism
-   **Hepatic glucuronidation** is the main pathway.
-   **Renal Excretion:** Metabolites (M6G, M3G, Normeperidine) accumulate in kidney failure.
    -   **Caution:** Avoid Morphine and Meperidine in renal failure. Use **Fentanyl** (clean metabolism) or Hydromorphone.

### Acute Adverse Effects ("MORPHINES")
-   **M**iosis (Pinpoint pupils) - *Tolerance does NOT develop.*
-   **O**rthostatic Hypotension (Histamine release).
-   **R**espiratory Depression (Decreased response to $CO_2$) - *Cause of death in overdose.*
-   **P**hysical Dependence/Pain control.
-   **H**istamine Release (Itching/Pruritus, hypotension) - *Morphine/Codeine/Meperidine.*
-   **I**ncreased ICP (Head injury contraindication - secondary to increased $pCO_2$ causing vasodilation).
-   **N**ausea/Vomiting (Stimulates CTZ).
-   **E**uphoria.
-   **S**edation.

### Chronic Adverse Effects
-   **Constipation:** Receptors in GI tract decrease motility. *Tolerance does NOT develop.* Regimen needed (Senna/Stool Softener).
-   **Tolerance:** Needing higher doses for same effect. Develops to: Analgesia, Euphoria, Sedation, Respiratory Depression.
    -   **NO Tolerance:** Miosis, Constipation.

### Opioid Toxidrome (The "Triad")
1.  **Coma** (CNS Depression)
2.  **Miosis** (Pinpoint Pupils)
3.  **Respiratory Depression** (Cyanosis, Apnea)

:::correlate
**Serotonin Syndrome**
Combining **Meperidine**, **Tramadol**, or **Dextromethorphan** with MAOIs/SSRIs/Linezolid can trigger Serotonin Syndrome.
-   **Presentation:** Hyperthermia, Autonomic Instability, Hyperreflexia/Clonus, Agitation.
-   **Libby Zion Case:** Caused by Meperidine + MAOI.
:::

## Clinical Uses beyond Pain

1.  **Cough Suppression (Antitussive):**
    -   **Dextromethorphan:** NMDA antagonist isomer of codeine analog. No analgesic/addictive properties at varying doses.
    -   **Codeine:** Effective at sub-analgesic doses.
2.  **Diarrhea:**
    -   **Loperamide (Imodium):** $\mu$ agonist that does **not** cross BBB. Acts locally on gut.
    -   **Diphenoxylate:** Combined with Atropine to discourage abuse.

## Review Questions

**1. A 65-year-old male with end-stage renal disease requires opioid analgesia for a fracture. Which agent is the safest choice to avoid metabolite accumulation and toxicity?**

A. Morphine
B. Meperidine
C. Fentanyl
D. Codeine
E. Hydrocodone

<details>
<summary><strong>Click to Reveal Answer</strong></summary>

**Answer: C (Fentanyl)**

**Rationale**:
Fentanyl is metabolized by the liver to inactive metabolites that are nontoxic, making it safer for patients with renal failure.
- A: Morphine metabolizes to M6G (potent) and M3G (neuroexcitatory), which accumulate in renal failure.
- B: Meperidine metabolizes to Normeperidine, causing seizures; strictly contraindicated.
- D, E: Codeine/Hydrocodone have active metabolites that can accumulate.

</details>

**2. A patient chronically taking high-dose morphine for cancer pain arrives with respiratory depression and pinpoint pupils. Naloxone is administered, stabilizing breathing. Which of the following effects of morphine will persist despite chronic use (i.e., minimal tolerance develops)?**

A. Euphoria
B. Sedation
C. Constipation
D. Nausea
E. Analgesia

<details>
<summary><strong>Click to Reveal Answer</strong></summary>

**Answer: C (Constipation)**

**Rationale**:
Tolerance develops to most opioid effects (analgesia, sedation, respiratory depression, euphoria). Little to **no tolerance** develops to **Constipation** and **Miosis**. Patients on chronic opioids almost always require a bowel regimen.

</details>

**3. A heroin addict is admitted for surgery. To control pain, the resident administers an analgesic that unexpectedly precipitates severe agitation, abdominal cramps, and dilated pupils. Which drug was likely given?**

A. Methadone
B. Fentanyl
C. Buprenorphine
D. Hydromorphone
E. Oxycodone

<details>
<summary><strong>Click to Reveal Answer</strong></summary>

**Answer: C (Buprenorphine)**

**Rationale**:
Buprenorphine is a **partial mu agonist**. In a patient tolerant to a full agonist (heroin), a partial agonist acts as a net **antagonist**, displacing the full agonist from the receptor and precipitating acute withdrawal (agitation, mydriasis, GI distress). Pentazocine (mixed agonist-antagonist) would do the same.

</details>

**4. Which opioid is associated with serotonin syndrome when combined with SSRIs and can lower the seizure threshold?**

A. Morphine
B. Fentanyl
C. Tramadol
D. Codeine
E. Methadone

<details>
<summary><strong>Click to Reveal Answer</strong></summary>

**Answer: C (Tramadol)**

**Rationale**:
Tramadol has a dual mechanism: weak mu agonism + inhibition of Serotonin/NE reuptake. This reuptake inhibition poses a risk for **Serotonin Syndrome** (with other serotonergic drugs) and it lowers the **seizure threshold**. Meperidine also carries a risk of serotonin syndrome.

</details>

**5. Which agent works as an anti-diarrheal by stimulating mu receptors in the GI tract without crossing the blood-brain barrier?**

A. Dextromethorphan
B. Loperamide
C. Diphenoxylate
D. Naloxone
E. Methylnaltrexone

<details>
<summary><strong>Click to Reveal Answer</strong></summary>

**Answer: B (Loperamide)**

**Rationale**:
Loperamide is a peripheral mu agonist that does not cross the BBB (due to P-glycoprotein efflux). It slows gut motility to treat diarrhea without causing CNS effects (euphoria/analgesia).
- C: Diphenoxylate crosses BBB at high doses (formulated with atropine).
- E: Methylnaltrexone is a peripheral *antagonist* used for opioid-induced constipation.

</details>

## Mind Map Structure

### Opioid Analgesics

#### Mechanism of Action
- **Receptors**: Mu, Kappa, Delta (Gi GPCRs)
- **Cellular Effect**: Close Ca2+ (Pre), Open K+ (Post) -> Hyperpolarization

#### Classification
- **Strong Agonists**: Morphine, Fentanyl, Methadone, Meperidine
- **Moderate Agonists**: Codeine, Oxycodone, Hydrocodone
- **Partial/Mixed**: Buprenorphine, Pentazocine (Precipitate Withdrawal)
- **Antagonists**: Naloxone (Rescue), Naltrexone (Maintenance)

#### Clinical Pearls
- **Renal Failure**: Use Fentanyl
- **Toxicity**: Coma, Miosis, Apnea (Give Naloxone)
- **Tolerance**: None to Miosis/Constipation
