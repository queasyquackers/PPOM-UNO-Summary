# Lecture #59: History and Physical Exam of the Aging Patient

**Lecturer:** P. Ryo, D.O.

## The Aging Population and Demographics (Slide 2-5)

The global population is undergoing a significant demographic shift, with the number of older adults increasing rapidly. By 2050, the U.S. population over 65 is expected to nearly double. This "epidemiologic transition" means that non-communicable chronic diseases are replacing infectious diseases as the primary burden of illness.

Because of this shift, there is a concomitant change in the "dependency ratio"—the ratio of those in the workforce to those depending on it (children, retirees). As the population ages, the demand for medical care, social services, and pension systems increases, often straining available resources.

## General Principles of Aging (Slide 6)

Geriatric medicine focuses on care for those >65 years old (with "Oldest Old" being >80). There are four fundamental principles that govern the physiology and clinical presentation of the aging patient:

1.  **Homeostatic Decline:** Organ reserves decrease, making patients less able to tolerate stress (illness, injury).
2.  **Multifactorial Comorbidities:** Patients rarely have just one problem; conditions interact.
3.  **Polypharmacy:** Multiple medications increase the risk of adverse drug events and interactions.
4.  **Atypical Presentation:** Disease often presents non-specifically (e.g., confusion instead of fever/pain).

## Physiological Changes of Aging (Slide 7-12)

Aging affects every organ system, leading to a progressive decline in functional reserve. This "homeostenosis" means that while resting function may be preserved, the body's ability to respond to maximal demand is reduced.

### Cardiovascular System
-   **Vascular Stiffening:** The aorta and arteries become rigid (arteriosclerosis), leading to increased systolic blood pressure and widened pulse pressure.
-   **Cardiac Function:** Maximal heart rate declines due to loss of pacemaker cells. There is a decreased response to beta-adrenergic stimulation.
-   **Structural Changes:** Left ventricular hypertrophy (mild) can occur to compensate for increased afterload.
-   **Clinical Finding:** An **S4 heart sound** is a common and often *normal* finding in healthy elderly adults due to decreased ventricular compliance (atrial kick pushing against a stiff ventricle).

### Respiratory System
-   **Mechanical Changes:** Chest wall stiffening and kyphosis reduce compliance.
-   **Lung Parenchyma:** Loss of elastic recoil causes air trapping and increased residual volume. Alveolar surface area decreases, reducing gas exchange capacity.
-   **Protective Mechanisms:** Cough reflex is blunted, increasing the risk of aspiration and pneumonia.

:::correlate
Atelectasis & Pneumonia

- **Pathophysiology:** Decreased lung elasticity, weaker respiratory muscles, and kyphosis lead to poor aeration. Blunted cough reflex enables aspiration.
- **Presentation:** Fever may be absent. Tachypnea or confusion may be the only signs.
- **Risk Factors:** Immobility, hospital admission, dysphagia.
- **Lecturer's Point:** Because physiologic reserves are low, a respiratory infection can rapidly lead to failure.
:::

### Gastrointestinal System
-   **Sensation:** Blunted peritoneal signs. A patient with an acute abdomen (e.g., appendicitis, diverticulitis) may have minimal guarding or rebound tenderness.
-   **Motility:** generally slows, leading to constipation.
-   **Oral Health:** Gingival recession and decreased saliva (xerostomia) increase the risk of caries and periodontal disease, affecting nutrition.

### Renal and Genitourinary System
-   **Renal Function:** GFR declines with age. Drug clearance is reduced, necessitating dose adjustments.
-   **Bladder:** Capacity decreases, and residual volume increases. Involuntary bladder contractions become more common.
-   **Men:** Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) is very common, causing obstruction.
-   **Women:** Atrophic vaginitis (due to estrogen loss) causes dryness, dyspareunia, and increased urinary tract infection risk.

:::correlate
Urinary Incontinence

- **Pathophysiology:** Multifactorial—bladder modification (detrusor instability), pelvic floor weakness, BPH (men), or functional inability to reach the toilet.
- **Presentation:** Involuntary leakage (Stress, Urge, Overflow, or Functional).
- **Lecturer's Point:** Incontinence is **NOT** a normal part of aging; it is a common but pathologic condition that should be treated to prevent social isolation.
:::

### Nervous System
-   **Sensory:** Decreased vibratory sense and proprioception (increasing fall risk).
-   **Reflexes:** Deep tendon reflexes may be diminished, especially at the ankles.
-   **Thermoregulation:** Susceptibility to hypothermia increases. Fever response to infection is blunted.

:::correlate
Presbycusis

- **Pathophysiology:** Sensorineural hearing loss due to loss of hair cells in the cochlea.
- **Presentation:** Bilateral, symmetric, high-frequency hearing loss. Difficulty hearing speech in noisy environments.
- **Social Impact:** Can lead to depression and social withdrawal if untreated.
:::

## Approach to the Geriatric Patient (Slide 13-17)

### The Office Environment
The physical setting should be adapted to minimize barriers.
-   **Lighting:** Bright, glare-free lighting accommodates presbyopia and cataracts.
-   **Access:** Remove clutter (fall hazards). Use chairs with arms to assist in standing.
-   **Temperature:** Keep rooms warm, as elderly patients are sensitive to cold.

### Communication Strategies
-   **Pace:** Slow down. Allow time for the patient to process questions and respond.
-   **Sensory Aids:** Ensure the patient is wearing their glasses and hearing aids.
-   **Position:** Sit at eye level, facing the patient directly to allow for lip-reading.
-   **Health Literacy:** Use large font for printed instructions. Avoid medical jargon.

## Functional Assessment (Slide 18-20)

Functional status is the "vital sign" of geriatrics. It is a stronger predictor of mortality and quality of life than specific disease diagnoses.

### Activities of Daily Living (ADLs)
These are basic self-care tasks required for survival. **DEATH** is a common mnemonic (Dressing, Eating, Ambulating/Transfer, Toileting, Hygiene/Bathing).
*   **Transcript items:** Bathing, Dressing, Toileting, Transferring, Continence, Feeding.

### Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs)
These are more complex skills needed for independent living in the community. **SHAFT** is a mnemonic (Shopping, Housekeeping, Accounting, Food prep, Telephone).
*   **Transcript items:** Telephone, Shopping, Food preparation, Housekeeping, Laundry, Transportation, Medications, Finances.
*   **Significance:** IADL impairment often precedes ADL impairment and is an early sign of cognitive decline (e.g., trouble managing finances).

:::highyield
**The "10-Minute Screener"**
A rapid, validated tool to assess key geriatric domains:
1.  **Vision:** Ask about difficulty; objective test (Snellen).
2.  **Hearing:** Whisper test or audiometry.
3.  **Mobility:** Timed Up and Go (TUG) test.
4.  **Urinary Incontinence:** "In the last year, have you lost your urine and gotten wet?"
5.  **Nutrition:** Weight loss >10lbs in 6 months?
6.  **Memory:** Three-item recall.
7.  **Depression:** "Do you often feel sad or depressed?"
:::

## Medication Management (Slide 21-22)

### The Beers Criteria
A list of "Potentially Inappropriate Medications" (PIMs) in older adults, published by the American Geriatrics Society (AGS).
-   **Goal:** Reduce adverse drug events (falls, delirium, GI bleeds).
-   **Common Culprits:** Benzodiazepines (falls/confusion), Anticholinergics (confusion/constipation), NSAIDs (GI bleed/renal injury), PPIs (C. diff/osteoporosis).
-   **Nuance:** It is a guideline, not a law. Sometimes a medication is necessary despite being on the list, but it requires careful documentation and monitoring.

### Polypharmacy
Defined as using multiple drugs (typically >5).
-   **Adverse Risk:** Increases exponentially with the number of drugs.
-   **Review:** "Brown Bag" review (bring all pill bottles to every visit) is the gold standard for reconciliation.

## Cognitive Screening (Slide 23-26)

Cognitive decline is common but often goes unreported. Early detection allows for planning and safety interventions.

| Test | Description | Pros | Cons |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **MMSE** (Folstein) | 30-point scale. Tests orientation, registration, attention, recall, language. | Widely known. Score <24 suggests impairment. | Copyrighted. Education/Language bias. Less sensitive for Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI). |
| **MoCA** (Montreal) | 30-point scale. Tests executive function, visuospatial, naming, memory. | **Excellent sensitivity for Mild Cognitive Impairment.** | Takes longer (~10-15 min). Requires training. |
| **Mini-Cog** | 1. Three-word registration<br>2. Clock Drawing Test<br>3. Three-word recall | Very fast (<3 min). High sensitivity for dementia. | Less detailed than MoCA. |

:::correlate
Dementia vs. Delirium

- **Dementia:** Chronic, progressive decline in cognition (months to years). Attention is usually intact until late stages.
- **Delirium:** **Acute** onset (hours to days), fluctuating course, **inattention** (hallmark). Usually reversible if underlying cause (UTI, meds, electrolyte imbalance) is treated.
- **Lecturer's Point:** The Mini-Cog is a great quick screen for baseline deficits, but always assess for acute changes (delirium) first.
:::

## Fall Risk and Mobility (Slide 27)

Falls are the leading cause of fatal and non-fatal injuries in older adults.
-   **Risk Factors:** Polypharmacy (>4 meds), environmental hazards (rugs, poor lighting), lower extremity weakness, sensory impairment.
-   **Assessment Tool:** **Timed Up and Go (TUG)**.
    -   Patient starts sitting, stands, walks 3 meters (10 ft), turns, walks back, and sits.
    -   **Normal:** < 10 seconds.
    -   **Abnormal (High Risk):** > 20 seconds (Transcript references >20s as abnormal/cutoff for high risk).

## Health Maintenance and Screening (Slide 28-35)

Decisions for screening should be individualized based on life expectancy (>10 years rule) and functional status, rather than chronological age alone.

### Cancer Screening
-   **Breast:** Mammography every 1-2 years until age 75. Discuss pros/cons after 75.
-   **Colorectal:** Colonoscopy/FIT testing until age 75. Decision 76-85 is individual. Stop >85.
-   **Prostate:** PSA screening is controversial; shared decision making based on risk/benefit.
-   **Lung:** Low-dose CT for high-risk smokers (50-80 yo, 20 pak-year history, current or quit <15y ago).

### Immunizations
-   **Influenza:** Annual (High-dose preferred for >65).
-   **Pneumococcal:** PCV15/20 followed by PPSV23 (if 15 used).
-   **Zoster (Shingles):** Shingrix (2 doses). Recommended for all >50.
-   **Tdap:** Tetanus booster every 10 years.
-   **COVID-19:** Follow current CDC guidelines.

### Osteoporosis Screening
-   **Tool:** **DEXA Scan** (Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry).
-   **Indication:**
    -   All Women **≥ 65 years**.
    -   Men **≥ 70 years**.
    -   Younger postmenopausal women or men 50-69 with risk factors (fragility fracture, steroids, RA, smoking, alcohol).
-   **Diagnosis (T-Score):**
    -   **Normal:** ≥ -1.0
    -   **Osteopenia:** -1.0 to -2.5
    -   **Osteoporosis:** ≤ -2.5 (Treatment indicated)
-   **FRAX Score:** Calculates 10-year fracture risk. Use to decide treatment in patients with Osteopenia (Treat if Hip Fracture risk ≥3% or Major Osteoporotic Fracture risk ≥20%).

:::highyield
**Elder Abuse & Neglect**
- **Types:** Physical, Sexual, Emotional, Financial, Neglect (including Self-Neglect).
- **Perpetrators:** 90% are family members.
- **Screening:** No single tool, but ask direct questions: "Do you feel safe at home?", "Is anyone hurting you?"
- **Action:** Mandatory reporting to **Adult Protective Services (APS)** if there is *suspicion*. You do not need proof.
:::

## Review Questions

**1. A 72-year-old female presents for a wellness visit. She has no complaints. Review of systems is positive only for occasional knee stiffness. In assessing her functional status using the "10-Minute Screener", which of the following findings would indicate a need for further evaluation for fall risk?**

A. Timed Up and Go (TUG) test of 8 seconds
B. Inability to recall 1 of 3 words after 1 minute
C. Positive response to "Do you often feel sad?"
D. Timed Up and Go (TUG) test of 25 seconds
E. Distant visual acuity of 20/30

<details>
<summary><strong>Click to Reveal Answer</strong></summary>

**Answer: D (Timed Up and Go (TUG) test of 25 seconds)**

**Rationale**:
The Timed Up and Go (TUG) test assesses mobility and fall risk. A time of less than 10 seconds is considered normal. A time greater than 20 seconds (as stated in the lecture) indicates impaired mobility and a high risk for falls, requiring intervention (PT, home safety eval). 8 seconds is normal. The other options refer to different domains (Cognition, Depression, Vision) but D is the specific mobility finding.

</details>

**2. An 80-year-old male with a history of hypertension and osteoarthritis is brought in by his daughter. She is concerned because "he seems slower." On physical exam, he has an S4 heart sound. His blood pressure is 150/80 mmHg. Which of the following is the most likely physiological explanation for the S4 finding in this patient?**

A. Severe Aortic Stenosis causing outflow obstruction
B. Dilated cardiomyopathy with reduced ejection fraction
C. Decreased left ventricular compliance due to aging
D. Mitral Regurgitation due to valve prolapse
E. Left Bundle Branch Block

<details>
<summary><strong>Click to Reveal Answer</strong></summary>

**Answer: C (Decreased left ventricular compliance due to aging)**

**Rationale**:
An S4 heart sound (atrial gallop) occurs when the atria contract forcefully against a stiff, non-compliant ventricle. In the elderly, arterial stiffening leads to increased afterload and mild LV hypertrophy/stiffness. The lecturer explicitly states that an S4 is a common and often "normal" finding in the aging heart due to these changes.

</details>

**3. During a medication review for a 78-year-old female, you note she is taking Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) for sleep. You decide to recommend stopping this medication based on the Beers Criteria. What is the primary concern with using this class of medication in the elderly?**

A. Increased risk of gastrointestinal bleeding
B. Increased risk of anticholinergic effects (confusion, falls, urinary retention)
C. Risk of electrolyte abnormalities (hyponatremia)
D. High potential for addiction and withdrawal
E. Risk of drug-induced liver injury

<details>
<summary><strong>Click to Reveal Answer</strong></summary>

**Answer: B (Increased risk of anticholinergic effects)**

**Rationale**:
Diphenhydramine is a first-generation antihistamine with strong anticholinergic properties. In the elderly, anticholinergics are high-risk ("Beers List") because they cause confusion, sedation, constipation, urinary retention, and increased fall risk due to orthostasis and cognitive slowing.

</details>

**4. You are evaluating a 67-year-old female for osteoporosis. She has no history of fractures. Her DEXA scan reveals a T-score of -1.8 in the lumbar spine and -1.6 in the total hip. Based on these findings, how would you classify her bone density status?**

A. Normal bone density
B. Osteopenia (Low bone mass)
C. Osteoporosis
D. Severe Osteoporosis
E. Indeterminate

<details>
<summary><strong>Click to Reveal Answer</strong></summary>

**Answer: B (Osteopenia)**

**Rationale**:
The WHO definitions based on T-scores are:
- Normal: ≥ -1.0
- Osteopenia: Between -1.0 and -2.5
- Osteoporosis: ≤ -2.5
Since her score is -1.8, she falls in the Osteopenia range. Treatment decisions would then be based on her FRAX score.

</details>

**5. Which of the following immunizations is recommended for virtually all immunocompetent adults over the age of 50 to prevent a painful dermatomal rash and post-herpetic neuralgia?**

A. Pneumococcal 23 (PPSV23)
B. Tetanus, Diphtheria, Pertussis (Tdap)
C. Meningococcal B (MenB)
D. Recombinant Zoster Vaccine (Shingrix)
E. Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib)

<details>
<summary><strong>Click to Reveal Answer</strong></summary>

**Answer: D (Recombinant Zoster Vaccine)**

**Rationale**:
The Recombinant Zoster Vaccine (Shingrix) is recommended for adults starting at age 50 to prevent Shingles (Herpes Zoster) and its complications. It is a 2-dose series. While Tdap and Pneumococcal are also important, Zoster is the specific vaccine for the condition described.

</details>

## Mind Map Structure

### The Aging Patient

#### Physiology of Aging
- **Cardiovascular**: Arterial stiffness -> Increased SBP, S4 heart sound
- **Respiratory**: Decreased compliance, blunted cough reflex (Pneumonia risk)
- **Renal**: Decreased GFR, decreased drug clearance
- **Sensory**: Presbycusis (High freq loss), Presbyopia, Cataracts

#### Functional Assessment
- **ADLs (DEATH)**: Dressing, Eating, Ambulating, Toileting, Hygiene
- **IADLs (SHAFT)**: Shopping, Housekeeping, Accounting, Food prep, Telephone
- **Fall Risk**: TUG Test (>12s abnormal)

#### Screening & Prevention
- **Cognition**: MMSE, Mini-Cog, MoCA (Best for MCI)
- **Osteoporosis**: DEXA (Women >65), T-score < -2.5
- **Vaccines**: Flu, Pneumococcal, Shingles (>50), Tdap
- **Meds**: Beers Criteria (Avoid Anticholinergics, Benzos)
