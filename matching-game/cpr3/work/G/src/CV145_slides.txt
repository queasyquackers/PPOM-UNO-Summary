Normal Human Sleep
•
Normal human sleep comprises two states-non-rapid eye 
movement (NREM) and REM sleep-that alternate cyclically 
across a sleep episode
•
REM sleep:
–
muscles are atonic
–
dreaming is typical
–
bursts of rapid eye movements
•
Sleep begins in NREM and progresses through deeper NREM stages 
(stages N1, N2, N3/4 (SWS)) before the first episode of REM sleep 
approximately 70 to 100 minutes later.
•
Thereafter, NREM sleep and REM sleep cycle with a period of 
approximately 90 minutes

--- SLIDE 15 ---
Normal Human Sleep and Aging
• Age-related changes are predictable:
–
Newborns enter REM sleep (called active sleep) before 
NREM (called quiet sleep) and have a shorter sleep cycle 
(approximately 50 minutes)
–
Sleep stages emerge as the brain matures during the first 
year
–
At birth, active sleep is approximately 50% of total sleep and 
declines over the first 2 years to approximately 20% to 25%
–
Slow wave sleep (stages 3 and 4) decreases across 
adolescence by 40% from preteen years and continues to 
decline into old age, particularly in men and less so in 
women
–
REM sleep as a percentage of total sleep is approximately 
20% to 25% across childhood, adolescence, adulthood, and 
into old age except in dementia. 
1

--- SLIDE 16 ---
Normal Human Sleep
•
Generalizations about Sleep in the Normal 
Young Adult
–
Sleep is entered through NREM sleep
–
NREM sleep and REM sleep alternate with a period 
near 90 minutes
–
SWS predominates in the first third of the night and 
is linked to the initiation of sleep
–
REM sleep predominates in the last third of the 
night and is linked to the circadian rhythm of body 
temperature
1

--- SLIDE 17 ---
Normal Human Sleep
– Generalizations about Sleep in the Normal Young 
Adult
•
Wakefulness in sleep usually accounts for less than 5% of 
the night
•
Stage 1 sleep generally constitutes approximately 2% to 
5% of sleep
•
Stage 2 sleep generally constitutes approximately 45% to 
55% of sleep
•
SWS sleep generally constitutes approximately 13% to 
23% of sleep
•
NREM sleep, therefore, is usually 75% to 80% of sleep
•
REM sleep is usually 20% to 25% of sleep, occurring in 
four to six discrete episodes. 
1

--- SLIDE 19 ---
Normal Human Sleep and Aging
• Altered sleep architecture:
–
increased percentage of time spent in stage 1
–
decreased percentage spent in SWS
–
percentage of time spent in rapid eye movement (REM) 
sleep demonstrates a small (2% to 3%) decrease from 
middle to old age
–
sleep efficiency lowers with age as well, declining from 
approximately 86% at about age 45 to about 79% in those 
older than 70
–
Brief arousals are common in aging and occur at rates of 
about 15 per hour in the elderly

INTRODUCTION
•
The term "sleep apnea" encompasses a number of different clinical problems.
•
In obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), the most common form of sleep apnea, episodes 
of apnea occur during sleep as a result of airway obstruction at the level of the 
pharynx. 
2

--- SLIDE 24 ---
EPIDEMIOLOGY 
A large prevalence study in state employees found that 
undiagnosed sleep disordered breathing "is prevalent 
and has a wide range of severity in middle aged women 
and men"
In this study, 9.1 percent of men and 4.0 percent of 
women had apnea/hypopnea indices of 15 or more 
events per hour. 
Young, T, Palta, M, Dempsey, J, et al. The occurrence of sleep-disordered breathing among middle-aged 
adults. N Engl J Med 1993; 328:1230 
2

--- SLIDE 25 ---
EPIDEMIOLOGY
It is estimated that, in the United States alone, more 
than 3 million men and 1.5 million women meet at least 
one definition of OSA (apnea/hypopnea index of five or 
more plus a complaint of daytime sleepiness)
It also appears that clinicians are recognizing OSA in their 
patients with increasing frequency
2

--- SLIDE 26 ---
PATHOPHYSIOLOGY 
•
The pharynx, serves a number of functions which seem to conflict, it must be 
collapsible, for speech and swallowing and must be a conduit for airflow.  
•
The answer to problem involves a group of muscles that can change the shape of 
the pharynx when we swallow or talk, but hold it open during inhalation. 
2

--- SLIDE 27 ---
•
The following physiologic changes are seen with sleep:
–
Reduced tonic input to upper airway muscles
–
Diminished reflexes that protect the pharynx from collapse
PATHOPHYSIOLOGY 
2

--- SLIDE 28 ---
•
Sleep is associated with pharyngeal narrowing and a 
substantial increase in inspiratory resistance even in normal 
individuals.
•
An abnormal pharynx can be kept open in wakefulness by an 
appropriate compensatory increase in dilator muscle activity, 
but during sleep this compensation fails and the airway 
collapses.
•
Partial collapse results in snoring, hypopneas, and, in some 
cases, prolonged obstructive hypoventilation. Complete 
closure results in an apnea.
PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

--- SLIDE 61 ---
Restless Legs Syndrome and Periodic Limb Movements 
during Sleep 

Neurologic condition characterized by an urge to move, 
usually associated with paresthesia, that occurs or worsens at 
rest and is relieved by activity. 

Most patients report difficulty falling asleep or waking up 
shortly after sleep onset with unpleasant leg sensations

They also often experience excessive daytime fatigue and 
somnolence, probably as a consequence of disrupted 
nocturnal sleep. 
6

--- SLIDE 62 ---
Restless Legs Syndrome and Periodic 
Limb Movements during Sleep 
Clinical Diagnosis {URGE pneumonic}
The urge to move or unpleasant sensations
accompanied or caused by uncomfortable and unpleasant 
sensations in the legs 
begin or worsen during periods of rest or inactivity such as 
lying and sitting 
partially or totally relieved by movement, such as walking or 
stretching, at least as long as activity continues 
worse in the evening or night than during the day or occur only 
in the evening or night 
6

--- SLIDE 63 ---
Restless Legs Syndrome and Periodic Limb Movements 
during Sleep 
• Treatment:
–
Dopaminergic Agonists: Pramipexole, Ropinirole
–
Dopaminergic Medications: Levadopa
–
Opioids
–
Anticonvulsants: Carbamazepine not useful. Gabapentin 
showed subjective improvement at doses of 300 to 2400 
mg/day
–
Benzodiazepines, including clonazepam, nitrazepam, 
lorazepam, and temazepam, improve the quality of sleep

--- SLIDE 65 ---
Narcolepsy
•
Syndrome of unknown origin that is characterized by:
–
Abnormal sleep tendencies
–
Excessive daytime sleepiness
–
Disturbed nocturnal sleep
–
Sleep onset REM periods
–
Cataplexy
–
Sleep paralysis
–
Hypnagogic hallucinations
6

--- SLIDE 66 ---
Narcolepsy

The first symptoms often develop near the age of puberty

The peak age at which reported symptoms occur is 15 to 25 years

A second, smaller peak of onset has been noted between 35 and 45 years and near menopause in 
women

There is often a marked delay of over 10 years before diagnosis is made, especially if cataplexy is 
initially absent
6

--- SLIDE 67 ---
Narcolepsy
Diagnosis:
The nocturnal polysomnogram may indicate the underlying cause of sleepiness
MSLT indicates the severity of the problem
Once the nocturnal sleep recording has eliminated specific diseases, the MSLT 
confirms the diagnosis of narcolepsy with the presence of two or more sleep-onset 
REM periods. 
6

--- SLIDE 68 ---
Narcolepsy
Diagnosis:
The ***multiple sleep latency test (MSLT)*** was designed to measure physiologic 
sleep tendencies in the absence of alerting factors
It consists of five scheduled naps, usually at 9 am, 11am, and 1, 3, and 5 pm
The subject is polygraphically monitored in a comfortable, soundproof, dark bedroom, 
while wearing street clothes 
6

--- SLIDE 69 ---
Narcolepsy
•
Diagnosis:
•
Genetic testing: human leukocyte antigen DQB1*0602 
•
In most patients with narcolepsy, there is an absence or very low level of hypocretin in the 
cerebrospinal fluid
6

CRITERIA FOR 
DEFINING INSOMNIA
 
•
 
Difficulty falling asleep, or
 
 
•
 
Difficulty staying asleep, or
 
 
•
 
Early-morning awakening, or 
 
•
 
Nonrefreshing or nonrestorative sleep.
With daytime consequences or distress lasting at least 1 month 
despite opportunity and circumstances to sleep.
The daytime consequences of insomnia include impaired cognitive function, 
fatigue or tiredness, depressed mood or irritability, and, in adults, impaired 
performance at work, and, in children, poor performance in school.
7
