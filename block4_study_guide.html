📋 Lec 149 — ABO Groups, Rh Disease & Transfusion Medicine

ABO Blood Group System

  • H Gene: codes for fucosyltransferase → adds fucose to paragloboside → creates H-antigen (the foundation)
  • A Gene: N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase → adds N-acetylgalactosamine to H-substance
  • B Gene: galactosyltransferase → adds galactose to H-substance
  • O Gene: amorph — no functional enzyme; H-substance unmodified
  • Antibodies: naturally occurring by 3–6 months; predominantly IgM (Type A/B); Type O produces significant IgG (crosses placenta!)
  • ABO antibodies fix complement → rapid intravascular hemolysis
Bombay Phenotype (h/h) = no H-gene → can't make H-substance → phenotypes as O but has anti-H → can ONLY receive Bombay blood. Reverse-grouping trap: serum has anti-A + anti-B + anti-H (vs true Type O which has only anti-A + anti-B).

Rh System & HDFN

  • D-antigen: most immunogenic Rh antigen; Rh+ = has D; Rh− = lacks D
  • Anti-D: NOT naturally occurring; requires sensitization; IgG → extravascular hemolysis (spleen)
  • Rh-HDFN mechanism: 1st pregnancy = Rh− mom sensitized by Rh+ fetus → anti-D IgG formed; 2nd pregnancy = IgG crosses placenta → attacks fetal RBCs
  • In utero: fetal anemia → extramedullary hematopoiesis → Hydrops Fetalis
  • Post-natal: Kernicterus — unconjugated bilirubin deposits in basal ganglia
  • ABO-HDFN: Type O mom + Type A/B baby; can occur 1st pregnancy (pre-formed); generally milder
RhoGAM: passive anti-D IgG given at 28 weeks and within 72 hours postpartum. Clears fetal RBCs before maternal sensitization.

Blood Components & Transfusion Reactions

Component Therapy
  • pRBCs: ↑ O₂ capacity; Hb <7 threshold; 1 unit = +1 Hb, +3% Hct; stored refrigerated 35–42 days

📋 Lec 151 — Laboratory Medicine & Anemias Part 1

RBC Basics & Indices

  • Erythrocytes: biconcave disc, anucleate; lifespan ~120 days; recycled by splenic macrophages
  • Reticulocytes: immature RBCs; normal 1–2%; basophilic (residual RNA); mature in 24–48h
  • Rule of Three: RBC × 3 = Hb; Hb × 3 = Hct
  • MCV: 80–100 fL (normal); classifies anemias as micro/normo/macrocytic
  • RDW: measures size variability (anisocytosis)
  • Transfusion: 1 unit pRBC = +1 g/dL Hb, +3% Hct

Iron Metabolism & Studies

  • Serum Iron: circulating iron level
  • Ferritin: iron storage protein (macrophages + liver); best marker of iron stores
  • TIBC: represents circulating transferrin
  • % Saturation: fraction of transferrin bound to iron
  • Ferritin and TIBC are inversely related — low stores → liver ↑ transferrin production
  • Iron absorption: duodenum; regulated by hepcidin (blocks ferroportin)
  • Absorption pathway: dietary Fe³⁺ → reduced by DcytB ferrireductase → enters enterocyte via DMT1 → exits basolateral via ferroportin → bound by transferrin. Vitamin C enhances; PPIs/antacids/gastrectomy reduce.
  • FEP (Free Erythrocyte Protoporphyrin): ↑ in IDA and lead poisoning; normal in thalassemia — useful discriminator.
  • IDA stages: stores deplete first (↓ ferritin) → serum iron drops → normocytic with ↑RDW → only later becomes microcytic. Early IDA can fool you.

📋 Lec 152 — Laboratory Medicine & Anemias Part 2

Macrocytic Anemias (MCV > 100)

Folate (B9) Deficiency
  • Absorbed in jejunum; stores last only months
  • Causes: poor diet ("tea and toast"), alcoholics, pregnancy, methotrexate
  • Labs: ↓ folate, ↑ homocysteine, normal MMA
  • No neurological symptoms
Vitamin B12 Deficiency
  • Absorbed in terminal ileum (requires Intrinsic Factor from parietal cells)
  • Causes: pernicious anemia (#1), Crohn's, D. latum tapeworm, pancreatic insufficiency
  • Labs: ↓ B12, ↑ homocysteine, ↑ MMA
  • Subacute Combined Degeneration: dorsal columns (proprioception/vibration) + lateral corticospinal tracts
Treatment trap: Giving folate alone to a B12-deficient patient will correct the anemia but worsen the neurological damage. Always rule out B12 deficiency first!
Both ↑ homocysteine. Only B12 ↑ MMA. "MMA = M for Methylcobalamin = B12 only." Hypersegmented neutrophils (≥5 lobes) = hallmark of megaloblastic anemia.

Normocytic Anemias — Hemolysis vs Underproduction

  • Corrected Reticulocyte Count (CRC): Retic % × (Patient Hct / 45)
  • CRC > 3% = peripheral destruction (hemolysis) or acute blood loss
  • CRC < 3% = bone marrow underproduction (↓ EPO, marrow failure)

Extrinsic Hemolytic Anemias

  • Warm AIHA (IgG): extravascular; SLE, CLL, methyldopa; Direct Coombs (+); "Warm and Gooey (IgG)"
  • Cold AIHA (IgM): complement-mediated; Mycoplasma pneumoniae, EBV mono; cold exposure trigger
  • MAHA: mechanical shearing through narrowed vessels; schistocytes; TTP, HUS, DIC, HELLP
  • Macroangiopathic: prosthetic heart valves, severe AS
  • Infections: malaria (Plasmodium), babesiosis (Maltese cross on smear)

Anemia of Underproduction (CRC < 3%)

  • Renal failure: ↓ EPO production → normocytic anemia; Tx: exogenous EPO
  • Parvovirus B19: infects RBC progenitors; "slapped cheek" rash; aplastic crisis in sickle cell/thalassemia
  • Aplastic anemia: HSC damage → pancytopenia; fatty hypocellular marrow; causes: radiation, benzene, chloramphenicol, EBV
  • Fanconi anemia: congenital DNA repair defect; short stature, thumb/radial defects, café-au-lait spots
  • Diamond-Blackfan: congenital pure RBC aplasia; triphalangeal thumbs, craniofacial defects; macrocytic

Additional Anemia Pearls

Orotic Aciduria
  • UMP synthase deficiency → can't make pyrimidines → megaloblastic anemia
  • NOT corrected by B12 or folate (key trap)
  • Orotic acid in urine + NO hyperammonemia (vs OTC deficiency, which has hyperammonemia)
  • Tx: uridine triacetate
Non-Megaloblastic Macrocytic
  • Causes: alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, hydroxyurea, 5-FU, AZT
  • NO hypersegmented neutrophils (key distinction from B12/folate)
  • MCV usually only mildly elevated (100–110)
  • Universal hemolysis labs: ↑ LDH, ↑ unconjugated bilirubin, ↓ haptoglobin, ↑ reticulocytes, pigmented (bilirubin) gallstones
  • Hemosiderinuria: appears days after intravascular hemolysis (renal tubular cells shed iron); distinct from acute hemoglobinuria
  • Drug-induced AIHA: methyldopa (warm), penicillin/cephalosporins (hapten), quinidine (immune complex). Direct Coombs (+).
  • Pernicious anemia mechanism: autoimmune destruction of parietal cells → no IF + ↑ gastric pH + atrophic gastritis. Associated with Hashimoto's, vitiligo, T1DM. ↑ gastric adenocarcinoma risk.

📋 Lec 158 — Hemostasis

Primary Hemostasis (Platelet Plug Formation)

1. Adhesion
  • Endothelial injury exposes subendothelial collagen and Von Willebrand Factor (vWF) (from Weibel-Palade bodies).
  • Platelets bind vWF via the GpIb receptor.
2. Activation & Aggregation
  • Platelets change shape and degranulate.
  • ADP (binds P2Y12 receptor) and TXA₂ (vasoconstrictor) promote further activation.
  • GpIIb/IIIa receptors on platelets bind Fibrinogen to cross-link and aggregate into a plug.
Antiplatelet Drugs: Aspirin blocks COX-1 (↓ TXA₂). Clopidogrel blocks the ADP P2Y12 receptor.

Secondary Hemostasis (Coagulation Cascade)

Stabilizes the weak platelet plug by converting soluble fibrinogen into an insoluble fibrin mesh.

Natural Anticoagulants & Fibrinolysis

  • Protein C & S: Vitamin K dependent. Inactivate factors Va and VIIIa.
  • Antithrombin (ATIII): Binds and degrades Thrombin, IXa, and Xa. (Heparin works by greatly enhancing ATIII).
  • Fibrinolysis: Plasminogen is converted to Plasmin (by tPA), which degrades the fibrin mesh.
  • Vit K factor mnemonic "1972 + C/S": Factors II, VII, IX, X + Protein C & S all require vit-K-dependent γ-carboxylation (warfarin's targets).
  • Vit K deficiency labs: prolongs BOTH PT and PTT. Factor VII shortest t½ → PT rises first.

Bleeding Disorders Quick ID

  • vWF Disease: Most common. Defect in vWF. (Primary hemostasis problem).
  • Bernard-Soulier: GpIb receptor deficiency (adhesion fails).
  • Glanzmann: GpIIb/IIIa deficiency (aggregation fails).
  • Thrombocytopenia: ↓ platelets, ↑ bleeding time.
  • Hemophilia: Elevated aPTT, deep joint bleeding.

Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

  • Pathophysiology: widespread activation of coagulation → consumption of clotting factors AND platelets → simultaneous thrombosis + hemorrhage
  • Causes: sepsis (gram-negative, endotoxin), obstetric complications (amniotic fluid embolism, placental abruption), malignancy (acute promyelocytic leukemia), trauma, burns
  • Labs: ↑ PT, ↑ aPTT, ↓ platelets, ↓ fibrinogen, ↑↑ D-dimer (fibrin degradation products), schistocytes on smear
  • Treatment: treat underlying cause; FFP (replace factors), cryoprecipitate (replace fibrinogen), platelet transfusion; heparin may be used in chronic DIC
DIC = "Die Is Cast": everything is consumed → ALL coag labs abnormal. Unlike other disorders that affect only one pathway.

Hypercoagulable States (Thrombophilias)

  • Factor V Leiden: most common inherited thrombophilia. Factor V resistant to inactivation by Protein C → DVT/PE. Heterozygous = 5× risk; homozygous = 50×
  • Prothrombin Gene Mutation (G20210A): 2nd most common. ↑ prothrombin levels → ↑ thrombin generation
  • Antithrombin Deficiency: heparin resistance (heparin works through AT!). ↑ risk of venous thrombosis
  • Protein C or S Deficiency: cannot inactivate Va/VIIIa. Warfarin skin necrosis (C has shortest half-life)
  • Antiphospholipid Syndrome: lupus anticoagulant, anti-cardiolipin, anti-β2GP1. Paradox: ↑ aPTT in vitro but hypercoagulable in vivo. Recurrent pregnancy loss + thrombosis. Mixing study does NOT correct (unlike factor deficiency); false-positive RPR/VDRL.

Mixing Study Interpretation

  • Purpose: distinguish between factor deficiency vs inhibitor when PT or aPTT is elevated
  • Method: mix patient plasma 1:1 with normal plasma, then re-measure clotting time
  • Corrects → Factor Deficiency: normal plasma supplies the missing factor (e.g., hemophilia)
  • Does NOT correct → Inhibitor present: an antibody inhibits the factor even when supplied (e.g., lupus anticoagulant, factor VIII inhibitor)

Sickle Cell Anemia (Pediatric Complications)

Acute Crises
  • Dactylitis: Often 1st sign in infants (hand/foot swelling).
  • Acute Chest Syndrome: New infiltrate + fever/hypoxia. #1 cause of death.
  • Splenic Sequestration: Shock, rapidly falling Hb, elevated reticulocytes, massive spleen.
  • Aplastic Crisis: Parvovirus B19 infection. Very low reticulocytes.
Chronic Management
  • Functional Asplenia: Autosplenectomy by age 5-6 due to infarctions. Massive risk for encapsulated bacteria (Sepsis).
  • Prophylaxis: Daily Penicillin until age 5. Pneumococcal/Hib/Meningococcal vaccines.
  • Treatment: Hydroxyurea (↑ HbF), Transcranial Doppler (stroke risk).

Pediatric Bleeding Disorders

  • Hemorrhagic Disease of Newborn: Vitamin K deficiency (poor placental transfer, sterile gut). Week 1 bleeding (umbilicus, GI, brain). Prevented by IM Vit K at birth.
  • Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP): Acute isolated thrombocytopenia 1-4 weeks post-virus. Petechiae/purpura in healthy child. Anti-GpIIb/IIIa Abs. Tx: Observation, IVIG, steroids.
  • Hemophilia A (VIII) / B (IX): X-linked recessive. Deep bleeding (hemarthrosis, iliopsoas muscle). Normal PT, ↑ aPTT.
  • Von Willebrand Disease: Autosomal dominant. Mucocutaneous bleeding (epistaxis, heavy menses). Tx: Desmopressin (DDAVP).

Bone Tumors

  • Osteosarcoma: Metaphysis of long bones (knee). "Sunburst" pattern, Codman's triangle. Associated with Rb/p53 mutations. Occurs during growth spurt.
  • Ewing Sarcoma: Diaphysis. "Onion skinning" periosteal reaction. Sheets of small round blue cells. t(11;22) translocation. Presents with systemic signs (fever, wt loss).

📋 Lec 150 — Hematopoiesis

Hematopoietic Stem Cell Differentiation

  • Pluripotent HSC → common myeloid progenitor (CMP) + common lymphoid progenitor (CLP). HSC marker: CD34+ (used to identify/harvest stem cells for BMT).
  • CMP → megakaryocyte (platelets), erythrocyte (RBCs), granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils), monocytes
  • CLP → T cells, B cells, NK cells
  • Erythropoiesis: proerythroblast → basophilic → polychromatic → orthochromatic → reticulocyte → mature RBC
  • EPO: produced by peritubular interstitial cells in renal cortex in response to hypoxia; stimulates erythroid proliferation + differentiation
  • Thrombopoietin (TPO): produced by liver; stimulates megakaryocyte maturation → platelet production
  • Colony-Stimulating Factors: G-CSF (neutrophils), M-CSF (monocytes), GM-CSF (granulocytes + monocytes)
  • Sites: yolk sac (weeks 3–8) → liver/spleen (6 wk–birth) → bone marrow (birth onward); adults = axial skeleton

Pathology of Hemorrhage (Lec 161)

  • Primary Hemostasis Defects: Platelet/vWF issues. Present with mucosal bleeding, petechiae, purpura. e.g. ITP, TTP, HUS, vWD.
  • ITP (Immune Thrombocytopenic Purpura): Anti-GpIIb/IIIa antibodies. Acute (kids post-viral) vs Chronic (adults). Splenic destruction.
  • TTP (Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura): ADAMTS13 deficiency → uncleaved vWF multimers → microthrombi. Pentad: FAT RN (Fever, Anemia, Thrombocytopenia, Renal, Neuro). Schistocytes.
  • Qualitative Platelet Disorders: Normal platelet count, but prolonged bleeding time. Bernard-Soulier: GpIb deficiency (adhesion defect) — giant platelets + mild thrombocytopenia. Glanzmann: GpIIb/IIIa deficiency — normal platelet size/count.
  • Ristocetin Cofactor Assay: tests vWF function. vWD = no agglutination, corrects when vWF added. Bernard-Soulier = no agglutination, fails to correct with vWF (GpIb is broken).
  • Henoch-Schönlein Purpura (IgA Vasculitis): classic peds tetrad — palpable purpura (buttocks/lower legs) + arthralgia + abdominal pain + IgA nephropathy. Often post-URI. Normal platelets/coags. Usually self-limited.
  • Secondary Hemostasis Defects: Coagulation factor issues. Present with deep tissue bleeding, hemarthrosis. e.g. Hemophilia A (VIII) & B (IX).

Pathology of Thrombosis (Lec 162)

  • Virchow's Triad: Endothelial injury, Stasis/Turbulence, Hypercoagulability.
  • Factor V Leiden: Most common inherited thrombophilia. Mutated Factor V is resistant to cleavage by Protein C.
  • Lines of Zahn: Alternating pale (platelet/fibrin) and dark (RBC) layers. Distinguishes true pre-mortem thrombus from post-mortem clot.
  • Arterial vs Venous: Arterial thrombi usually at sites of turbulence/endothelial injury (atherosclerosis). Venous thrombi usually at sites of stasis (DVT).
  • Trousseau Syndrome: migratory superficial thrombophlebitis with visceral malignancy — classically pancreatic adenocarcinoma (also lung, GI). May precede cancer diagnosis.
  • Paradoxical Embolism: venous thrombus crosses to arterial circulation via PFO/ASD → systemic embolism (stroke despite DVT source).

📋 Lec 181, 182 — Skin Pathology: Non-Neoplastic & Tumors

Non-Neoplastic Skin Pathology (Lec 181)

  • Urticaria (Hives): Type I hypersensitivity (IgE/Mast cell degranulation). Dermal edema. Wheals that come and go.
  • Eczema (Atopic Dermatitis): Type IV hypersensitivity. Spongiosis (epidermal edema). Pruritic, flexural surfaces.
  • Psoriasis: Autoimmune (T-cell mediated). Acanthosis (epidermal hyperplasia), parakeratosis (nuclei in stratum corneum), absent stratum granulosum. Auspitz sign (bleeding when scale removed).
  • Bullous Pemphigoid: IgG against hemidesmosomes (BP180). Tense bullae, do not rupture easily. Eosinophils. Negative Nikolsky.
  • Pemphigus Vulgaris: IgG against desmoglein 1/3 (desmosomes). Flaccid bullae, rupture easily, oral mucosa involved. Positive Nikolsky. "Tombstone" appearance.

Additional Non-Neoplastic Path (Lec 181)

  • Erythema Multiforme: T-cell-mediated (Type IV). Target ("iris") lesions on extensor surfaces/palms/soles. #1 trigger = HSV recurrence; also Mycoplasma. SJS = severe form with mucosal involvement.
  • Lichen Planus path: sawtooth rete ridges, Civatte (apoptotic) bodies, band-like lymphocytic infiltrate at the DEJ. Hep C association.
  • Dermatitis Herpetiformis path: neutrophilic microabscesses at dermal papillae tips + granular IgA deposition (NOT linear). Anti-tissue/epidermal transglutaminase. Tx: gluten-free diet + dapsone.
  • Hereditary Angioedema: C1-esterase inhibitor deficiency → unopposed bradykinin. Recurrent non-pruritic facial/laryngeal/GI swelling. NO urticaria; does NOT respond to epi/antihistamines. Tx: C1-INH concentrate, ecallantide, icatibant. (ACE-I angioedema = same bradykinin mechanism.)
  • Pemphigus IF: "fishnet" intercellular IgG (around keratinocytes). BP IF: linear IgG along BMZ. Distinguish on direct immunofluorescence.

Skin Tumors (Lec 182)

  • Seborrheic Keratosis: Benign squamous proliferation. "Stuck-on" appearance, horn cysts (keratin filled). Leser-Trélat sign (sudden onset) associated with GI malignancy.
  • Actinic Keratosis: Pre-malignant lesion (can become SCC). Sun-exposed areas. Sandpaper texture. Parakeratosis and dysplasia.
  • Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC): Most common skin cancer. Sun-exposed areas (upper lip). Pearly papule with telangiectasias. Palisading nuclei with retraction artifact. Rarely metastasizes.
  • Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC): Second most common. Sun exposure, arsenic, chronic wounds (Marjolin ulcer). Lower lip. Keratin pearls and intercellular bridges.
  • Melanoma: Malignant tumor of melanocytes. ABCDE criteria. S-100, HMB-45, Melan-A positive. Depth of invasion (Breslow thickness) is the most important prognostic factor.

📋 Lec 190, 191, 192 — Physiology: Thermoregulation & Temp Emergencies

Thermoregulation (Lec 190)

  • Hypothalamus: The body's thermostat. Anterior = cooling (parasympathetic), Posterior = heating (sympathetic).
  • Heat Production: BMR, muscle activity (shivering), non-shivering thermogenesis (brown fat in neonates via UCP1/thermogenin).
  • Heat Loss: Radiation (60% at rest), Evaporation (22% at rest, primary mechanism during exercise/sweating), Conduction, Convection.
  • Fever vs Hyperthermia: Fever = set point is raised by pyrogens (PGE2). Hyperthermia = set point is normal, but body cannot dissipate heat fast enough (e.g. heat stroke).
  • Pyrogen Cascade: infection/tissue damage → TLR/macrophage → IL-1, IL-6, TNF, IFN → preoptic hypothalamic endothelium → PGE2 → ↑ set point. Antipyretics (NSAIDs/APAP) inhibit at PGE2 (COX); corticosteroids inhibit cytokine release.
  • Sweat physiology: eccrine glands innervated by postganglionic CHOLINERGIC sympathetic (boards trap — sympathetic but ACh, not NE). Duct contains CFTR + ENaC — explains the elevated sweat chloride in CF.
  • Fever phases: "Chills" (set-point rising → vasoconstriction + shivering, feels cold) → "Crisis" (resolution → vasodilation + sweating).

Cold-Related Injuries (Lec 191)

  • Trench Foot: Prolonged exposure to non-freezing cold/moisture. Vasospasm → ischemia.
  • Frostbite: Freezing of tissue. Ice crystals form. Rewarm in circulating warm water (37-39°C). Do not rub! Clear blisters = favorable; Hemorrhagic = poor prognosis.
  • Hypothermia: Core temp <35°C (95°F). Osborn J waves on EKG. Mild (shivering) vs Moderate (loss of shivering, confusion) vs Severe (coma, VFib). Handle gently to avoid inducing VFib. "You're not dead until you're warm and dead."

Heat-Related Injuries (Lec 192)

  • Acclimatization: Takes 1-2 weeks. Increased sweat volume, decreased sweat sodium (aldosterone effect), earlier onset of sweating.
  • Heat Exhaustion: Volume depletion from sweating. Core temp <40°C (104°F). Normal mental status. Sweating is present. Tx: oral/IV fluids, cooling.
  • Heat Stroke: Medical emergency! Core temp >40°C (104°F) + CNS dysfunction (confusion, ataxia, coma). Anhidrosis (classic/non-exertional) or profuse sweating (exertional). Tx: Rapid cooling (ice water immersion is gold standard for exertional).
Key distinction: Mental status changes (confusion, delirium) are the hallmark of Heat Stroke vs Heat Exhaustion.

Heat Stroke — Pathophysiology & Treatment Pearls

  • Cascade: hyperthermia → cellular protein denaturation → gut barrier breakdown → endotoxin release → systemic inflammatory response → endothelial damage → MOSF + DIC + rhabdomyolysis + AKI
  • Classic vs Exertional: Classic = elderly, no AC, comorbidities/drugs, anhidrotic. Exertional = athletes/military in first ~4 days; profuse sweating preserved.
  • CNS findings: ataxia, seizures, upgoing plantars (Babinski), cerebellar dysfunction (cerebellum especially heat-sensitive)
  • Cooling: evaporative cooling + large fan = preferred for classic heat stroke. Ice-water immersion = gold standard for exertional only. Treat shivering during cooling with benzodiazepines.
  • Antipyretics (APAP/NSAIDs) have NO ROLE — set point isn't elevated; they may worsen hepatic/renal injury
  • Vasopressor choice: prefer β1 (dobutamine) over α-agonists (norepi worsens cutaneous vasoconstriction)
Drugs that impair thermoregulation (predispose to heat illness): anticholinergics (impair sweating), phenothiazines/antipsychotics (anticholinergic + central dopamine), diuretics (volume), β-blockers/CCBs (blunt tachycardic response), sympathomimetics (↑ heat production), ethanol (ADH suppression, vasodilation).

📋 Lec 153 — Hematologic Pharmacology: Iron, B12, Folate & EPO

Iron Supplementation

Ferrous Sulfate (Oral Iron)
MOA: provides elemental iron for hemoglobin synthesis.
Indication: Iron deficiency anemia (first-line oral therapy).
PK: absorbed in duodenum; enhanced by vitamin C (acidic environment); inhibited by antacids, calcium, tetracyclines, PPIs.
AE: GI distress (#1 — nausea, constipation, black stools), iron overload with chronic use.
Monitoring: reticulocyte count ↑ in 5–7 days; Hb normalizes in 6–8 weeks; continue 3–6 months to replenish stores.
Toxicity: acute iron poisoning (children) → hemorrhagic gastroenteritis → shock → hepatic necrosis. Tx: Deferoxamine (IV iron chelator).

B12 & Folate Supplementation

  • Cyanocobalamin (Vitamin B12): IM or PO. Required for methionine synthase (DNA synthesis). Deficiency → megaloblastic anemia + neurologic findings (subacute combined degeneration, peripheral neuropathy).
  • Folic Acid: PO. Required for thymidylate synthase. Deficiency → megaloblastic anemia WITHOUT neuro findings. Supplementation in pregnancy prevents neural tube defects.
Exam trap: Always check B12 before giving folate alone — folate can mask B12 deficiency anemia while neurologic damage progresses!

Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents & Iron Overload

  • Epoetin alfa (EPO) / Darbepoetin: Recombinant EPO. Stimulates erythroid progenitor cells. Used in CKD-associated anemia, chemo-induced anemia. AE: hypertension, pure red cell aplasia (anti-EPO antibodies), thromboembolic events. Target Hb 10–11 (not normal — increases mortality).
  • IV Iron (Iron Sucrose, Ferric Carboxymaltose): Used when oral iron fails/intolerant, CKD patients on EPO. AE: anaphylaxis risk (test dose recommended for some formulations).
  • Iron Chelators: Deferoxamine (IV, acute poisoning), Deferasirox (PO, chronic overload in transfusion-dependent patients e.g. thalassemia).

Anticoagulant & Antiplatelet Pearls

  • Fondaparinux: synthetic pentasaccharide — pure Factor Xa inhibitor via AT. Safe in HIT (no PF4 cross-reactivity). NOT reversed by protamine. Renal clearance.
  • HIT type II timing: typically 5–14 days after heparin start. Avoid LMWH (cross-reactivity). Switch to argatroban (hepatic clearance — safe in renal failure), bivalirudin, or fondaparinux.
  • Clopidogrel pharmacogenomics: prodrug activated by CYP2C19. Poor metabolizers → treatment failure (esp. East Asian populations); consider prasugrel or ticagrelor instead.
  • Ticagrelor adverse effects: distinctive dyspnea (~15%) and bradyarrhythmias (mechanism: ↑ adenosine).
  • Reye Syndrome: ASA + viral illness in children → encephalopathy + microvesicular hepatic steatosis. Avoid ASA in kids (use APAP/ibuprofen).
  • Aspirin-Exacerbated Respiratory Disease (Samter triad): asthma + nasal polyps + ASA/NSAID sensitivity (mechanism: ↑ leukotriene shunt).

Antiplatelets (Primary Hemostasis)

  • Aspirin: Irreversibly acetylates COX-1 → ↓ TXA₂ synthesis. Lasts life of platelet (7-10 days).
  • P2Y12 (ADP) Receptor Inhibitors: Prevent activation of GP IIb/IIIa. Clopidogrel & Prasugrel are irreversible prodrugs. Ticagrelor is reversible.
  • GP IIb/IIIa Inhibitors: Eptifibatide, Tirofiban. Block the final common pathway (fibrinogen cross-linking). Used in PCI.

Fibrinolytics (Clot Busters)

  • tPA Analogs: Alteplase, Tenecteplase, Reteplase.
  • Mechanism: Bind fibrin and convert plasminogen to plasmin, which degrades the fibrin mesh.
  • Indications: Acute ischemic stroke (<4.5 hrs), STEMI (if PCI unavailable).
  • Adverse Effects: Severe hemorrhage. Contraindicated in prior intracranial hemorrhage, recent surgery, active bleeding.

Tenofovir, Emtricitabine, Lamivudine, Abacavir, Zidovudine

  • Mechanism: Competitively inhibit RT; lack 3'-OH group causing chain termination. Needs host phosphorylation.
  • Tenofovir (TDF vs TAF): TDF causes renal toxicity (specifically Fanconi syndrome — proximal tubulopathy, phosphate wasting) and bone loss. TAF is a prodrug with less toxicity.
  • Abacavir: HLA-B*5701 screening mandatory to prevent fatal hypersensitivity.
  • Zidovudine (AZT): classic NRTI for perinatal/PMTCT prophylaxis. AE: bone marrow suppression (anemia, neutropenia).
  • Class Tox: Mitochondrial toxicity (lactic acidosis, hepatic steatosis) — mostly older generation.

INSTIs (Integrase Inhibitors)

Bictegravir, Dolutegravir, Raltegravir

  • Mechanism: Blocks insertion of proviral DNA into host genome.
  • Features: Preferred base for initial ART. High barrier to resistance. Well tolerated (some weight gain, headache).

NNRTIs (Non-Nucleoside RT Inhibitors)

Efavirenz, Rilpivirine

  • Mechanism: Allosteric inhibitors of RT. Do not require phosphorylation. Low barrier to resistance.
  • Tox: Rash — including SJS/TEN and DRESS (especially nevirapine, also efavirenz). Efavirenz: Neuropsychiatric effects (vivid dreams, depression). CYP inducers/inhibitors.

Protease Inhibitors (PIs)

Darunavir, Atazanavir

  • Mechanism: Prevent cleavage of gag/pol polyproteins → immature, noninfectious virions.
  • "Boosting": Always given with Ritonavir or Cobicistat (strong CYP3A4 inhibitors) to increase PI levels.
  • Tox: Metabolic syndrome (hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, lipodystrophy).

Entry / Fusion Inhibitors & Pharmacokinetic Boosters

  • Enfuvirtide (T-20): Binds gp41 → blocks viral-host membrane fusion. SQ injection only. Reserved for salvage therapy. AE: injection site reactions.
  • Maraviroc: CCR5 antagonist → blocks viral entry via CCR5 co-receptor. Must perform tropism assay first (only works on R5-tropic virus). AE: hepatotoxicity, cough.
  • Ibalizumab: Monoclonal Ab against CD4. Post-attachment inhibitor. IV, used in multidrug-resistant HIV.
  • Ritonavir / Cobicistat: CYP3A4 inhibitors used as pharmacokinetic boosters (increase levels of PIs and some INSTIs). Ritonavir itself is a PI but used at sub-therapeutic doses for boosting.
  • Cabotegravir + Rilpivirine (Cabenuva): long-acting IM injection q1–2 months as maintenance therapy in virologically suppressed patients — adherence aid. Cabotegravir alone is also approved for injectable PrEP.

📋 Lec 167 & 169 — HIV, Retroviruses, & Opportunistic Infections

HIV Virology & Pathogenesis

Viral Structure & Genes
  • Diploid: Contains two copies of positive-sense ssRNA.
  • env gene: Cleaved into gp120 (attachment to CD4) and gp41 (fusion/entry).
  • gag gene: Core structural proteins (p24 capsid, p17 matrix).
  • pol gene: Reverse transcriptase, integrase, protease.
Disease Course
  • Acute Retroviral Syndrome: 2-4 wks post-exposure. Mononucleosis-like illness (fever, rash, adenopathy). HIGH viral load.
  • Latent Phase: Asymptomatic, but progressive drop in CD4.
  • AIDS: CD4 < 200 or presence of an AIDS-defining illness.

High-Yield Complications & Details

  • Measles → SSPE: subacute sclerosing panencephalitis; virus latent in CNS, reactivates 7–10 yrs later → neurodegeneration. Fatal.

  • Herpetic Whitlow: HSV on a finger (occupational hazard for dentists, healthcare workers).
  • Herpetic Gladiatorum: HSV outbreaks in wrestlers/contact athletes (skin-to-skin).
  • Forschheimer spots: petechiae on the soft palate in rubella — the rubella enanthem (parallel to Koplik spots in measles).
  • Reye Syndrome: aspirin + viral illness (varicella, influenza) → encephalopathy + microvesicular hepatic steatosis. Don't give ASA to kids with viral illness.

  • Tx: retinoids, methotrexate, biologics

Hereditary Angioedema (HAE)
  • C1-esterase inhibitor deficiency → unopposed bradykinin
  • Recurrent non-pruritic facial / laryngeal / bowel-wall swelling (abdominal pain mimicking acute abdomen)
  • NO urticaria; NO response to epi/antihistamines/steroids

DRESS Syndrome
  • Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia & Systemic Symptoms
  • Onset 2–8 weeks after starting drug (longer latency than SJS)

  • Hepatitis, nephritis, pneumonitis, myocarditis

Gynecological History Framework

  • Menarche: age at first menstruation (normal: 9–16 yrs; avg ~12). Precocious if <8
  • Menstrual Cycle: ask LMP (first day of last menstrual period), cycle length (normal 21–35d), duration (2–7d), flow (quantify by pads/tampons per day/hour)
  • Abnormal Bleeding: intermenstrual bleeding, postcoital bleeding, postmenopausal bleeding (always workup for endometrial cancer)
  • Dysmenorrhea: painful menses — primary (no pathology, prostaglandin-mediated) vs secondary (endometriosis, fibroids)
  • Menopause: 1 year without menses. Symptoms: hot flashes, night sweats, vaginal dryness, mood changes, sleep disturbance
  • Breast History: lumps/masses, skin changes, nipple discharge, self-breast exam timing (best 1 week after menses)

Obstetric History & Screening

  • Gravida/Para (GTPAL): G = total pregnancies; T = term (≥37 wk); P = preterm (20–36 wk); A = abortions/miscarriages; L = living children
  • Each pregnancy: year, gestational age at delivery, type (vaginal/C-section), complications (preeclampsia, GDM, preterm labor), birth weight
  • Pap Smear: screens for cervical cancer (HPV-related dysplasia). Start age 21; q3yr ages 21–29; q5yr with HPV co-test ages 30–65. Abnormal → colposcopy
  • Mammogram: screens for breast cancer. Guidelines vary — generally start age 40–50; q1–2yr. Ask about prior results, biopsies, family hx
  • Contraception: current method, satisfaction, side effects, future fertility desires
  • STI History: non-judgmental, ask about partners, protection (barrier use), prior STI testing/diagnoses
GTPAL Example: G3P1011 = 3 pregnancies, 1 term delivery, 0 preterm, 1 abortion, 1 living child.

📋 Lec 170 — Behavioral Medicine: Components of the Patient Encounter

Mental Health Epidemiology & Suicide

  • 1 in 5 adults experience mental illness annually (~23% post-COVID); >50% receive no treatment
  • Anxiety disorders = most common mental illness (~19%). Depression = leading cause of disability worldwide
  • LGBTQ+ population: ~44% prevalence of mental illness (highest demographic)
  • Suicide: 2nd–3rd leading cause of death ages 15–25. Tripled since 1950s. #1 risk factor = untreated depression
  • 90% of suicide victims have an underlying mental illness
  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: national emergency line for mental health crises
  • 50% of lifetime mental illness cases begin by age 14; 75% by age 24

Mental Status Exam (MSE)

  • Appearance: grooming, hygiene, dress, posture, eye contact
  • Behavior: psychomotor agitation/retardation, cooperative, guarded
  • Speech: rate (pressured, slow), volume, rhythm, latency
  • Mood: patient's subjective report ("I feel sad")
  • Affect: clinician's objective observation — flat, blunted, labile, congruent with mood?
  • Thought Process: logical/linear, tangential, circumstantial, loose associations, flight of ideas
  • Thought Content: delusions, obsessions, suicidal/homicidal ideation, paranoia
  • Perceptions: hallucinations (auditory, visual, tactile), illusions
  • Cognition: orientation (person/place/time/situation), attention (serial 7s), memory (3-word recall)
  • Insight & Judgment: awareness of illness; decision-making capacity

Screening Tools & Documentation

  • PHQ-9: depression screening (9 items, score 0–27). ≥10 = moderate depression. Includes SI question (#9)
  • GAD-7: anxiety screening (7 items). ≥10 = moderate anxiety
  • Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS): structured suicide risk assessment
  • CAGE: alcohol use screening — Cut down, Annoyed, Guilty, Eye-opener. ≥2 = positive
  • SOAP Note (Behavioral): S = chief complaint + HPI + screening scores; O = MSE + vitals; A = DSM-5 diagnosis; P = therapy, medications, safety plan, follow-up
Always assess for safety: every behavioral health encounter must include screening for suicidal ideation, homicidal ideation, and access to lethal means.

Organic Mimics & Workup for Behavioral Symptoms

  • Always rule out medical/organic causes before attributing symptoms to a primary psychiatric diagnosis. Standard workup:
    • EKG (arrhythmia → anxiety/palpitations; baseline before QT-prolonging psych meds)
    • CBC (B12 deficiency → depression/cognitive change; anemia → fatigue)
    • CMP (electrolytes, liver, renal — hyponatremia → AMS; uremia → confusion)
    • TSH (hypothyroid → depression; hyperthyroid → anxiety)
    • Lipid panel (baseline before atypical antipsychotics)
    • UA (UTI → AMS in geriatrics — classic vignette)
  • Classic mimic pairs: hypothyroidism → depression; arrhythmia/pheochromocytoma → anxiety; UTI → delirium in elderly; B12 deficiency → cognitive impairment + neuropsych

Additional Screening & Suicide Drill-Down

  • PHQ-2 / GAD-2: ultra-brief 2-question pre-screens (positive → administer full PHQ-9 / GAD-7)
  • Suicide-attempt drill-down: method used, how was patient discovered, feelings when help arrived, current SI/plan/intent/access to means
  • Self-harm beyond SI: skin-picking, hair-pulling (BFRBs), cutting, burning, substance abuse — all warrant assessment

Osteopathic Approach to the Behavioral Patient

  • Suboccipital release: ↓ sympathetic tone via vagal modulation — useful for anxiety, tension headache
  • Rib raising: normalizes sympathetic tone (T1–T12) — useful for anxiety, pain, GI/respiratory symptoms tied to stress
  • Both are gentle, low-risk indirect techniques compatible with most psychiatric patients

  • Scale: flaking dead epidermis (psoriasis, dermatitis)
  • Crust: dried serum, blood, or pus on surface (impetigo = honey-colored crust)
  • Eschar: thick black/brown crust indicating necrosis (anthrax, burns, pressure ulcer)

  • Fissure: linear crack in skin (athlete's foot, anal fissure)
  • Excoriation: scratch mark — linear erosion from patient scratching
  • Lichenification: thickened, leathery skin from chronic rubbing (atopic dermatitis)
  • Atrophy: thinning of skin (topical steroid overuse)

  • Alopecia: cicatricial (scarring — follicles destroyed; no orifices) vs non-cicatricial (orifices preserved). Hair pull test: grasp ~50 hairs, gently pull — >6 extracted = active loss.
  • Exclamation point hairs: alopecia areata (hairs taper at base)
  • Hirsutism vs Hypertrichosis: hirsutism = androgen-pattern hair in females (PCOS, CAH); hypertrichosis = excess hair in non-androgenic distribution
  • Nail terminology: pitting (psoriasis, alopecia areata), onycholysis (separation from nail bed — psoriasis, hyperthyroid), leukonychia (white discoloration), onychorrhexis (longitudinal ridging), trachyonychia (rough)

Dermatologic History & Distribution Patterns

  • Distribution clues: sun-exposed (AK, SCC, lupus), extensor (psoriasis), flexural (eczema), intertriginous (candida, inverse psoriasis), dermatomal (zoster)

  • Palms & Soles involvement: secondary syphilis, RMSF, hand-foot-mouth, erythema multiforme
  • History framework: onset/duration, acute vs chronic, constant vs intermittent, triggers (new products, travel, sun, occupational), associated symptoms (pruritus, pain, fever, arthralgias), sick contacts, prior treatments tried
  • Palpation is essential: must touch the lesion to distinguish flat vs raised, smooth vs rough, mobile vs fixed

📋 Lec 160 / Lab 28 — Muscle Energy Technique (MET) for Lower Extremity

MET Principles (Review)

  • MET = Direct technique: the physician engages the restrictive barrier (moves TOWARD the barrier, away from freedom)
  • Patient force: 10–25% of maximum strength (isometric contraction)
  • Hold: 3–5 seconds per contraction
  • Relax: patient fully relaxes between contractions; re-engage the new barrier
  • Repetitions: 3–5 reps; may finish with a gentle passive stretch
  • Isometric: physician and patient apply equal and opposite force — no movement occurs
Key concept: the patient pushes toward their freedom (the direction of ease) while the physician resists. After relaxation, the physician moves the joint further into the barrier.

📋 Lec 183b — Trigger Points

Trigger Point Diagnosis

  • Definition: a hyper-irritable spot within a taut band of skeletal muscle → produces a referred pain pattern (pain is felt distant from the trigger point itself)
  • 3 Required Criteria: (1) Tender point, (2) within a taut band, (3) produces a specific referred pain pattern
  • Confirmatory signs (NOT required): Jump sign (patient withdrawal), local twitch response (transient contraction of taut band on palpation), cool skin overlying trigger point (↓ blood flow from fascial restriction)
  • Histology: contracted sarcomeres in the trigger point knot, with elongated sarcomeres on either side compensating
  • Imaging: ultrasound can visualize taut bands (echogenic area within muscle); NOT required for diagnosis
  • Multiple contracture knots can exist within a single trigger point — important for needle treatment (must move needle to break up all knots)
  • Trigger points are a component of Myofascial Pain Syndrome

Trigger Point Treatment

  • OMT Approaches: direct pressure (ischemic compression), spray & stretch (vapocoolant + passive stretch), MET, counterstrain
  • Dry Needling: insert needle into trigger point → elicit local twitch response → move needle to break up multiple contracture knots. Ultrasound guidance near delicate neurovascular structures.
  • Injection: local anesthetic (lidocaine) or saline into trigger point; similar mechanism to dry needling
  • Prevention: address perpetuating factors — posture, ergonomics, nutritional deficiencies (B12, iron, vitamin D), stress, sleep
