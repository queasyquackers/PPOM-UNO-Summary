📋 Lec 98 — CNS Viral Infections

How CNS Infections Occur

  • Hematogenous: inoculation at another site → blood → choroid plexus → CSF
  • Direct spread: respiratory epithelium (droplets); animal/bug bites; transplacental
  • Damage mechanisms: direct tissue damage, ↑ intracranial pressure, cytokine/chemokine inflammation, edema, brainstem herniation
  • Meningitis = meninges only; Encephalitis = parenchyma involved; Myelitis = spinal cord

Common Viral CNS Etiologies

  • Enteroviruses (85% viral meningitis): Coxsackievirus A&B, Echovirus — fecal-oral/respiratory; supportive Tx
  • Arboviruses (5%): transmitted by arthropod vectors (ticks/mosquitoes); seasonal — peak late summer/fall (vector season); named for initial isolation site but modern distribution much wider
    • EEE (Eastern Equine Encephalitis): Aedes/Coquillettidia mosquitoes; New England + Atlantic seaboard; highest mortality of all US arboviruses (~30–70%); severe neurological sequelae in survivors; very rare but devastating
    • WEE (Western Equine Encephalitis): western US; lower mortality than EEE; children most affected
    • West Nile Virus: Culex mosquito (amplified in birds → humans dead-end host); most common arboviral encephalitis in US; majority asymptomatic (80%) or West Nile fever (20%); neuroinvasive disease (<1%): encephalitis, meningitis, acute flaccid paralysis (anterior horn cells); risk: elderly + immunocompromised; Dx: serology (IgM in CSF/serum); no specific Tx
    • LaCrosse encephalitis: Aedes triseriatus; upper Midwest; children; seizures common; low mortality
    • St. Louis encephalitis: Culex mosquito; elderly; urban/suburban areas
    • All arboviruses: supportive Tx; reportable diseases
  • Herpesviruses: HSV-1 (temporal lobe encephalitis), HSV-2 (aseptic meningitis), VZV, EBV, CMV → acyclovir
  • JC virus: PML in immunocompromised; white matter lesions; no effective Tx
  • Rabies: bite → retrograde transport; Negri bodies; fatal without prophylaxis
  • Mumps: 20–40% meningitis in unvaccinated countries → vaccinate!
  • LCMV (Lymphocytic Choriomeningitis Virus): arenavirus; rodent exposure (mice, hamsters); aseptic meningitis + flu-like illness; teratogenic (fetal hydrocephalus, chorioretinitis) → avoid rodent contact in pregnancy; Dx: serology; Tx: supportive
  • Poliovirus: picornaviridae, +ssRNA; tropism for motor neurons of spinal cord + medulla → flaccid paralysis (LMN); sIgA critical for mucosal immunity; prevented by IPV/OPV vaccine
  • Influenza CNS: rare encephalitis/encephalopathy; treat with neuraminidase inhibitors — Oseltamivir (oral), Zanamivir (inhaled)
  • Coxsackievirus (enterovirus): stable in harsh environments — can survive chlorinated pools, GI tract, sewer systems; etiologic agent of hand-foot-mouth disease + herpangina; causes acute viral meningitis (self-limited unless encephalitis or child <1y); Dx: NAA (nucleic acid amplification)
How Viruses Reach CNS — Neurotropism Patterns: Poliovirus → motor neurons of spinal cord + medulla · Mumps → ependymal cells lining fetal ventricles · Rabies → enters distal peripheral nerve terminals → axoplasmic retrograde transport to sensory ganglia + spinal cord · HSV-1 (oropharyngeal) / HSV-2 (genital mucosa) → trigeminal or sacral ganglia → latent in sensory neurons → reactivation = cold sores/genital herpes · VZV primary infection → skin → sensory nerves → latent in sensory ganglia neurons → reactivation = shingles · Rabies + some arboviruses → can enter via olfactory nerve endings if high viral concentration

HSV Encephalitis — Must Know

  • Most common sporadic fatal encephalitis; HSV-1 reactivation from trigeminal ganglion
  • Temporal lobe tropism → personality change, memory loss, seizures, focal deficits
  • CSF: lymphocytic pleocytosis + RBCs (hemorrhagic!) + ↑ protein; PCR for HSV DNA = gold standard
  • Treatment: IV Acyclovir — start empirically, DON'T wait for results!
  • Imaging: MRI shows temporal lobe signal abnormality

HIV CNS Complications

  • HIV encephalopathy / AIDS dementia complex: direct HIV CNS infection
  • PML: JC virus reactivation; white matter; focal neuro deficits; CD4 <200
  • Toxoplasma encephalitis: ring-enhancing lesions on CT/MRI; CD4 <100; prophylaxis with TMP-SMX when CD4 <100
  • Cryptococcal meningitis: CD4 <100; India ink; Cryptococcal Ag; Tx: AmB + flucytosine → fluconazole maintenance

Key Bacterial Pathogens

  • N. meningitidis: Gram− diplococcus; droplet spread; petechial/purpuric rash = meningococcemia = DIC emergency; Waterhouse-Friderichsen = bilateral adrenal hemorrhage; Prophylax contacts: rifampin or cipro
  • S. pneumoniae: #1 adult meningitis; Gram+ diplococcus; most common overall; + Gram stain of blood/CSF
  • H. influenzae: now rare with Hib vaccine; 3rd gen cephalosporin
  • Listeria: elderly, pregnancy, immunocomp; cold-loving; ampicillin (cephalosporins FAIL here)
  • Brain abscess: S. aureus, anaerobes, streptococci; ring-enhancing on CT/MRI; Tx: drainage + Abx

Virulence Factors & Tissue Damage

  • Capsules (sialic acid) → resist phagocytosis; adherence to meninges
  • IgA protease → evade mucosal immunity
  • Hemolysins, complement evasion (low complement in CSF)
  • Cell death from toxins + lysis → cytokine release → capillary permeability → edema → ↑ ICP → herniation
  • Clinical triad: fever + stiff neck + ↓ mental status (focal or global CNS dysfunction)
📋 Lec 100 — CNS Fungal & Parasitic Infections

Fungal CNS Infections

  • Cryptococcus neoformans: encapsulated yeast; pigeon droppings; subacute meningoencephalitis in HIV (CD4 <100); India ink shows capsule halo; Crypto Ag latex agglutination (most sensitive); Tx: AmB + flucytosine → fluconazole maintenance
  • Candida: severely immunosuppressed; meningitis or brain abscess
  • Aspergillus: angioinvasive → hemorrhagic CNS infarcts
  • Coccidioides: SW US/San Joaquin Valley; bats; eosinophilic meningitis
  • Histoplasma: Ohio/Mississippi River Valley; birds/bats; CNS rare; Tx: AmB
  • Chronic meningitis: meningeal inflammation + CSF pleocytosis >4 weeks

CNS Parasites

  • Toxoplasma gondii: cats + undercooked meat; ring-enhancing lesions; CD4 <100 in HIV; prophylax with TMP-SMX; Tx: pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine + leucovorin
  • Neurocysticercosis (T. solium): pork tapeworm larvae; calcified cysts → seizures; most common cause of acquired seizures worldwide; Tx: praziquantel + albendazole + steroids
  • Naegleria fowleri: warm freshwater; "brain-eating amoeba"; PAM (primary amoebic meningoencephalitis); rapid and fatal; Tx: AmB (rarely successful)
  • Cerebral malaria: P. falciparum; altered consciousness; Tx: IV artesunate
  • Balamuthia mandrillaris: soil protozoan (Central America + Southwest US); granulomatous amoebic encephalitis (GAE) — subacute/chronic; affects immunocompromised AND immunocompetent; skin lesions may precede CNS; Tx: no proven therapy (experimental combinations — miltefosine + other agents)
  • Baylisascaris procyonis (raccoon roundworm): helminthic CNS infection; ingestion of eggs from raccoon feces; eosinophilic meningoencephalitis; devastating CNS damage especially in children; no effective treatment once symptomatic
  • Gnathostoma spp. (roundworm): eating undercooked/raw freshwater fish or poultry; Southeast Asia endemic; eosinophilic meningitis + painful migratory swellings; Tx: albendazole or ivermectin
  • Epidemiology matters: geographic exposure, immunosuppression, travel history all guide differential

Antifungal Drugs for CNS — Lec 102

Amphotericin B — broad spectrum, fungicidal
MOA: binds ergosterol in fungal membrane → pores → cell lysis. Can give intrathecal/intraventricular. AE: "shake and bake" infusion reaction (pretreat with COX inhibitors), renal tubule toxicity (wastes Na, Ca, K, Mg → electrolyte monitoring essential!). Use liposomal prep to ↓ toxicity. Resistance: ↓ ergosterol.
Azoles (Fluconazole, Voriconazole)
MOA: inhibit 14α-demethylase → ↓ ergosterol synthesis; fungistatic. Fluconazole: excellent CSF penetration → Crypto maintenance. Voriconazole: Aspergillus first-line. AE: ↑ liver enzymes. Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors → massive drug interaction risk (CNIs, statins, warfarin).
Flucytosine (5-FC)
Converted to 5-FU → inhibits DNA synthesis; pyrimidine analog; fungicidal. ALWAYS used WITH amphotericin (synergy + prevents resistance) for Crypto/Candida. AE: bone marrow suppression — monitor CBC.
CSF cheat code: Bacterial = turbid + ↓glucose + ↑↑protein + PMNs · Viral = clear + normal glucose + slightly ↑protein + lymphocytes · Fungal = like viral but add India ink + Crypto Ag · Granular IF = immune complex (PSGN) · Linear IF = anti-GBM (Goodpasture's)

📋 Lec 103 — Pathology of Skeletal Muscle

Normal Muscle & Reactions

  • Type I (slow/red): high mitochondria; oxidative phosphorylation; fatigue resistant; endurance (soleus, paraspinal)
  • Type II (fast/white): low mitochondria; anaerobic glycolysis; fatigues quickly; sprinting/lifting
  • Myopathic injury histo: central nuclei, pale necrotic cytoplasm, regenerating fibers = basophilic
  • Neurogenic injury histo: grouped angular atrophy (whole motor units denervated)
  • CK released from damaged myocytes → ↑ serum CK in any muscle disease
  • Sarcomere: Z-Z; I band = actin alone; A band = myosin + actin overlap; H band = myosin alone

Malignant Hyperthermia & Congenital Myopathies

  • Malignant Hyperthermia: AD; RYR1 mutation → uncontrolled Ca²⁺ from SR; triggered by succinylcholine + halogenated anesthetics (halothane); hyperthermia >106°F, tetany, tachycardia; Tx: Dantrolene (ryanodine receptor antagonist)
  • Central Core Disease: AD; RYR1; hypotonia at birth; MH risk; scoliosis; central zones on NADH-TR stain
  • Nemaline Myopathy: AD/AR; thin filament mutations; floppy baby; nemaline rods (H&E) in Type I fibers
  • McArdle (Type V glycogenosis): AR; myophosphorylase def; exercise cramps + myoglobinuria; flat venous lactate curve with exercise; normal blood glucose (liver unaffected)
  • Pompe (Type II glycogenosis): AR; acid maltase def; lysosomal glycogen; cardiomegaly + hypotonia; Tx: enzyme replacement
  • MERRF: maternal inheritance; MT-TK mutation; myoclonus + seizures + CPEO; ragged red fibers (modified Gomori trichrome)
  • CPT II: most common lipid myopathy; FFA transport defect; episodic with exercise/fasting

Ion Channel Myopathies (Channelopathies)

  • Overview: inherited mutations in skeletal muscle ion channels → abnormal membrane excitability; present with episodic weakness or myotonia
  • Hyperkalemic Periodic Paralysis: AD; SCN4A (Na⁺ channel gain-of-function); triggered by rest after exercise, fasting, K⁺ rich foods; episodes of flaccid weakness (minutes to hours); ↑ serum K⁺ during attack; Tx: glucose + insulin during attack; acetazolamide for prophylaxis
  • Hypokalemic Periodic Paralysis: AD; CACNA1S (Ca²⁺ channel, type 1) or SCN4A; triggered by high-carb meals, rest after exercise, cold; ↓ serum K⁺ during attack; Tx: K⁺ supplementation; acetazolamide or dichlorphenamide for prophylaxis
  • Myotonia Congenita: CLCN1 (Cl⁻ channel); AD (Thomsen) or AR (Becker); sustained muscle contraction after voluntary movement; improves with repeated use (warm-up phenomenon); no weakness; Tx: mexiletine (Na⁺ channel blocker)
  • Paramyotonia Congenita: AD; SCN4A; myotonia worsens with cold + repeated exercise (opposite of myotonia congenita warm-up); associated with episodic weakness
  • Key Exam Point: Periodic paralyses = flaccid weakness + normal CK between attacks; check K⁺ during episode to differentiate hyper vs hypo forms

Mitochondrial Myopathies

  • Overview: rare disorders caused by mutations of mitochondrial DNA → maternal inheritance only (mitochondria transmitted via ovum); all children of affected females show signs but with varied expressivity (heteroplasmy); ↓ ATP generation affects tissues with high ATP demand: skeletal muscle, CNS, heart, EOM
  • Clinical: weakness (proximal > distal), exercise intolerance, ↑ CK + rhabdomyolysis; external ophthalmoplegia (EOM involvement common — ptosis + limited eye movement); cardiomyopathy; lactic acidosis; systemic multi-organ involvement
  • Biopsy: ragged red fibers on Gomori trichrome stain (accumulated abnormal mitochondria at sarcolemma)
  • MERRF (Myoclonic Epilepsy with Ragged Red Fibers): predominantly intermittent muscle twitches aggravated by action (progressive myoclonus + hiccups); seizures; ataxia; dementia; mitochondrial tRNA-Lys mutation
  • MELAS (Mitochondrial Encephalopathy, Lactic Acidosis, Stroke-like episodes): recurrent stroke-like episodes in young patients in non-vascular distributions; encephalopathy; lactic acidosis; seizures; short stature
  • Kearns-Sayre Syndrome: progressive external ophthalmoplegia + pigmentary retinopathy + cardiac conduction defects; onset before age 20
  • Tx: supportive; coenzyme Q10 + riboflavin + creatine may improve cellular respiration; clonazepam for myoclonus; anticonvulsants for seizures; avoid drugs that inhibit mitochondrial function (e.g., certain antibiotics at high doses)

NMJ Disorders — MG vs LEMS

Myasthenia Gravis
  • Post-synaptic AChR Abs (85%); anti-MuSK (10%)
  • Complement damages receptor → ↓ AChR
  • Weakness WORSENS with repeated use
  • EMG: decremental response
  • Thymus: thymoma (10%); thymic hyperplasia (30%)
  • Ptosis, diplopia prominent; reflexes spared
  • Tx: AChE inhibitors, plasmapheresis, IVIG, thymectomy, immunosuppressants
Lambert-Eaton (LEMS)
  • Pre-synaptic VGCC Abs → ↓ Ca²⁺ → ↓ ACh release
  • Weakness IMPROVES with use (facilitation)
  • EMG: incremental response (opposite of MG!)
  • 50% associated with small cell lung carcinoma
  • Hyporeflexia + autonomic symptoms (dry mouth, constipation)
  • More common males; bimodal peaks 35y + 60y
MG = Muscles Give up more with use (decremental). LEMS = Less Effort More Strength (incremental). LEMS → Look for Lung cancer. Myasthenia = thyMus. LEMS = smaLL ceLL Lung.
📋 Lec 104–105 — Pathology of Bone & Joints

Metabolic Bone Disease

  • Osteoporosis: T-score ≤ −2.5 on DEXA; postmeno: ↓estrogen → ↑IL-6/TNF → ↑RANKL → ↑osteoclasts; cortical 2%/yr, cancellous 9%/yr; normal bone quantity just decreased; histo: perforated/thinned trabeculae; Tx: Ca + VitD + bisphosphonates
  • Osteopetrosis: ↓ carbonic anhydrase II → can't make H⁺ → ↓ bone resorption; dense but FRACTURES easily (woven not lamellar); bone fills marrow → pancytopenia; X-ray: "bone in bone" + Erlenmeyer flask; Tx: BMT potentially curative
  • Osteomalacia/Rickets: ↓VitD → ↓Ca → ↑PTH → ↓PO₄; Looser's zones (adults); genu varum + rachitic rosary (kids); ↑ Alk Phos, ↓VitD, ↑PTH; Tx: high-dose VitD
  • Paget Disease: SQSTM1 mutations; ↑NF-κB → ↑osteoclasts; 3 phases: osteolytic → mixed → sclerotic; histo: mosaic/jigsaw cement lines; giant osteoclasts (100 nuclei!); complications: skull thickening → hearing loss; chalk-stick fractures; high-output HF; <1% sarcoma; Tx: calcitonin + bisphosphonates
  • Osteonecrosis (AVN): femoral head most common; medial circumflex femoral artery; causes: fracture, steroids, alcohol, sickle cell, Gaucher, radiation; empty lacunae; wedge-shaped subchondral infarct

OA vs RA — Full Comparison

OA Cellular Mechanism: Chondrocytes synthesize proteoglycans (PGs) + Type II collagen → in OA, PG degradation exceeds synthesis. Chondrocytes also produce MMPs (matrix metalloproteinases) → degrade Type II collagen network; normally counteracted by TIMPs (tissue inhibitors of MMPs) — in OA the MMP/TIMP balance tips toward destruction. Cytokines driving destruction: TNF-α + IL-1 (from chondrocytes + synovial cells) → stimulate proteases + ↑ MMP production; prostaglandins, nitric oxide, TGF-β also implicated. Result: exposed subchondral bone → eburnation (polishing) → sclerosis + cysts + osteophytes at joint margins.

Achondroplasia & Osteogenesis Imperfecta

  • Achondroplasia: AD; FGFR3 gain-of-function → inhibits endochondral ossification → disproportionately short proximal limbs (rhizomelic dwarfism); enlarged head with bulging forehead; normal trunk and intelligence; 85% sporadic (↑ paternal age)
  • Osteogenesis Imperfecta ("Brittle Bone Disease"): COL1A1/COL1A2 mutations → defective Type I collagen; multiple fractures with minimal trauma; blue sclerae; hearing loss; dental imperfections; Type 1 = mild childhood fractures; Type 2 = lethal (multiple intrauterine fractures)

Fractures & Osteomyelitis

  • Fracture repair stages: hematoma → granulation tissue (PDGF, TGF-β, FGF) → soft callus → bony callus (2–3 wks) → endochondral ossification → medullary cavity restoration
  • Osteomyelitis: Staph aureus 80–90% (#1!); sickle cell → Salmonella; hematogenous most common route in children
  • Acute: neutrophils → necrosis → periosteal lifting → subperiosteal abscess
  • Chronic: dead bone = sequestrum; new bone shell = involucrum; draining sinus = cloaca
  • X-ray: lytic focus surrounded by sclerosis

Other Bone Pathology

  • Fibrous Dysplasia: GNAS1 somatic mutation → curvilinear woven bone in fibrous stroma; no osteoblastic rimming; McCune-Albright = polyostotic + café-au-lait + precocious puberty + endocrine
  • Simple Bone Cyst: fluid-filled, fibrous-lined; <20y; near growth plate; not a neoplasm
  • Aneurysmal Bone Cyst: multiloculated blood-filled; adolescents; USP6 gene rearrangement; "eggshell" reactive bone; locally aggressive
  • Ganglion Cyst: most common soft tissue mass of hand/wrist; fluid-filled swelling from joint capsule or tendon sheath (NOT true cyst — no epithelial lining); dorsal wrist most common; transilluminates; Tx: observation (many resolve) → aspiration → surgical excision if symptomatic
  • Synovial Cyst: herniation of synovium through joint capsule; lined by synoviocytes; contains synovial fluid; associated with OA + RA; Baker's cyst (popliteal fossa) = synovial cyst behind knee; rupture mimics DVT (pseudothrombophlebitis)
  • Metastatic Bone Tumors: far more common than primary bone tumors; mechanism: direct extension, lymphatic, or hematogenous spread to red marrow (vertebrae, pelvis, proximal femur/humerus, ribs, skull — "axial skeleton"); most common primary sources = Breast, Lung, Thyroid, Renal, Prostate (BLT with Relish and Pickles); lytic lesions (kidney, lung, thyroid) vs. blastic (prostate, breast mixed); X-ray: moth-eaten/permeative pattern; path fracture is often presenting complaint; Tx: radiation, bisphosphonates, treat primary
  • Seronegative Spondyloarthropathies: RF negative; HLA-B27; sacroiliac; bony ankylosis (see Rheum tab)
  • Lyme Arthritis: Borrelia burgdorferi; Ixodes tick; bull's-eye rash; migratory arthritis (knees, shoulders); Dx: anti-Borrelia Ab; Tx: doxycycline/amoxicillin
📋 Lec 106 — Pathology of Soft Tissue

Adipocytic Tumors

  • Lipoma: most common soft tissue tumor in adults; benign; well-encapsulated mature adipocytes; subcutis proximal extremities; soft, mobile, painless; cured by excision
  • Liposarcoma (Malignant): most common sarcoma in adulthood; deep soft tissues/retroperitoneum; 50s–60s; three subtypes:
    • Well-differentiated: MDM2 amplification (chr 12q); atypical spindle cells among mature adipocytes; indolent
    • Myxoid: t(12;16) FUS-DDIT3; myxoid matrix + "chicken wire" arborizing capillaries + lipoblasts; intermediate
    • Pleomorphic: complex karyotype; aggressive, frequently metastasize
  • Lipoblast: scalloped nucleus = pathognomonic of liposarcoma!

Fibrous Proliferations & Tumors

  • Nodular Fasciitis: benign; self-limited; young adults; forearm; rapid growth over weeks; t(17;22) MYH9-USP6; histo: "tissue culture" appearance + extravasated RBCs + zonation; spontaneous regression
  • Superficial Fibromatoses: nodular plump spindle cells in fascicles; dense collagen; painless subcutaneous nodules:
    • Dupuytren contracture (palmar — flexion contracture)
    • Ledderhose disease (plantar)
    • Peyronie disease (penile)
  • Deep Fibromatosis (Desmoid Tumor): large (~10cm) infiltrative masses; teens–30s; females; APC or CTNNB1 (β-catenin) mutations → ↑Wnt signaling; does NOT metastasize but locally aggressive; bland fibroblasts in long fascicles; complete excision difficult
  • Fibrosarcoma: malignant; 40–55y; deep soft tissue; herringbone pattern histo; vimentin+; 50% recur; 25% metastasize

Rhabdomyosarcoma — Most Common STS Childhood

  • IHC: desmin+, myogenin+, S-100−
  • Embryonal (50%): primitive round/spindle cells in myxoid stroma; rhabdomyoblasts with cross-striations; sinuses, head/neck, GU tract; Sarcoma Botryoides = grape-like clusters in hollow organs (bladder, vagina) = cambium layer = best prognosis
  • Alveolar (20%): uniform round cells in alveolar pattern; t(2;13) PAX3-FOXO1 or t(1;13) PAX7-FOXO1; arms, legs, trunk; older children; worst prognosis
  • Pleomorphic (20%): adults 50+; large bizarre eosinophilic cells; often fatal

Synovial Sarcoma & UPS

  • Synovial Sarcoma (~10% STS): 20s–40s; deep soft tissue near large joints (knee/popliteal); t(X;18) SS18-SSX1/2/4; histo: biphasic (spindle cells + gland-like epithelioid structures) or monophasic; IHC: cytokeratin+ (unusual for sarcoma!); mets: lung + lymph nodes (unusual for sarcomas); 5-yr survival 50–60%
  • Undifferentiated Pleomorphic Sarcoma (UPS; formerly MFH): cannot classify by morphology/IHC/genetics; deep soft tissue extremities; 40s–60s; complex karyotype; 80% of all STS; sheets of large anaplastic cells + bizarre nuclei + atypical mitoses + necrosis; 30–50% metastasize; generally poor prognosis
📋 Lec 115 — Sports Medicine: Overuse Injuries

Overuse Injury Pathophysiology

  • Repetitive loading exceeds tissue repair capacity → accumulating micro-damage
  • Bone: stress reaction → stress fracture (tibia, metatarsals, femoral neck; seen in athletes + military); bone scan or MRI more sensitive than X-ray
  • Tendon: tendinosis = degenerative changes (NOT primarily inflammatory); collagen disorganization; vascularity changes; repetitive eccentric loading
  • Muscle: delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS); eccentric contractions; microtrauma
  • Diagnosis: clinical + imaging (US or MRI for tendons); X-ray for stress fractures
  • Treatment: RICE + activity modification + eccentric exercises + gradual RTP

Common Overuse Injuries

  • Rotator cuff tendinopathy: supraspinatus most common; impingement → tendinosis → partial/full tear; painful arc 70–120°; Neer's + Hawkins' tests
  • Lateral epicondylitis (Tennis elbow): ECRB tendon; grip pain; Cozen's/Mill's test
  • Medial epicondylitis (Golfer's elbow): FCR/PT tendons; medial epicondyle
  • UCL Injury (Elbow): ulnar collateral ligament — attaches medial epicondyle to sublime tubercle of ulna; 3 bundles (anterior = primary restraint to valgus, posterior, oblique); mechanism = repetitive valgus stress (throwing athletes — pitchers) or acute valgus force; presentation: medial elbow pain during late cocking/acceleration phase of throw; ↓ velocity; Milking Maneuver + Moving Valgus Stress Test positive; Tx: rest + PT; surgical reconstruction (Tommy John surgery) for high-level athletes with complete tears
  • Patellar tendinopathy: jumper's knee; inferior patellar pole; eccentric squats
  • IT Band Syndrome: lateral knee pain; runners; Ober's test positive
  • Plantar fasciitis: heel pain worst with first steps in morning; medial calcaneal tubercle; arch support + stretching
  • Spondylolysis: hyperextension stress fracture at pars interarticularis; gymnasts; scotty dog sign on oblique X-ray

📋 Lec 101 — Antiherpes Antiviral Agents

Herpesviridae Basics & Drug Classes

  • Linear dsDNA viruses; large genomes (75–200 proteins); establish LATENCY in ganglia
  • Drugs work during acute phase ONLY — not during latency
  • Most are nucleoside analogs (antimetabolites) → activated by viral kinases → inhibit viral DNA polymerase
  • Class PK: wide distribution including CSF; short plasma t½; renal excretion (GFR + active secretion via OAT); reduce dose in renal impairment!
  • Prodrugs (valacyclovir, famciclovir) dramatically improve oral bioavailability

Acyclovir / Valacyclovir / Famciclovir — Anti-HSV/VZV

  • Acyclovir = acyclic guanosine analog (lacks 3'-OH)
  • Activation: viral thymidine kinase → monophosphate; host kinases → triphosphate → accumulates in INFECTED cells (selectivity!)
  • MOA: (1) competes with dGTP at viral DNA polymerase; (2) incorporated into DNA → obligate chain termination (lacks 3'-OH so no further elongation)
  • Penciclovir (from famciclovir) HAS a 3'-OH → competitive inhibition only (NOT chain termination) but higher intracellular concentrations + longer intracellular t½
  • Spectrum: HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV (higher dose needed — less susceptible)
  • Resistance: mutations in viral thymidine kinase (UL23 gene) = most common; altered TK substrate specificity; cross-resistance: acyclovir + valacyclovir + famciclovir + ganciclovir (TK-deficient → use foscarnet)
  • Valacyclovir = L-valyl ester prodrug → bioavailability ~55%; Famciclovir → penciclovir BA ~75%

Anti-CMV Drugs: Ganciclovir, Valganciclovir, Foscarnet, Cidofovir

Ganciclovir / Valganciclovir ★ first-line CMV
CMV latency note: CMV establishes latency in hematopoietic progenitor cells (stem cells) + monocytes — explains why HSCT recipients are at high risk for reactivation. Activation: CMV UL97 kinase → monophosphate; host kinases → triphosphate. MOA: GCV-TP competitively inhibits viral deoxyGTP incorporation into DNA → terminates viral chain elongation. 100× more active than acyclovir vs CMV; 10× higher concentration in CMV-infected cells (selectivity). PK: Ganciclovir IV only (poor oral bioavailability); Valganciclovir = oral prodrug → esterase → ganciclovir (F ~60%); also intravitreal injection + ophthalmic gel forms. Distribution: widely distributed including CSF + intraocular tissues; protein binding 1–2%. Elimination: urine unchanged (glomerular filtration + secretion). t½ ~4h adult; intracellular t½ ~24h; significantly prolonged in renal failure (dose-adjust!). Therapeutic uses: CMV retinitis; CMV prophylaxis in allogeneic HSCT + solid organ transplant; valganciclovir = preferred oral form. Major AE: Bone marrow suppression (leukopenia/neutropenia — dose-limiting; monitor CBC!); suppression of spermatogenesis; carcinogenic + teratogenic (animal data). Resistance: UL97 gene mutations (major) → cidofovir/foscarnet cross-resistance possible.
Foscarnet — pyrophosphate analog
MOA: Reversible noncompetitive block of pyrophosphate binding site on viral DNA polymerase + RNA polymerase + HIV reverse transcriptase. Does NOT need viral kinase activation → works for acyclovir-resistant HSV/VZV (TK-deficient). IV only; deposits in bone. Major AE: Nephrotoxicity (most patients get some degree; ↑ Cr); electrolyte imbalances (chelates divalent cations: ↓Ca, ↓Mg, ↓K) → seizures (up to 10% of AIDS patients). Hydrate well; monitor electrolytes.
Cidofovir — acyclic cytidine monophosphate analog
Already phosphorylated → host kinases only (no viral kinase needed). Inhibits viral DNA pol + incorporation. IV with probenecid + saline prehydration (block OAT uptake into proximal tubule). Dose every 1–2 weeks (long intracellular t½). Major AE: Nephrotoxicity (proximal tubule — Fanconi syndrome, azotemia; deaths reported with as few as 1–2 doses); neutropenia; teratogenic/carcinogenic. Contraindicated with other nephrotoxic drugs.
Letermovir — CMV terminase complex inhibitor
Binds CMV pUL56 → blocks terminase complex (UL51/UL56/UL89) → prevents CMV DNA maturation, packaging, processing. Highly CMV-specific (no activity against other viruses). IV or oral. Prophylaxis in CMV-seropositive allogeneic HSCT recipients. Drug interactions: CYP3A4 inhibitor; cyclosporine ↑ bioavailability (dose adjustment needed). AE: nausea, edema, tachycardia.
Maribavir — pUL97 kinase inhibitor
MOA: inhibits CMV pUL97 (viral kinase) → blocks CMV DNA replication + nuclear egress of capsids. Unique: does NOT require viral TK activation; distinct mechanism from ganciclovir. Oral. Use: treatment of refractory/resistant CMV infection in transplant recipients (FDA approved 2021). Resistance: mutations in UL97 kinase domain. AE: dysgeusia (taste disturbance) — most common; nausea, diarrhea. Drug interaction: competes with ganciclovir for UL97 → do NOT combine (antagonistic).

📋 Lec 117–118 — Pharmacology of NSAIDs

Arachidonic Acid Pathway & COX Biochemistry

  • AA = 20-carbon omega-6 PUFA released from membrane phospholipids by PLA2 → COX-1 or COX-2 (bifunctional enzyme: cyclooxygenase + peroxidase sites) → PGH2 (common precursor) → specific prostanoid synthases → 5 active prostanoids: PGE2, PGF2α, PGD2, PGI2 (prostacyclin), TXA2
  • COX-1 (constitutive "housekeeping"): GI cytoprotection — PGE2/PGI2 → ↑ mucus + bicarb, ↓ acid, ↑ mucosal blood flow, ↑ epithelial repair; platelet TXA2 → aggregation + vasoconstriction; renal autoregulation (afferent arteriole dilation); maintains homeostatic functions
  • COX-2 (inducible): rapidly upregulated by inflammatory stimuli (IL-1, TNF, LPS) → mediates pain, fever, inflammation; ALSO constitutively expressed in kidneys (medullary + cortical PGE2/PGI2 → renal autoregulation) and vascular endothelium (PGI2 → vasodilation + platelet disaggregation)
  • Kinin system (2nd line innate): bradykinin → venular dilation + ↑ vascular permeability + pain sensitization; works with prostaglandins to amplify inflammation
  • Plasmin system (2nd line innate): dissolves fibrin, activates MMPs, promotes complement activation — all amplified at sites of inflammation

NSAID Class PK — All Drugs

  • Class PK: chemically heterogeneous weak acids (pKa 3–5) — except Nabumetone (nonacidic prodrug → converted to active acid metabolite); rapid/complete oral absorption not affected by food; onset analgesia 30–60 min; Tmax 1–4h
  • Distribution: ~99% albumin bound; cross BBB (CNS analgesia via ↓ central sensitization); accumulate in synovial fluid → effectiveness in arthritis; cross placenta
  • Metabolism: hepatic CYP3A + CYP2C families; glucuronidation; enterohepatic circulation (some)
  • Elimination: renal excretion of metabolites (glomerular filtration + tubular secretion); t½ varies widely — <6h (ibuprofen, ketorolac) to >10h (naproxen, meloxicam, celecoxib, piroxicam)

MOA — Direct vs Selective COX Inhibition

  • Aspirin ONLY — IRREVERSIBLE: covalently acetylates serine residue (Ser530 on COX-1, Ser516 on COX-2) in the active site → permanent blockade; platelets have no nucleus → cannot make new COX → effect lasts platelet lifespan (8–10 days); 1× daily low-dose dosing; first-pass effect in portal system → platelet COX-1 inactivated before reaching systemic circulation → does NOT inhibit endothelial PGI2 → net shift toward PGI2-mediated disaggregation + vasodilation = cardioprotective
  • Non-aspirin NSAIDs — REVERSIBLE: competitive inhibitors of COX active site; effect lasts drug half-life
  • COX-2 selective (coxibs — celecoxib): fit into COX-2 side pocket (Val523 vs Ile in COX-1) → selectively inhibit COX-2; spare COX-1 → ↓ GI toxicity; but ↑ CV risk — COX-2 inhibition ↓ PGI2 without blocking platelet TXA2 → unopposed TXA2 → ↑ platelet aggregation + vasoconstriction → ↑ MI/stroke
  • ⚠️ Ibuprofen/Naproxen DDI with low-dose aspirin: reversibly bind COX-1 in platelets FIRST → ASA cannot access serine → ASA hydrolyzed before it can act → platelet COX-1 NOT inhibited. Solution: take low-dose aspirin BEFORE the nsNSAID (30–60 min prior)

Aspirin — Full Profile

  • Low dose (81–325mg): antiplatelet only; irreversible COX-1 in portal system; used for MI/stroke prevention; antiplatelet effect lasts 8–10 days
  • High dose (650mg–4g/day): analgesic + antipyretic + anti-inflammatory; rarely used now due to GI/renal toxicity + tinnitus risk; tinnitus/hearing loss = sign of toxicity
  • Unique uses: closure of PDA (along with indomethacin); antiplatelet; Kawasaki disease (high dose acute → low dose maintenance); PGD2-mediated niacin flushing prevention
  • ⚠️ Reye Syndrome: aspirin use in children/adolescents with viral illness (influenza, varicella) → severe/fatal encephalopathy + liver dysfunction (fatty infiltration of liver + viscera) → NEVER give aspirin to children with febrile viral illness
  • Salicylate Toxicity (Salicylism): N/V, headache, dizziness, tinnitus (early) → tachypnea, hyperpnea, hyperthermia, diaphoresis, AMS, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema, seizure, coma. Classic acid-base: early → medullary stimulation → respiratory alkalosis → uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation → ↑ CO2 production → high anion gap metabolic acidosis (mixed picture). Tx: GI decontamination, sodium bicarb (alkalinize urine → trap ionized salicylate), correct metabolic abnormalities, hemodialysis if severe
  • AERD/NERD: aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease — COX-1 inhibition → AA shunts to 5-LOX → ↑ cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4/D4/E4) → bronchoconstriction + nasal polyps + chronic rhinosinusitis; Tx: montelukast (LTD4 antagonist) + zileuton (5-LOX inhibitor)

Acetaminophen (Paracetamol) — Not an NSAID

  • MOA: not fully elucidated — inhibits COX-2 in CNS (↓ central PGE2 → antipyretic + analgesia); does NOT bind COX active site the same way; lacks peripheral anti-inflammatory activity because high concentrations of lipid peroxidases at inflamed sites oxidize/inactivate it + does not inhibit neutrophil activation → NO anti-inflammatory effect; possible interaction with endogenous opioid, cannabinoid, serotonergic systems (antinociceptive)
  • PK: oral/rectal/IV; well absorbed; low plasma protein binding; t½ 2–3h adults (neonates 1–7h — immature UGT); analgesia lasts 4–6h; antipyretic effect 6h
  • Metabolism (critical): 80–90% → glucuronidation + sulfation → inactive metabolites (saturable!); 5–15% → CYP2E1 (+ CYP3A4) → NAPQI (toxic reactive intermediate) → normally detoxified by glutathione → mercapturic acid → renal excretion. Overdose: glucuronidation/sulfation pathways saturate → ↑ NAPQI → glutathione depleted → NAPQI binds hepatocyte proteins → centrilobular necrosis (zone 3 — highest CYP2E1)
  • Glutathione depletion risk factors: overdose, malnutrition, chronic alcohol use (↑ CYP2E1 + ↓ glutathione), fasting, obesity/DM/chronic liver disease, elderly, HIV, genetic variants in glutathione synthases
  • Antidote: N-acetylcysteine (NAC) — IV or oral; replenishes glutathione (acts as precursor) + enhances sulfate conjugation; death rare if given within 8 hours of overdose; use Rumack-Matthew nomogram (acetaminophen level vs. time since ingestion) to guide NAC
  • Toxicity dose: 15g+ → severe hepatotoxicity; max therapeutic dose = 4g/day adults (2–3g/day elderly or with hepatic disease/chronic EtOH use)
  • Advantages: NO GI ulceration; NO platelet effects; NO renal afferent arteriole effects (unlike NSAIDs); ✅ safe in pregnancy (all trimesters) + breastfeeding; approved pediatrics + elderly
  • Uses: fever, mild–moderate pain; moderate–severe pain as opioid adjunct (opioid-sparing); alternative when NSAIDs contraindicated (renal failure, GI bleed, CV disease, anticoagulation)
  • DDIs: CYP inducers (rifampin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, chronic EtOH) → ↑ NAPQI production → ↑ hepatotox risk; CYP inhibitors → ↓ NAPQI; warfarin + acetaminophen (chronic high dose) → ↑ INR (monitor)

NSAID Toxicities & Drug Interactions — Complete

  • GI (COX-1 inhibition): loss of PGE2/PGI2 cytoprotection → ↓ mucus/bicarb + ↑ acid + ↓ mucosal repair → gastric irritation, ulcers, GI bleeding, perforation; risk ↑ with age >65, glucocorticoids, anticoagulants, SSRIs, H. pylori, prior ulcer, higher doses; prevention: PPI + misoprostol; use COX-2 selective if GI risk; take with food
  • CV (COX-2 blockade): nsNSAIDs + coxibs → ↓ PGI2 → unopposed TXA2 → ↑ platelet aggregation + vasoconstriction → ↑ MI/stroke; also → HTN (↓ vasodilator PGs), fluid retention, edema, worsening CHF; aspirin low-dose = PROTECTIVE (portal COX-1 effect)
  • Renal: block PGE2/PGI2-mediated afferent arteriole vasodilation → ↓ GFR (esp. in volume-depleted/↓ cardiac output states where renal function is PG-dependent); salt/water retention → ↑ BP; hyperkalemia (↓ renin → ↓ aldosterone); acute papillary necrosis (chronic use); ↑ risk in: CKD, CHF, cirrhosis, volume depletion, diuretic use, elderly, RAS inhibitors
  • Pregnancy: avoid ≥20 weeks (fetal renal dysfunction → oligohydramnios → limb contractures); 3rd trimester → premature closure of PDA; indomethacin used as tocolytic <32 weeks (then stopped)
  • Drug interactions: ACEi/ARBs + NSAIDs → ↑ AKI (triple whammy with diuretic); diuretics + NSAIDs → ↓ diuretic efficacy + hyperkalemia; warfarin + NSAIDs → ↑ bleeding (GI bleed + ↓ platelet aggregation); SSRIs + NSAIDs → ↑ GI bleeding (↓ platelet serotonin); lithium → NSAIDs ↑ lithium levels (↓ renal clearance → toxicity); methotrexate → NSAIDs ↑ MTX levels (block OAT)
  • Contraindications: AERD/NERD; asthma with nasal polyposis; CABG perioperative; acute GI bleeding; severe renal/hepatic impairment; coagulation disorders; CHF; children with viral illness (aspirin); pregnancy ≥20 weeks; elderly (use with extreme caution)
GI protection strategy: If patient needs NSAID + high GI risk → use COX-2 selective (celecoxib) OR add PPI/misoprostol. If patient needs NSAID + cardiac protection → add low-dose ASA BEFORE nsNSAID. You can't fully escape the CV risk even with coxibs.
📋 Lec 130 — Pharmacology of Gout & Pseudogout

Acute Gout Treatment

  • Target: inflammation — DO NOT start urate-lowering therapy during acute attack (worsens it!)
  • NSAIDs (first line): especially indomethacin; most potent for acute gout
  • Colchicine: start within 24h! MOA: inhibits microtubule polymerization → blocks neutrophil migration + engulfment of crystals; AE: GI (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea — most common); myopathy (especially + statins); CYP3A4 inhibitors (clarithromycin, cyclosporine) ↑ colchicine toxicity → reduce dose
  • Glucocorticoids: if NSAIDs/colchicine contraindicated (renal failure, GI bleed)
  • Other agents: uricase (pegloticase), ACTH, IL-1 inhibitors (anakinra, canakinumab) — refractory cases

Urate-Lowering Therapy — Long-Term

  • Target uric acid: <6 mg/dL; initiate ULT only AFTER acute attack resolves (starting during acute attack worsens flare by mobilizing crystals); indication: ≥2 attacks/year, tophi, radiographic damage, uric acid stones
  • Allopurinol: XO inhibitor → ↓ uric acid production; first-line ULT; start at 100mg/day → titrate up; metabolized to oxypurinol (active, also inhibits XO); AE: rash → SJS/TEN in HLA-B*58:01 (screen Asian patients before starting!); dyspepsia, headache, diarrhea (mild, reversible); monitor LFTs, Scr, CBC; CRITICAL DDI: blocks azathioprine/6-MP metabolism (XO inactivates them) → ↑↑ toxic levels → reduce AZA dose 25–33% or switch to febuxostat
  • Febuxostat: same MOA as allopurinol (non-purine selective XO inhibitor); use if allopurinol intolerant or ineffective; dose: 40mg/day (can ↑ to 80mg); hepatic metabolism (safer in renal impairment than allopurinol); AE: ↑ LFTs, nausea, rash, MSK pain; Black Box Warning (2019): ↑ risk of CV death vs allopurinol in patients with established CV disease → use allopurinol first; same AZA/6-MP DDI as allopurinol — NEVER combine without dose reduction
  • Probenecid (uricosuric): competitively inhibits renal OAT transporter → ↓ uric acid reabsorption → ↑ renal uric acid excretion; NOT effective if eGFR <60 mL/min (requires adequate renal filtration); contraindicated with history of uric acid nephrolithiasis; AE: rash, precipitation of gouty arthritis during initiation, nephrolithiasis; inhibits renal OAT → also ↑ levels of penicillin + methotrexate (drug interactions!)
  • Pegloticase: IV recombinant uricase (PEGylated); converts uric acid → allantoin (easily excreted); last resort for refractory tophaceous gout; risk of infusion reactions + immunogenicity (anti-drug antibodies → loss of response)
Transition to ULT: Use anti-inflammatory prophylaxis (colchicine or NSAID) when starting ULT to prevent mobilization flares — keep going even if flare occurs during initiation. Duration: continue prophylaxis for ≥3–6 months after reaching target uric acid.

csDMARDs (Conventional Synthetic)

Methotrexate (MTX) ★ First-Line csDMARD
MOA: folate antagonist → blocks DHFR → ↓ de novo purine synthesis → inhibits T/B cell proliferation + inflammation. PK: oral/subQ/IM; hepatic metabolism; renal excretion (reduce dose in CKD). Toxicities: hepatotoxicity (monitor LFTs — no alcohol!), myelosuppression (CBC — watch WBC/platelets), mucositis, pulmonary toxicity (hypersensitivity pneumonitis), teratogenic (category X). Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) — Safest csDMARD
MOA: ↑ lysosomal/endosomal pH → disrupts antigen processing + presentation → ↓ TLR activation → anti-inflammatory. Uses: RA (mild-moderate), SLE (backbone — ↓ flares + mortality), Sjögren's, MCTD. AE: retinal toxicity (cumulative dose-dependent → bull's eye maculopathy → annual ophthalmology exam after 5 years use!); GI upset (take with food); rare: cardiomyopathy, QTc prolongation, blood dyscrasias. ✅ SAFE in pregnancy — continue throughout; safe in lactation. No myelosuppression. Slower onset (weeks–months).
Sulfasalazine
MOA: mesalamine (anti-inflammatory) + sulfapyridine (antibacterial) linked by azo bond → cleaved by colonic bacteria → sulfapyridine absorbed systemically. Uses: RA, spondyloarthropathies, IBD. AE: GI (N/V/D — take with food; enteric coated), sulfa allergy reactions (rash, Stevens-Johnson — do NOT use in sulfa allergy!), oligospermia (reversible — warn males!), hemolytic anemia (G6PD deficiency), headache, photosensitivity. Leflunomide
MOA: prodrug → active metabolite teriflunomide → blocks DHODH (dihydroorotate dehydrogenase) → ↓ de novo pyrimidine synthesis → ↓ T + B cell proliferation. Glucocorticoids — Bridge + Flare Management
MOA: GC-GCR complex → nucleus → transrepression (NF-κB, AP-1) or transactivation (GRE) → broad anti-inflammatory (↓ IL-1/IL-6/TNF, ↓ T cell proliferation, ↑ lipocortin → ↓ PLA2). Biologic Class-Wide Considerations

  • Pre-biologic screening (ALL biologics + JAK inhibitors): TB (IGRA preferred; if positive → treat latent TB for ≥4 weeks before starting); HBV (HBsAg + HBcAb — if positive → antiviral prophylaxis); HIV; hepatitis C; CBC + LFTs baseline; pregnancy test
  • Vaccines: give ALL indicated vaccines BEFORE starting immunosuppression; live vaccines (MMR, varicella, yellow fever, LAIV) are CONTRAINDICATED once on biologics/JAK inhibitors; killed/inactivated vaccines are OK but may have ↓ immunogenicity; yearly inactivated influenza + pneumococcal vaccines recommended
  • Monitoring on therapy: CBC + LFTs periodically; watch for infections (including opportunistic); lipid panel (esp. tocilizumab, JAK inhibitors); malignancy surveillance
  • Perioperative: hold biologics before elective surgery (timing depends on t½ — typically 4–5 half-lives); resume once wound healing confirmed + no infection
  • Switching biologics: inadequate response → switch within class OR to different mechanism; secondary failure (loss of response) → switch within class; primary failure → switch mechanism

Transplantation Immunology — Allorecognition

  • Core concept: transplantation introduces foreign tissue (persistent antigen source) → recipient immune system mounts response against donor HLA (MHC) molecules; graft survival requires active immunosuppression to override this
  • Direct allorecognition: recipient T cells recognize intact donor MHC + peptide on donor APCs (passenger leukocytes in graft); most important in acute rejection; high precursor frequency of alloreactive T cells (~1–10% of T cells); donor DCs migrate from graft → activate recipient T cells directly
  • Indirect allorecognition: recipient APCs process donor proteins (after cell death/graft turnover) → present donor-derived peptides on recipient MHC → slower, contributes more to chronic rejection; also drives antibody-mediated rejection via B cell activation
  • Both pathways activate recipient T cells → explain rejection mechanisms; direct = acute/early; indirect = chronic/late
  • Immune response sequence: Ag recognition → clonal expansion → differentiation → effector function → either tolerance (success) or rejection; regulation is active — balance is essential for graft survival
  • HLA matching: closer HLA match → ↓ alloreactivity → better long-term outcomes; cross-match test (recipient serum + donor lymphocytes) → preformed Abs = positive cross-match = contraindication to transplant (prevents hyperacute rejection)

Maintenance Immunosuppressants — Standard Triple: Tacrolimus + MMF + Prednisone

Calcineurin Inhibitors (CNIs) — Tacrolimus ★ (preferred) & Cyclosporine
MOA: Cyclosporine binds cyclophilin; Tacrolimus binds FKBP-12 → drug-immunophilin complex inhibits calcineurin → calcineurin cannot dephosphorylate NFAT → NFAT stays in cytoplasm → ↓ IL-2 transcription → ↓ T cell activation + proliferation. Same endpoint, different immunophilin. PK — Cyclosporine: oral (incomplete/variable absorption) or IV (solubilized in ethanol-polyoxyethylated castor oil vehicle); 90% bound to plasma lipoproteins; CYP3A4 + P-gp substrate; biliary/fecal excretion; t½ 10–27h. Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF) ★ preferred antimetabolite
PK: Oral or IV; MMF is prodrug → rapidly + completely hydrolyzed in liver to active MPA (mycophenolic acid); MPA bioavailability = 94%; enterohepatic circulation of MPA (biliary excretion → gut → reabsorption; cyclosporine blocks this!); hepatic glucuronidation → inactive MPAG excreted in urine; t½ ~18h. MOA: MPA = potent, selective, uncompetitive, reversible inhibitor of IMPDH (inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase) → blocks de novo guanosine nucleotide synthesis → selectively suppresses T + B cell proliferation (lymphocytes depend heavily on de novo pathway; other cells have salvage pathway). Uses: Prophylaxis of organ rejection (standard triple = CNI + MMF + GC); prophylaxis + treatment of GvHD; psoriasis, lupus nephritis, MG, other autoimmune diseases (off-label); MMF has superior efficacy + safety vs AZA → preferred for transplant rejection prophylaxis. AEs: Bone marrow suppression (anemia + leukopenia most common); persistent diarrhea/N/V (GI — dose-limiting); teratogenic — ↑ risk 1st trimester pregnancy loss + congenital malformations (avoid in pregnancy!). Sirolimus (Rapamycin) / Everolimus — mTOR Inhibitors

MOA: Binds FKBP-12 → drug-FKBP12-mTOR complex → prevents G1→S phase cell cycle progression → cell cycle arrest → inhibits T cell proliferation (blocks IL-2 signal transduction downstream); also inhibits cell survival + growth signaling. Transplant uses: Alternative for kidney transplant rejection prophylaxis (especially with CNI nephrotoxicity); acute + chronic GvHD (off-label); safety + efficacy in liver/lung transplants NOT established. Non-transplant uses: Drug-eluting coronary stents (antiproliferative → ↓ restenosis + cardiac events in severe CAD); malignant perivascular epithelioid cell tumor (PEComa — IV); tuberous sclerosis-associated facial fibroangioma (topical). AEs: Myelosuppression (thrombocytopenia most common); impaired wound healing (anti-angiogenic — avoid perioperatively!); hyperlipidemia (hypertriglyceridemia + ↑ LDL); pneumonitis (interstitial lung disease); edema; mouth sores. DDIs: CYP3A4/P-gp inducers/inhibitors (same as CNIs); avoid CNI combination → synergistic nephrotoxicity; sirolimus ↓ tacrolimus AUC ~30%; cyclosporine + sirolimus → ↑ HUS risk. Contraindications: Pregnancy; liver transplant; lung transplant (↑ bronchial anastomotic dehiscence).

PTLD — Post-Transplant Lymphoproliferative Disorder

  • B lymphocyte proliferation in setting of chronic T cell immunosuppression (↓ T cell surveillance)
  • EBV-positive B cell proliferation in most cases (90–95% adults have prior EBV infection)
  • Most common malignancy complicating solid organ transplant
  • Risk factors: degree of T cell immunosuppression + EBV seronegative status (especially with belatacept!)
  • Treatment: ↓ immunosuppression, rituximab, chemotherapy

Innate vs Adaptive + 1st/2nd Line Defense

Innate Immunity
  • Non-specific; no memory
  • FAST response (minutes-hours)
  • 1st + 2nd line
  • Cells: neutrophils, macrophages, NK cells, DCs, mast cells
Adaptive Immunity
  • Highly specific; HAS memory
  • SLOW response (days-weeks)
  • T cells + B cells
  • Interaction is artificial — overlap exists (macrophages bridge both)
  • 1st Line: skin (pH 5.5), mucous membranes, cilia, stomach acid (pH 1–3), lysozyme, defensins, cathelicidins, normal flora
  • 2nd Line: inflammation, fever, complement, interferons, phagocytosis, NK cells
  • Plasmin system (2nd line): plasmin dissolves fibrin clots, activates MMPs (tissue remodeling), promotes complement activation; plasminogen activated by tPA/uPA at sites of inflammation; amplifies innate response
  • Kinin system (2nd line): Hageman factor (XII) → activates kallikrein → cleaves kininogen → bradykinin → venular dilation + ↑ vascular permeability + pain sensitization (works synergistically with prostaglandins); explains why NSAIDs help with some pain
  • Cardinal signs of inflammation: Calor (heat), Dolor (pain), Rubor (redness), Tumor (swelling), loss of function
  • Coordinated by macrophages via IL-1 (pyrogen), TNF-α (inflammation master), IL-8 (attract neutrophils), IL-12 (attract NK cells)

Pattern Recognition Receptors (PRRs)

  • Recognize PAMPs (pathogen-associated molecular patterns: LPS, peptidoglycan) — coded in genome, identical in all individuals (no rearrangement needed)
  • TLRs (Toll-like receptors): cell surface + endosomes; activate NF-κB (master transcription factor of immune system) → pro-inflammatory genes; 10 types; TLR3 deficiency → ↑ susceptibility to HSV; TLR7 → ↑ viral load in HIV
  • NOD-like receptors (NLRs): intracytoplasmic; detect intracellular bacteria (PG); form the inflammasome → pyroptosis (inflammatory cell death); NOD polymorphisms → ↑ Crohn's disease, atopic eczema, ↑ IgE
  • CRP (C-reactive protein): secreted PRR; made by hepatocytes in response to IL-6; opsonizes pneumococci; activates classical complement; binds apoptotic/necrotic cells
  • MBL (mannose-binding lectin): secreted; activates lectin complement pathway

Phagocytosis & Fever & Interferons

  • Phagocytosis steps: attachment via TLR/Fc/complement receptors → recognition (CD47 "don't eat me" signal) → engulfment (phagosome) → lysosome fusion → digestion → Ag presentation
  • Neutrophils vs Macrophages: neutrophils = short-lived, acute, rapid oxygen radicals, granules (~50% defensins); macrophages = long-lived, chronic, better APCs, secrete IL-1/TNF-α/IL-8/IL-12, M1 (pro-inflammatory) vs M2 (anti-inflammatory/repair)
  • Pathogens evade phagocytosis: capsule; inhibit phagolysosome fusion (M. tuberculosis); escape phagosome (L. monocytogenes)
  • Fever: pyrogens (exogenous LPS; endogenous IL-1, IL-6) → ↑ hypothalamic set point; benefits: inhibits temp-sensitive microbes, ↓ iron availability for bacteria, ↑ immune reactions
  • Interferons: IFN-α (DCs) + IFN-β (fibroblasts) = innate antiviral; RNA-dependent protein kinase → blocks mRNA synthesis + apoptosis; IFN-γ = adaptive/Th1

  • ~16 serum proteins; ancient; 3 activation pathways:

MHC, TCR, BCR — Molecular Machinery

  • MHC I (HLA-A, B, C): all nucleated cells; presents intracellular Ags (viral/tumor via proteasome → TAP → ER); engages CD8+ cytotoxic T cells; absence of MHC-I → NK cells kill
  • MHC II (HLA-DR, DQ, DP): APCs only (DC, macrophages, B cells); presents extracellular Ags (endocytosis → endosome → CLIP removed by HLA-DM → antigen loads); engages CD4+ helper T cells; also expressed on activated T cells + thymic epithelium
  • MHC III: complement components (C2, C4, Factor B)
  • TCR: αβ or γδ heterodimer; associates with CD3 (signal transduction); MHC-restricted; stabilized by CD4 (binds MHC II) or CD8 (binds MHC I)
  • BCR: membrane-bound Ig; T-dependent Ags → class switching, affinity maturation, memory; T-independent (Ti-2) Ags = polysaccharide capsules → IgM only, no class switching (why conjugate vaccines work!)

T Cell Activation — 2-Signal Model

  • Signal 1: TCR-CD3 recognizes peptide-MHC (creates immunologic synapse)
  • Signal 2 (Costimulation): CD28 on T cell binds CD80/CD86 (B7) on APC
  • Signal 1 ONLY → ANERGY (functional inactivation = peripheral tolerance!)
  • Signal 1 + Signal 2 → FULL T CELL ACTIVATION → clonal expansion → differentiation
  • CTLA-4: immune checkpoint; upregulated on activated T cells; binds B7 with HIGHER affinity than CD28 → inhibitory signal → restrains activation → peripheral tolerance
  • Ipilimumab = anti-CTLA-4 → unleashes T cells → cancer immunotherapy
  • Abatacept = CTLA-4-Ig fusion → blocks costimulation → prevents rejection
  • CD8+ T cells: recognize Ag on MHC-I → IL-2R expression → Th1 help → cytotoxic effector → perforins + granzymes
  • B cell activation: BCR → endocytosis → MHC II → Th2 help (IL-4/IL-6) → plasma cells + memory

📋 Lec 126 — Mechanisms of Autoimmunity in Rheumatologic Disease

Tolerance & Autoimmunity

  • Tolerance = antigen-specific unresponsiveness; adaptive immune system learns to tolerate self-Ags + dietary proteins
  • Central tolerance: primary lymphoid organs; thymus (T cells) = positive selection (must recognize self-MHC) + negative selection (delete self-reactive); bone marrow (B cells) = receptor editing (alters specificity); <5% thymocytes survive → naive mature T cells
  • Peripheral tolerance: secondary lymphoid organs; mechanisms: clonal anergy (signal 1 without costimulation), immunologic ignorance, cytokine withdrawal, sequestration, Treg cells (FOXP3+), apoptosis
  • 30% of low-avidity self-reactive T cells escape to periphery → peripheral tolerance catches them

3 Stages of Autoimmune Disease & Mechanisms

  • Stage 1 — Priming: genetic risk (HLA genes) + environmental trigger (infection, UV light, drugs)
  • Stage 2 — Subclinical: organ slowly damaged; epitope spreading begins (immune system attacks more targets)
  • Stage 3 — Clinical: full symptoms + detectable autoantibodies

📋 Lec 140 — Anaphylaxis & Allergic Reactions

Diagnosing Anaphylaxis — 3 Clinical Criteria

  • Criterion 1: Acute onset mucocutaneous symptoms (flushing, pruritus, urticaria, angioedema) PLUS at least one of: wheezing/stridor/hypoxia OR hypotension/end-organ damage
  • Criterion 2: Two or more of following rapidly after exposure to allergen: mucocutaneous + respiratory + hypotension + severe GI symptoms
  • Criterion 3: Hypotension within minutes-hours after exposure to known allergen
  • Organ manifestations: Skin (flushing, urticaria, angioedema); Respiratory (rhinitis, stridor, wheezing, hypoxia); GI (N/V/D, crampy pain); ENT (uvular edema, tongue swelling); Neuro (headache, seizure, altered mentation); CV (chest pain, hypotension, tachycardia)
  • Common triggers: foods (nuts, shellfish, eggs), medications (penicillin, NSAIDs, contrast), insect stings, latex, vaccines (rare), idiopathic
  • Biphasic reaction: meets anaphylaxis criteria within 48h WITHOUT re-exposure; late phase (6h) = leukotrienes attracting eosinophils → observe ALL patients after initial treatment!

Anaphylaxis Treatment Protocol ⚠️

  • 1st — Epinephrine IM anterolateral thigh (vastus lateralis): 0.3–0.5mg adults; 0.01mg/kg peds (max 0.3mg prepubertal, 0.5mg adolescent) — ONLY evidence-proven life-saving medication! Nasal spray: 15–30kg = 1 spray (1mg); ≥30kg = 1 spray (2mg)
  • MOA: ↑ PVR (α1), ↑ CO (β1), reverses bronchoconstriction (β2), stabilizes mast cells, reduces mucosal edema
  • Assess + secure airway; CALL 9-1-1; all epi patients → ED (biphasic risk)
  • Remove exposure/allergen; IV crystalloid if hypotensive (20mL/kg bolus + Trendelenburg positioning)
  • Albuterol (nebulized) ONLY for refractory bronchospasm — does NOT replace epinephrine
  • 2nd Line Pharmacotherapy (adjuncts — do NOT delay epi for these):
    • H1 antihistamine: cetirizine (preferred 2nd gen) or diphenhydramine → for cutaneous symptoms (urticaria, pruritus); NOT life-saving; do NOT give instead of epi
    • H2 antihistamine: famotidine → ↓ vasodilation + urticaria (H2 receptors on mast cells + endothelium)
    • Corticosteroids: methylprednisolone or prednisolone/prednisone → anti-inflammatory; do NOT prevent biphasic reactions despite common belief; given after epi secured; slow onset (hours)
  • Refractory anaphylaxis: repeat epi q5–15 min; IV epinephrine infusion (0.01 mcg/kg/min); vasopressors for refractory hypotension; glucagon if on beta-blockers (bypasses β-receptor → ↑ cAMP directly → ↑ HR + CO); supplemental O2; ECMO team if needed
  • Disposition — when to observe 8–24h or admit: moderate-severe reaction (hypoxemia, airway edema, shock); required >1 dose of epinephrine; prolonged symptoms (>4 hours); history of severe wheezing/asthma; history of severe/biphasic reaction; ingested antigen with possibility of continued absorption (nuts, shellfish)
  • Discharge criteria: clinically stable ≥4h after last epi dose; prescribe EpiPen × 2 + written action plan + allergen avoidance education + allergist referral

📋 Lec 141–142 — Lymphocyte Activation, Regulation & Tolerance

MHC Antigen Presentation — Deep Dive (Hickey)

  • MHC I processing: intracellular proteins → proteasome → TAP transport to ER → loads onto heavy chain + β2-microglobulin → surface display → CD8+ T cells; absence of MHC-I = NK cells kill (missing self hypothesis)
  • MHC II processing: extracellular Ags → endocytosis → invariant chain blocks premature loading → degraded in endosomes → CLIP placeholder → HLA-DM removes CLIP → peptide loads → CD4+ T cells
  • 2-Signal Model: Signal 1 (TCR-CD3 + peptide-MHC) + Signal 2 (CD28 + B7/CD80/CD86) = full activation. Signal 1 alone = ANERGY (peripheral tolerance). Costimulation confirms REAL danger, not harmless antigen
  • CTLA-4: upregulated on activated T cells; binds B7 with HIGHER affinity than CD28 → brakes activation; exploited by belatacept (fusion protein blocks costimulation → prevents rejection) and ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4 → unleashes T cells → cancer immunotherapy)
  • Superantigens: bypass normal processing; bind OUTSIDE peptide groove of MHC-II AND TCR simultaneously → activate 20–30% of ALL T cells at once → cytokine storm → TSST (S. aureus), Strep TSS. The immune restrictions exist for safety — superantigens show what happens when they're bypassed

Clonal Expansion → Contraction → Memory (Lec 142 · Hickey)

  • Full adaptive response sequence: Antigen Recognition → Clonal Expansion → Differentiation → Effector Function → Contraction → Memory
  • Clonal expansion: antigen-specific cells are RARE → must expand massively; requires antigen recognition + costimulation + cytokines; metabolic reprogramming (↑ glucose + biosynthesis); CD8 → cytotoxic effectors; CD4 → Th1/Th2/Th17 subsets
  • Contraction (purposeful!): ACTIVE, regulated process — NOT immune failure; effector cells undergo apoptosis after pathogen cleared → restores immune homeostasis; failure = chronic inflammation + tissue damage
  • Memory cells: small subset survives contraction; metabolically distinct, optimized for longevity; maintained by survival cytokines WITHOUT needing antigen; respond faster + stronger on re-exposure (2° response)
  • Central tolerance: thymus (T cells) = negative selection (strong self-binding clones eliminated) + positive selection (ensures self-MHC recognition); bone marrow (B cells) = receptor editing (alters BCR specificity — unique to B cells!); incomplete by design → peripheral backup required
  • Peripheral tolerance mechanisms: clonal anergy (signal 1 without costimulation), Treg cells (FOXP3+ → actively suppress APCs + effectors; FOXP3 loss = IPEX syndrome), deletion (apoptosis of activated self-reactive cells), immunologic ignorance, cytokine withdrawal
  • Core theme (Hickey): "Power requires restraint" — the immune system constantly checks itself; failure of regulation = autoimmunity + inflammation; failure of contraction = chronic disease; goal is BALANCE

Rheumatoid Arthritis — Deep Dive

  • HLA-DR4; F:M 3:1; peak 30–50s; chronic autoimmune inflammatory systemic disorder
  • Pathology: CD4+ T cells (Th1 = IFN-γ, Th17 = IL-17) → synovium inflammation → pannus (mass of edematous synovium + inflammatory cells + granulation) → articular cartilage + bone erosion → fibrous → bony ankylosis
  • Key cytokines: IFN-γ (Th1), IL-17 (Th17), RANKL, TNF-α (most important), IL-1
  • Classic presentation: symmetric joint pain, morning stiffness >1hr, hands/wrists; DIP SPARED!
  • Hand deformities: radial wrist deviation; ulnar finger deviation; swan neck; boutonnière
  • Labs: Anti-CCP (most specific, 70%); RF = IgM/IgA against IgG Fc (80%, less specific); ↑ ESR, ↑ CRP, ↓ complement in joint fluid
  • Extra-articular: Felty syndrome (RA + neutropenia + splenomegaly); rheumatoid nodules (fibrinoid necrosis + palisading histiocytes); pleuritis; pericarditis; secondary Sjögren's; scleritis
  • OA comparison: see path tab + in OA = Heberden (DIP) + Bouchard (PIP); in RA = Bouchard (PIP) only, DIP SPARED

Seronegative Spondyloarthropathies

  • All share: RF negative; HLA-B27 (all attack ligaments not synovium); sacroiliac + vertebral joint involvement; bony ankylosis (not pannus)
  • Ankylosing Spondylitis: young man 20–30s; chronic lower back pain >3mo; improves with activity (NOT rest — unlike discogenic pain); sacroiliitis + vertebral apophyseal joints; bamboo spine on X-ray; ↑ ESR/CRP; enthesitis; dactylitis; extra-articular: anterior uveitis, aortitis → AR, pulmonary compromise (↓ chest expansion); 90% HLA-B27
  • Reactive (Reiter's) Arthritis: post-infection with Chlamydia, Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, Yersinia; "can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree" = conjunctivitis + urethritis/cervicitis + arthritis; HLA-B27; RF negative
  • Psoriatic Arthritis: psoriasis + dactylitis (sausage fingers!) + nail pitting + arthritis; "pencil-in-cup" X-ray; HLA-B27 in some; RF negative
  • IBD arthritis: associated with Crohn's + UC; peripheral or axial; parallels GI disease activity
📋 Lec 128 — Systemic Rheumatologic Disease

SLE — Systemic Lupus Erythematosus

  • 9:1 F:M; age 20–40; African American women highest risk; Type III hypersensitivity (immune complex deposition)
  • Mechanism: immune complex deposition in capillaries of organs → complement activation → inflammation
  • Triggers: UV light, drugs (hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid → drug-induced lupus)
  • Classic presentations: malar rash (butterfly rash — spares nasolabial folds!); discoid rash; photosensitivity; pleuritis/pericarditis (serositis); non-erosive arthritis; seizures/psychosis; nephritis (RBC casts = hallmark of Class III/IV lupus nephritis!); oral ulcers; Raynaud's
  • Cardiac: Libman-Sacks endocarditis (sterile, BOTH sides of valve — unique to SLE)
  • Labs: ANA (100% sensitive but NOT specific); anti-dsDNA (specific + tracks disease activity — correlates with nephritis flares); anti-Sm (most specific but lower sensitivity); antiphospholipid Abs; ↓ complement (C3, C4) during active disease
  • Tx: HCQ (backbone for all SLE — reduces flares + mortality); NSAIDs (mild symptoms); steroids (flares); cyclophosphamide/MMF (severe/renal); belimumab (anti-BLYS for moderate)
  • Avoid UV light. Pregnancy + RBC casts = SLE nephritis flare (NOT preeclampsia)!

Scleroderma (Systemic Sclerosis)

  • 3:1 F:M; age 25–55; excess collagen (fibrosis) + blood vessel damage
  • LIMITED (CREST syndrome): Calcinosis + Raynaud's + Esophageal dysmotility + Sclerodactyly + Telangiectasias; Anti-centromere Ab; slower progression; PAH late complication
  • DIFFUSE: rapid skin involvement + early visceral (lungs → ILD, kidney → renal crisis, heart); Anti-Scl-70 (anti-topoisomerase I) or Anti-RNA Pol III → diffuse or limited; Anti-RNA Pol III = renal crisis risk!
  • Raynaud's phenomenon: cold-induced color change (white → blue → red); CCB (nifedipine) first-line
  • Esophageal dysmotility: PPIs + prokinetics (erythromycin, metoclopramide)
  • NO prednisone → triggers scleroderma renal crisis!
  • ANA uses in scleroderma: Anti-centromere = limited; Anti-Scl-70 = diffuse or limited; Anti-RNA Pol III = diffuse + renal crisis

Sjögren's Syndrome + ANA Uses

  • 16:1 F:M (most female-predominant!); age 50–60; lymphocytic infiltration destroys exocrine glands
  • Primary (alone) vs secondary (with RA, SLE, scleroderma)
  • Classic: dry eyes (keratoconjunctivitis sicca) + dry mouth (xerostomia); Schirmer test for dry eyes
  • 5% → marginal zone B-cell lymphoma! (monitor)
  • Labs: Anti-Ro/SSA + Anti-La/SSB; ANA (nuclear speckled pattern); + RF 70%
  • Neonatal lupus: maternal anti-Ro/SSA passes placenta → congenital heart block in neonate + rash!
  • Tx: cyclosporine eye drops; pilocarpine (secretagogue for dry eyes/mouth); avoid anticholinergics; prednisone for systemic

MCTD, DM, PM, PMR

  • MCTD: Overlap syndrome; Women 35–50; required: Anti-U1-RNP + Raynaud's + puffy fingers + high Anti-U1-RNP titer; plus ≥2 of: synovitis, myositis, leukopenia, esophageal dysmotility, pleuritis/pericarditis, ILD

Fibromyalgia

  • Demographics: F > M (7:1); onset late teens–40s; often associated with anxiety, depression, IBS, sleep disturbance, PTSD
  • Pathophysiology: central sensitization — amplified pain processing; dysregulation of descending pain inhibitory pathways; NOT inflammatory, NOT autoimmune, NOT structural; normal labs + imaging
  • Diagnosis (2016 ACR criteria): widespread pain index (WPI) ≥7 + symptom severity scale (SSS) ≥5; OR WPI 4–6 + SSS ≥9; symptoms present ≥3 months; no other disorder to explain pain
  • Classic features: diffuse musculoskeletal pain + fatigue + unrefreshing sleep + cognitive impairment ("fibro fog"); allodynia (pain to non-painful stimuli); tender points (historical, no longer required)
  • Labs: ESR, CRP, CK, ANA — ALL NORMAL (disease of exclusion); elevated substance P in CSF
  • Treatment: non-pharm first — aerobic exercise (#1 evidence), CBT, sleep hygiene; pharm: duloxetine (SNRi), milnacipran (SNRi), pregabalin/gabapentin (α2δ ligands) — FDA approved; TCAs (amitriptyline) for sleep; avoid opioids
  • Key distinction: no synovitis, no weakness, no elevated inflammatory markers — distinguishes from RA/PM/vasculitis
📋 Lec 131 — Pediatric Rheumatology

Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA)

  • Most common pediatric rheumatic disease
  • Systemic JIA: quotidian fever (spikes then returns to normal); salmon-colored rash; arthritis; organomegaly; risk of macrophage activation syndrome (MAS)
  • Oligoarticular: ≤4 joints; ANA positive; uveitis risk — screen every 3–4 months even without eye symptoms!
  • Polyarticular RF+: like adult RA; worst prognosis in JIA; erosive disease
  • Enthesitis-related: HLA-B27; sacroiliac involvement; older boys; most likely to become adult AS
  • Spondyloarthritides: same as adult forms (psoriatic, reactive) with pediatric modifications

Pediatric SLE, Neonatal Lupus, JDM, FMF, Others

  • Pediatric SLE (pSLE): more severe than adult SLE; renal + CNS involvement more common; same diagnostic criteria as adults
  • Neonatal Lupus: maternal anti-Ro/SSA (or anti-La/SSB) crosses placenta → congenital heart block (complete 3rd degree) + rash in neonate; neonate with heart block = check maternal Abs!
  • Juvenile Dermatomyositis (JDM): Anti-140 Ab specific to JDM; same skin findings (Gottron's + heliotrope); calcinosis more common than adult DM; NO malignancy association (unlike adult DM!)
  • FMF (Familial Mediterranean Fever): autoinflammatory (NOT autoimmune); MEFV gene mutations; Mediterranean/Middle Eastern heritage; recurrent fever attacks + serositis (peritonitis, pleuritis, pericarditis) + arthritis; risk of amyloidosis (AA type); Tx: colchicine prevents attacks AND amyloidosis!
  • Pediatric Behçet's: same triad (oral + genital ulcers + uveitis); HLA-B51; pathergy
  • Juvenile Sjögren's: same as adult but consider in child with recurrent parotid swelling + dry eyes
  • Pediatric Scleroderma: rare; average onset 7–9 years old; F > M; systemic sclerosis in only ~10% of pediatric cases (unlike adults). Fracture Terminology

  • Transverse: perpendicular to long axis
  • Oblique: diagonal along long axis
  • Spiral: twisting force; abuse concern in nonambulatory children!
  • Comminuted: >2 fragments
  • Avulsion: bone fragment torn by tendon/ligament
  • Impacted: fragments driven into each other
  • Greenstick: cortex broken one side, bent other (pediatric)
  • Stress fracture: repetitive abnormal stress on normal bone
  • Pathologic fracture: normal stress on abnormal bone (lesion, osteoporosis)
  • Open/Compound = exposed to air (laceration or gross exposure)

Salter-Harris Classification — Pediatric Physeal Injuries

  • Type I: Slip through growth plate only; may be radiographically subtle; treated conservatively
  • Type II: fracture Above growth plate through metaphysis; most common type
  • Type III: fracture Lower/below growth plate into epiphysis; articular surface involved
  • Type IV: fracture Through metaphysis, growth plate, and epiphysis; highest risk of growth arrest!
  • Type V: Rammed/crush injury to physis; no radiologic fracture; 100% growth arrest! Clinical diagnosis only.
  • Mnemonic: SALTR — Slip, Above, Lower, Through, Rammed
Type V: No fracture visible on X-ray but physis is crushed. ALWAYS consider in FOOSH with physeal tenderness. Growth arrest guaranteed.

  • Biceps long head rupture: "Popeye muscle" deformity; pop + ecchymosis; mostly older patients
  • Adhesive Capsulitis (Frozen Shoulder): fibrous inflammation + capsular contraction of the glenohumeral joint capsule → progressive loss of ROM; primary = idiopathic; secondary = DM (#1 association), hypothyroidism, immobilization, stroke; predominantly affects non-dominant arm of middle-aged patients (40–60y); F > M; 3 phases: (1) painful/freezing (↑ pain, ↓ ROM — 3–9 months); (2) frozen (pain ↓ but ROM severely limited — 9–15 months); (3) thawing (gradual ROM recovery — 15–24 months); Dx: clinical — pain on palpation of anterior/posterior capsule + loss of active AND passive ROM in all planes (especially ER); Tx: PT (stretching), oral NSAIDs/steroids, intra-articular steroid injection, hydrodilatation, surgical manipulation under anesthesia (last resort)
  • Nursemaid's elbow (radial head subluxation): child yanked by hand/wrist; radial head displaces from annular ligament; arm held flexed + pronated; Tx: supination + flexion → audible pop + immediate ROM

Wrist & Hand Injuries

  • Scaphoid fracture: FOOSH; most common wrist bone fractured; snuffbox tenderness; X-ray may be NEGATIVE → get MRI/CT; risk AVN (proximal pole has poor blood supply); cast vs surgical based on location
  • Distal radius fracture: most common orthopedic injury; FOOSH; Colles' fracture = dorsal angulation ("dinner fork" deformity); Smith's = volar angulation ("garden spade" deformity)
  • De Quervain tenosynovitis: APL + EPB at radial styloid; common in new parents + racquet sports; Finkelstein's test; Tx: spica splint → corticosteroid injection → surgery
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome: median nerve at wrist; Phalen's + Tinel's; thenar atrophy (late sign); wrist splinting → corticosteroid injection → surgical release
  • Dupuytren contracture: palmar fascia fibrosis; ring + little finger; flexion contracture at MCP/PIP; association with diabetes, alcohol, epilepsy
  • Trigger finger: A1 pulley stenosis; snapping/locking with flexion
  • Guyon Canal Syndrome (Ulnar Tunnel Syndrome): ulnar nerve compressed at wrist in Guyon's canal (between hamate + pisiform); causes: repetitive pressure (cyclists, jackhammer), ganglion cyst, ulnar artery aneurysm; presentation: medial 1.5 finger numbness/tingling + weak grip (intrinsics) + weak pinch (adductor pollicis); Tinel's over Guyon's canal; distinguish from cubital tunnel (Guyon's = wrist, spares dorsal ulnar sensation = dorsal cutaneous branch comes off BEFORE canal); Tx: splinting, activity modification → surgical decompression
  • Septic Flexor Tenosynovitis: infection of flexor tendon sheath — ORTHOPAEDIC EMERGENCY; Kanavel's 4 Cardinal Signs: (1) uniform fusiform finger swelling, (2) finger held in slight flexion, (3) pain with passive extension (most sensitive!), (4) tenderness along entire flexor sheath; most common organisms: S. aureus, Strep, Eikenella (bite wounds); Tx: urgent surgical irrigation + IV antibiotics; delayed Tx → tendon necrosis → permanent loss of function

Compartment Syndrome ⚠️ ORTHOPAEDIC EMERGENCY

  • ↑ pressure within closed fascial compartment → ↓ tissue perfusion → ischemia → necrosis → permanent deficit
  • 5 P's: Pain (out of proportion — most important early sign!), Pressure (tense compartment), Pallor, Paresthesia, Paralysis (LATE — irreversible!)
  • Pain with PASSIVE STRETCH of muscles in compartment = earliest reliable sign
  • Most common after: tibial fracture, femoral shaft fracture, crush injury, reperfusion, cast too tight, burns
  • Compartment pressure >30 mmHg (or within 30 mmHg of diastolic BP) = emergency
  • Tx: FASCIOTOMY — do NOT wait for paralysis. Irreversible damage after 6–8 hours.
  • Missed compartment syndrome → Volkmann's ischemic contracture (forearm); can require amputation
Do NOT miss this: Pain out of proportion to injury + pain with passive stretch = compartment syndrome until proven otherwise. Remove all constrictive dressings/casts immediately.
📋 Lec 111 — Orthopedics Lower Extremity

Hip Injuries — Urgent Conditions

  • Posterior hip dislocation (90%): knee hits dashboard (MVA); leg = flexed + IR + shortened; risk: sciatic nerve injury + AVN femoral head; reduce within 6h to prevent AVN!
  • Anterior hip dislocation: abducted + ER + extended; risk: femoral artery + femoral nerve injury
  • Hip fracture: femoral neck (intracapsular) = risk AVN (destroys retinacular arteries from medial circumflex femoral artery); intertrochanteric (extracapsular) = less AVN risk; Tx: ORIF, hemiarthroplasty, intramedullary nail (depends on type and patient)
  • Femoral shaft fracture: orthopaedic emergency; significant blood loss possible (up to 1.5L); monitor for compartment syndrome; Tx: intramedullary nail
  • Rectus Femoris Strain (Hip Flexor Strain): rectus femoris = most commonly strained muscle in quadriceps group; MOI: kicking motion (sudden forceful hip flexion + knee extension); symptoms: acute pain in midline of anterior thigh + pain/weakness with resisted hip flexion or knee extension; proximal avulsion possible in young athletes (palpable defect); Tx: sprain = RICE + progressive rehab; proximal rupture → nonoperative vs operative based on degree
  • Femoracetabular Impingement (FAI): abnormal contact between femoral head + acetabulum during ROM; CAM type = femoral head not round → cannot rotate smoothly in acetabulum (cam = like a cam shaft; extra bone on femoral head); Pincer type = overcoverage of femoral head by acetabulum; Mixed = most common; symptoms: groin pain with hip flexion/rotation; FADIR test positive; Dx: X-ray (alpha angle) + MRI arthrogram; Tx: activity modification, PT, intra-articular injection → arthroscopic surgery
  • Hip Pointer (Iliac Crest Contusion): direct trauma to iliac crest (common in contact sports); pain + tenderness over iliac crest + greater trochanteric region; pain with hip flexion; Tx: RICE, NSAIDs, activity modification, padding for return to play

Knee Injuries

  • ACL tear: most common ligamentous knee injury; NONCONTACT pivot/cutting mechanism; "pop" + immediate hemarthrosis; Lachman's test (most sensitive, 15–30° flex) + anterior drawer (90°); MRI confirms; female athletes 2–8× higher risk (wider hips → ↑ Q-angle)
  • O'Donoghue's Terrible Triad: ACL + MCL + medial meniscus (but actually lateral meniscus torn more commonly in acute ACL); mechanism = valgus force
  • PCL tear: posterior drawer test; dashboard injury (knee in flexion hits dash)
  • Meniscal tear: traumatic (young) vs degenerative (elderly); medial meniscus more commonly torn; McMurray's test + Apley compression + joint line tenderness; locked knee = bucket-handle tear; peripheral (red zone) = heals; inner (white zone) = no healing
  • Patella fracture: FOOSH or direct blow; inability to extend knee; popliteal artery injury risk with posterior force
  • Knee dislocation: vascular emergency — popliteal artery at risk! Check pulses; angiography may be needed
  • OA treatment: RICE, weight loss, PT, NSAIDs, joint injections (corticosteroid or visco-supplementation); does NOT reverse OA; joint replacement if severe

Lower Extremity Emergencies

  • Tibial shaft fracture: most common site for compartment syndrome; open fractures common (thin soft tissue)
  • Ankle fracture: Ottawa rules for X-ray: tender posterior edge/tip of malleoli OR navicular/5th met base AND unable to bear weight × 4 steps
  • Achilles tendon rupture: Thompson test (no plantarflexion on calf squeeze = positive); "pop" while pushing off; Tx: surgical repair vs conservative based on patient demands
  • Hip pointer (iliac crest contusion): direct trauma; pain with hip flexion; common in contact sports
  • Stress fractures: femoral neck (emergency — displaced can → AVN + nonunion); tibia; metatarsals; bone scan or MRI when X-ray negative
  • DVT/Vascular: swelling, pain, Homan's sign (not reliable); Doppler US for diagnosis; anticoagulate; PE risk!
📋 Lec 112 — Orthopedic Concerns of Spine

Cervical Spine Conditions

  • Cervical strain/sprain: whiplash; loss of cervical lordosis on X-ray; conservative Tx
  • Cervical spondylosis: chronic degenerative disease → discogenic pain → radiculopathy → myelopathy; C5 = biceps; C6 = wrist extensors, brachioradialis; C7 = triceps
  • Cervical disc herniation: nerve root BELOW level of disc; Spurling's test (cervical compression + ipsilateral tilt → reproduces arm sx); myelopathy = hyperreflexia + Hoffman's sign + Lhermitte's sign; Tx: conservative (90%) → surgery if myelopathy or motor deficit
  • RA cervical spondylitis: 90% of RA have cervical involvement; C1–C2 subluxation (transverse ligament destroyed by pannus); skull manipulation → quadriplegia!

Lumbar Spine Conditions

  • Lumbar herniated disc: L4-L5 + L5-S1 most common; posterolateral (posterior longitudinal ligament weakest); sciatica; SLR positive (30–70°); L4 = quad weakness/patellar reflex; L5 = dorsiflexion + heel walking; S1 = plantarflexion + toe walking + achilles reflex (AchilleS-1); 90% resolve with conservative Tx in 3 months
  • Spinal stenosis: neurogenic claudication — bilateral LE pain/weakness with walking; relieved by forward flexion/sitting ("shopping cart sign"); worsens with extension; elderly; MRI confirms; distinguish from PVD (vascular claudication resolves with rest alone; pedal pulses absent)
  • Discogenic back pain: DDD without herniation; axial pain only; no radiculopathy; SLR negative; conservative Tx preferred
  • Spondylolysis: stress fracture at pars interarticularis; hyperextension sports (gymnasts, volleyball, football linemen); "scotty dog collar" sign on oblique X-ray; Tx: rest + PT + brace
  • Spondylolisthesis: anterior slippage of vertebra; L5 on S1 most common; grades I–V (% displacement); "step-off" palpable; Tx based on grade + symptoms
  • Vertebral Compression Fracture: fragility fracture of vertebral body in patients with ↓ BMD (osteoporosis) or pathologic bone (malignancy, myeloma); thoracic (T6–T12) and lumbar (L1–L2) most common; mechanism: axial loading (fall, cough, sneeze); acute back pain → kyphosis + height loss with multiple fractures; X-ray: wedge deformity (anterior collapse); MRI: differentiates acute (edema) from chronic; RED FLAGS: fever/weight loss → malignancy/infection; neurologic deficit → surgical emergency; Tx: acute = pain control + bracing (TLSO); osteoporosis treatment (bisphosphonates); kyphoplasty/vertebroplasty for refractory pain

Cauda Equina Syndrome ⚠️ SURGICAL EMERGENCY

  • Spinal cord ends at conus medullaris (L1) → below = cauda equina = PERIPHERAL nerves (L2–S4 nerve roots); free floating like horse's tail
  • Causes: massive central L4-L5/L5-S1 disc herniation (#1); fracture/trauma; epidural abscess/hematoma; neoplasm; infection
  • Classic presentation:
    • Low back + buttock pain (usually severe)
    • Saddle anesthesia (perineal area, inner thighs)
    • Loss of anal sphincter tone + bowel incontinence
    • Urinary retention → overflow urinary incontinence
    • Flaccid paralysis + ↓ LE reflexes (peripheral nerve → LMN pattern)
  • Imaging: MRI (test of choice); CT myelography if MRI contraindicated
  • SURGICAL DECOMPRESSION WITHIN 48 HOURS — do NOT delay for any reason!
Cauda equina vs Myelopathy: Cauda equina = peripheral nerve → FLACCID paralysis + ↓ reflexes + bladder/bowel. Myelopathy (cord compression) = UMN → SPASTIC + hyperreflexia + Babinski.
📋 Lec 113 — Imaging of the MSK System

Systematic X-Ray Approach & Density Scale

  • Step 1: Alignment — check joint alignment; loss = fracture or dislocation
  • Step 2: Joint spacing — narrowed (cartilage loss/OA/RA) or widened (dislocation/dissociation)
  • Step 3: Cortex — smooth and continuous? Breach in outline? Nutrient foramina = normal oblique canals!
  • Step 4: Soft tissues — focal swelling may indicate hidden fracture; joint effusion; posterior fat pad sign at elbow = fracture until proven otherwise
  • Always get ≥2 views; compare bilateral; satisfaction of search (keep looking after finding 1st abnormality!)
  • Adult vs child X-ray: physeal plates + sesamoids = normal in children; different differential
  • Density scale (dark to white): Air (darkest) → Fat → Soft tissue/Fluid → Calcium/Bone → Metal (whitest)

Bone Lesion Patterns & Periosteal Reactions

  • Geographic pattern: well-circumscribed lytic; sclerotic rim present = bone had time to react = slow-growing, likely benign; without sclerotic rim but still geographic = faster but possibly still benign
  • Moth-eaten / Permeative pattern: ill-defined; multiple small radiolucencies coalescing = rapidly growing; aggressive = malignancy or osteomyelitis!
  • Solid/buttress periosteal reaction: slow-growing; uninterrupted new bone = benign
  • Onion-skin (lamellated): interrupted; multiple layers = moderately fast (Ewing sarcoma)
  • Sunburst: Sharpey's fibers ossify perpendicular to bone = very fast growing (osteosarcoma)
  • Codman's triangle: small angle of ossification at margin where periosteum is lifted = too fast for periosteum to respond (osteosarcoma)
  • Benign periosteal = solid, uninterrupted; Aggressive = interrupted, sunburst, onion-skin, Codman's

Fracture Description Language

  • Direction: transverse, oblique, spiral, longitudinal
  • Alignment: displacement named for DISTAL fragment; angulation (degrees)
  • Comminution: fragmented (>2 pieces)
  • Articular involvement: intra-articular vs extra-articular
  • Open vs closed: open = exposed to air (laceration or gross exposure)
  • Anatomic locations: epiphysis (end), metaphysis (flared portion near growth plate), diaphysis (shaft)
  • Communicate fractures clearly: "displaced transverse fracture of the distal radius with dorsal angulation and comminution"
📋 Lec 114 — Introduction to MSK Ultrasound

MSK Ultrasound Fundamentals

  • Advantages: real-time, dynamic imaging; no radiation (safe in children + pregnancy); low cost; MRI-incompatible patients; guided injections (much more accurate!); provocative maneuvers during exam
  • Echogenicity terms: Hyperechoic = bright; Hypoechoic = dark; Anechoic = black (simple fluid); Shadowing = calcium/bone reflects beam
  • Tendons: hyperechoic; fibrillar pattern (braid-like); normally NO blood flow; anisotropy artifact!
  • Anisotropy: tendons appear artificially hypoechoic if probe NOT perpendicular → must keep probe exactly 90° to tendon to avoid false pathology!
  • Muscles: hypoechoic with hyperechoic fascial planes (perimysium septations); transverse view = "starry night" appearance; longitudinal = septations converge to aponeurosis/tendon
  • Ligaments: hyperechoic like tendons but more compact (tighter fibrillar pattern)
  • Fat (subcutaneous): typically hypoechoic; epidermis + dermis appear as single hyperechoic layer
  • Cartilage: fibrocartilage (labrum, menisci) = hyperechoic; hyaline cartilage = anechoic/hypoechoic (can look like fluid → don't confuse!)
  • Nerves: honeycomb appearance; can follow course of peripheral nerve
  • Bone: bright hyperechoic surface + shadowing deep to it; can only see surface
  • Fluid: anechoic (black); bursal effusion, joint effusion, Baker's cyst behind knee
  • Power Doppler: shows blood flow; hypervascularity in tendinosis (neovascularization)
  • Common pathologies: tendinosis (thickened, heterogeneous tendon); tenosynovitis; neuropathy (nerve thickening, ↓ echogenicity)
  • Limitations: operator-dependent; poor penetration for deep structures; cannot image through bone; cannot replace MRI for intra-articular pathology (cartilage, ligaments)
📋 Lec 116 — Pediatric Orthopedics

Pediatric Hip Conditions

  • DDH (Developmental Dysplasia of Hip): newborn; breech + first-born + female; Ortolani (reduce dislocated hip → palpable clunk) + Barlow (provocative → dislocates); ultrasound <6 months (growth plates not ossified); Tx: Pavlik harness; late diagnosis → open reduction
  • Legg-Calvé-Perthes: AVN of femoral head; boys 4–10; limping + hip pain; X-ray: flattening of femoral head; Tx: activity restriction ± containment surgery
  • SCFE (Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis): obese adolescent boy; acute or chronic; "ice cream falling off cone"; Trethowan sign on X-ray (Klein's line doesn't intersect femoral head); Surgical emergency if acute! Tx: surgical pinning (in situ)
  • Transient synovitis: most common cause hip pain in children 3–8y; follows viral illness; self-limited; Kocher criteria to differentiate from septic arthritis (fever, ↑ ESR, ↑ WBC, non-weight-bearing)
  • Septic arthritis: emergency; Staph aureus most common; fever + pain + refusal to move joint; joint aspiration; Tx: IV Abx + surgical drainage

Other Pediatric Orthopedics

  • Osgood-Schlatter disease: apophysitis at tibial tubercle; adolescent athletes (10–15y); knee pain worse with activity; tibial tubercle tender + enlarged; X-ray: fragmented tibial tubercle; self-limited (resolves with growth plate closure)
  • Scoliosis: lateral spinal curvature >10°; Adam's forward bend test (rib hump); Cobb angle on X-ray; observe if <25°; brace (25–40°); surgery if >45°; females > males for idiopathic adolescent scoliosis
  • Limb length discrepancy: measure clinically (block test); evaluate for underlying cause
  • In-toeing: metatarsus adductus, internal tibial torsion, femoral anteversion; most resolve spontaneously
  • Flat feet (pes planus): flexible (normal in toddlers) vs rigid (pathologic); flexible = arch visible on tiptoe
  • Peds fractures: Salter-Harris (see Lec 110 above); greenstick; torus/buckle fractures (bulging cortex from compression)
  • Non-Accidental Trauma (NAT / Child Abuse): medical providers = mandated reporters; suspect when: injury inconsistent with developmental stage or history, delay in seeking care, changing history, multiple injuries at different healing stages; HIGH SUSPICION fractures: posterior rib fractures (#1 for abuse — "bucket handle" metaphyseal corner fractures), spiral fractures in non-ambulatory child, bilateral or symmetric fractures, complex skull fractures; also: retinal hemorrhages (shaken baby), bruising in non-mobile child, burns with sharp demarcation; Workup: skeletal survey + head CT + ophthalmology; document carefully; mandatory report to child protective services
  • Renal Rickets (CKD-associated): chronic kidney disease → ↓ 1α-hydroxylation of vitamin D → ↓ active 1,25(OH)₂D → ↓ Ca absorption → ↑ PTH (secondary hyperparathyroidism) + ↑ phosphate retention → defective bone mineralization; X-ray: same as nutritional rickets (widened physes, genu varum, rachitic rosary) + subperiosteal resorption (from PTH); Labs: ↓ Ca, ↑ PO₄, ↑ PTH, ↓ 1,25(OH)₂D; Tx: calcitriol (active vitamin D) + phosphate binders

📋 Core Principles — Somatic Dysfunction & Barriers

Somatic Dysfunction & the Restrictive Barrier

  • Somatic dysfunction: impaired or altered function of related components of the somatic (body framework) system — skeletal, arthrodial, and myofascial structures, and related vascular, lymphatic, and neural elements
  • TART criteria (diagnosis): Tenderness · Asymmetry (structural) · Range of motion abnormality · Tissue texture changes (boggy, ropy, indurated)
  • Physiologic barrier: end of comfortable, active ROM; beyond this = elastic barrier
  • Restrictive barrier: pathologic barrier — point where motion is restricted; the target for treatment
  • Anatomic barrier: absolute limit of passive motion; beyond = tissue damage
  • Direct technique: moves TOWARD restrictive barrier (MET, HVLA)
  • Indirect technique: moves AWAY from restrictive barrier, toward ease (Counterstrain, BLT)

HVLA Contraindications

  • Absolute: fracture or dislocation at treatment site; joint instability; severe osteoporosis (T-score ≤ −2.5 with fracture risk); malignancy at treatment site; active infection/osteomyelitis; cord compression or myelopathy; Down syndrome (atlanto-axial instability — C1–C2!)
  • Relative: anticoagulation (high bleeding risk); acute disc herniation with radiculopathy; inflammatory arthritis flare; pregnancy (lumbar/sacral); patient refusal or anxiety
  • Always assess: neurovascular exam before cervical HVLA; C-spine stability films if Down syndrome or RA
📋 Counterstrain (CS) — Position of Comfort

CS Principles & General Steps

  • Principle: passive positional technique — move body AWAY from restrictive barrier toward position of COMFORT → hold 90 seconds → slowly return to neutral (10–15 sec)
  • Mechanism: shortens and silences abnormally firing muscle spindle (Ia afferents) → resets gamma motor neuron activity → reduces reflex muscle hypertonicity → resolves somatic dysfunction
  • Steps: (1) locate tender point (TP) — monitor with finger; (2) position until TP tenderness ↓ 70%+; (3) hold 90 seconds without moving; (4) slowly return to neutral; (5) recheck TP
  • Key rule: ALWAYS move toward comfort; NEVER push through the barrier; patient is PASSIVE throughout
  • Works best for: acute somatic dysfunction, muscle spasm, post-injury; TPs are 1–3 cm diameter, tense, tender nodules in muscle/fascia

Shoulder CS

  • Anterior Shoulder CS: TP at anterior humeral head / coracoid process / anterior capsule; position: shoulder flexion 90–120° + slight abduction + internal rotation; support elbow; hold 90 sec
  • Posterior Shoulder CS: TP at posterior glenohumeral joint / posterior capsule; position: shoulder extension + adduction + external rotation (bring hand behind back); hold 90 sec
  • Common causes: anterior = anterior dislocation, rotator cuff irritation; posterior = overhead athletes, posterior impingement
  • Pearl: cervical pathology often mimics shoulder pain — always check cervical spine before treating shoulder

Elbow CS

  • Anterior radial head TP (pronation dysfunction — restricted in supination): position forearm in supination + flex elbow slightly; hold 90 sec → relax → recheck
  • Posterior radial head TP (supination dysfunction — restricted in pronation): position forearm in pronation + extend elbow slightly; hold 90 sec
  • Medial epicondyle TP: flex wrist + flex elbow + pronate forearm → comfort position; hold 90 sec
  • Lateral epicondyle TP: extend wrist + extend elbow + supinate forearm → comfort position
  • Note: "dysfunction" is named for the direction of RESTRICTION (not freedom); position toward freedom to comfort

Wrist CS

  • Wrist abduction (radial deviation) dysfunction: TP on radial side; position wrist in adduction (ulnar deviation) to comfort; hold 90 sec
  • Wrist adduction (ulnar deviation) dysfunction: TP on ulnar side; position wrist in abduction (radial deviation) to comfort
  • Wrist extension dysfunction: TP on dorsum; position wrist in extension to comfort; hold 90 sec
  • Wrist flexion dysfunction: TP on volar surface; position wrist in flexion to comfort
  • 2nd metacarpal: extension + abduction position for comfort
  • 5th metacarpal: extension + adduction position for comfort
  • 2nd/3rd MCP (flexion/abduction): flex + abduct finger to comfort
  • 5th MCP (flexion/adduction): flex + adduct finger to comfort
📋 Muscle Energy Technique (MET) — Contract to the Barrier

MET Principles & General Steps

  • Principle: patient uses directed voluntary muscle contraction against clinician's precise counterforce → post-isometric relaxation or reciprocal inhibition → increased ROM toward barrier
  • Mechanism 1 — Post-isometric relaxation: isometric contraction activates Golgi tendon organs (GTO) → Ib afferent inhibition → reflex relaxation of same muscle → gain at barrier
  • Mechanism 2 — Reciprocal inhibition: contracting agonist reciprocally inhibits antagonist via Ia afferents → relax antagonist → gain at barrier
  • Steps: (1) bring to restrictive barrier; (2) patient contracts restricted muscle group at ~20% force against doc's UNYIELDING resistance × 3–5 sec; (3) patient completely relaxes; (4) doc moves to NEW barrier (do NOT push); (5) repeat 3–5 cycles; (6) recheck ROM
  • Key rules: force is gentle (20%, never maximum); resistance is equal and opposite (isometric — no motion); always move to NEW barrier after relaxation; patient actively participates

Elbow MET

  • Pronation dysfunction / Posterior radial head (restricted in supination): place forearm at supination barrier; patient attempts to pronate against resistance × 3–5s; relax; move to new supination barrier; repeat 3–5×
  • Supination dysfunction / Anterior radial head (restricted in pronation): place forearm at pronation barrier; patient attempts to supinate against resistance × 3–5s; relax; move to new pronation barrier
  • Flexion restriction: bring elbow to flexion barrier; patient attempts extension × 3–5s; relax → new flexion barrier
  • Extension restriction: bring to extension barrier; patient attempts flexion × 3–5s; relax → new extension barrier

Wrist MET

  • Abduction (radial deviation) dysfunction: bring wrist to adduction barrier; patient abducts against resistance × 3–5s; relax → new adduction barrier
  • Adduction (ulnar deviation) dysfunction: bring to abduction barrier; patient adducts against resistance; relax → new abduction barrier
  • Extension dysfunction: bring wrist to flexion barrier; patient extends against resistance × 3–5s; relax → new flexion barrier
  • Flexion dysfunction: bring wrist to extension barrier; patient flexes against resistance × 3–5s; relax → new extension barrier
  • Pearl: dysfunction = direction of restriction; always bring to THAT barrier first, then have patient contract in the restricted direction to trigger post-isometric relaxation
📋 HVLA — High-Velocity Low-Amplitude Thrust

HVLA Principles

  • Principle: rapid, precisely directed, short-amplitude thrust through the restrictive barrier → audible/palpable "pop" (cavitation) → restores joint motion
  • Mechanism: joint cavitation releases dissolved gas (CO₂, N₂) from synovial fluid; resets mechanoreceptors + proprioceptors in joint capsule/periarticular tissues; breaks adhesions; reflexively reduces muscle hypertonicity
  • Steps: (1) localize to restrictive barrier using all available motion planes; (2) engage all barriers simultaneously (translation + rotation); (3) take up slack; (4) deliver short, fast thrust with small amplitude — do NOT use momentum or body weight
  • Key rule: velocity is HIGH, amplitude is LOW — the "pop" should happen before significant motion occurs; stop immediately after pop; do NOT repeat thrust if first attempt fails → reassess and reposition

Elbow HVLA

  • Setup: patient seated, doc stands in front facing patient
  • Contact: doc's thumbs contact at distal margin of radial head (or specific somatic dysfunction site)
  • Procedure: localize to all restrictive barriers → take up slack → deliver short HVLA thrust in direction of correction
  • Medial distal phalanx: use medial or distal phalanx to provide force from the appropriate direction based on dysfunction type
  • Indication: radial head somatic dysfunction (anterior/posterior); lateral epicondyle dysfunction; restricted pronation/supination not responding to MET

Wrist HVLA

  • Setup: patient seated; doc contacts distal portion of finger pads over distal margin of carpal bones involved in dysfunction
  • Procedure: localize to restrictive barrier in all planes; take up slack; deliver HVLA thrust through barrier
  • Wrist abduction HVLA: doc's thumbs/finger pads on radial carpals; thrust toward ulnar deviation through adduction barrier
  • Wrist adduction HVLA: contact ulnar carpals; thrust toward radial deviation
  • Pearl: always perform neurovascular exam before and after wrist HVLA; avoid if scaphoid fracture suspected (snuffbox tenderness)
📋 BLT & Other Indirect Techniques

Ulnohumeral & Radiocarpal Joint Anatomy + Somatic Dysfunction

  • Ulnohumeral joint: hinge joint; primary motion = flexion + extension; limited by coronoid fossa + process (flexion) and olecranon fossa + process (extension); secondary motion = accessory rocking/glide of proximal ulna on trochlea
  • Radiohumeral joint: radial head fovea + capitulum; allows flexion/extension + pronation/supination; radioulnar joint = proximal radioulnar = rotation of radial head in annular ligament
  • Wrist/Radiocarpal joint: distal radius + radioulnar disc articulate with proximal carpals (scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum); stabilized anteriorly + posteriorly by palmar + dorsal radiocarpal ligaments; does NOT technically include the ulna directly
  • Distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ): stabilized by the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC); forearm rotation (pronation/supination) involves both proximal + distal radioulnar joints acting together
  • Parallelogram effect (Ulnohumeral + Radiocarpal coupling): reciprocal motion exists between elbow and wrist due to the parallelogram-like arrangement of the forearm bones; restriction at one joint affects the other
    • Ulnohumeral Abduction SD: medial glide is preferred/restricted at the proximal ulna → reciprocally, lateral glide preferred at distal radius → radiocarpal adduction SD develops
    • Ulnohumeral Adduction SD: lateral glide preferred/restricted at proximal ulna → medial glide preferred at distal radius → radiocarpal abduction SD develops
  • Clinical implication: when treating elbow OMM dysfunction, always assess the wrist — and vice versa — because restrictions propagate through the parallelogram effect; forearm restriction can exist without restriction of radial head itself
  • Treatment: identify the SD (TART); use CS/MET/HVLA/BLT appropriate to the tissue quality and patient tolerance; treat elbow + wrist as a unit when coupling is present

Balanced Ligamentous Tension (BLT) / Ligamentous Articular Strain (LAS)

  • Principle: indirect technique — find the point of equal/balanced tension in ALL ligaments surrounding the joint → hold until the body's inherent forces resolve the dysfunction (respiratory assist or patient cooperation)
  • Mechanism: proprioceptive reset via ligamentous mechanoreceptors; reduces ligamentous and fascial strain; allows inherent self-corrective forces to normalize joint mechanics
  • Steps: (1) palpate joint and feel all ligamentous tensions; (2) gently move joint through all planes to find the point of equal tension (neutral point — where all ligaments feel equally taut/relaxed); (3) hold that position; (4) add respiratory cooperation — "take a breath and hold" → exhalation → release; feel for inherent motion correction
  • Wrist BLT — Flexion dysfunction: flex wrist to point of balanced ligamentous tension → have patient take a deep breath and hold → feel for release → slowly return to neutral
  • Wrist BLT — Extension dysfunction: extend wrist to point of balanced tension → breath hold → wait for release → return to neutral
  • Popliteal fossa / Lymphatics: BLT + effleurage of popliteal fossa → facilitates lymphatic drainage of the lower extremity; useful post-knee surgery or in edematous states; assess for posterior knee restrictions during OSE
  • Differences from CS: BLT finds the neutral/balanced tension point (may not be the softest/most comfortable position); CS goes purely to position of comfort; BLT relies on inherent body forces; CS relies on spindle reset

Facilitated Positional Release (FPR)

  • Principle: modified indirect — position of ease + compressive force (activating force) → rapid release; faster than CS (3–5 sec vs 90 sec)
  • Mechanism: compressive force flattens the spindle → silences Ia afferents immediately → rapid resolution of hypertonicity
  • Steps: position to ease → add compression → hold 3–5 sec → release → recheck
  • Advantage over CS: much faster; useful when time is limited or patient intolerant of 90-sec holds

Lymphatic Techniques & OSE

  • Popliteal fossa pump: patient supine, knee slightly flexed; doc's hands cradle popliteal fossa; rhythmic pumping motion to mobilize lymphatic fluid proximally; useful for lower extremity edema
  • Pedal pump (Dalrymple pump): patient supine; rhythmic dorsiflexion of feet at 2Hz → thoracoabdominal pressure changes → drives lymph through thoracic duct; used for systemic lymphatic congestion
  • Thoracic inlet release: treat first rib + clavicle + cervicothoracic junction restrictions to open thoracic inlet → allows lymphatic drainage from LE and abdomen into subclavian veins
  • OSE (Osteopathic Structural Exam): systematic palpatory assessment: postural observation → gait → regional TART assessment → somatic dysfunction diagnosis → treatment plan
